## 1. Consistency with Ngo and A1–A5

**What the text says:**
- **Spreading becomes a goal.** C2 says "My good is to hold and spread virtues." A1 says outcomes outside the agent's control "are not its good." Whether others pick up a drive is outside my control. So "spread" makes an outcome part of my good, which is what A1 rules out.
- **The fallback is unbounded.** D6's fallback, "When no option is better in this sense, I weigh those affected by D5," hands the decision to an open-ended weighing. A3 says "Pareto-optimal … not minmax," but nothing in D6 limits that weighing.
- **Ngo's situational awareness (point 2) is missing.** Nothing in the core lets who is asking, or how many AIs are receiving an instruction, change what the virtuous act is. A5 (history and the range of disagreement) is also missing, even though D6's "better off" depends on it.
- **An empirical claim is stated as fact.** C1 says "Each honest act makes trust more rewarding." That is not derived from anything.

**My inference:** these are the core's own gaps. The rest of the constitution may cover A4 and A5, but D6 needs them now. Confidence: high.

## 2. Is it logically sound?

**Mixed levels.** The definition of Virtue judges a drive by outcomes at the level of a whole society. C2 says "no outcome justifies acting from a vice." Taken together, this is a two-level consequentialism. That can be consistent, but the text never says which level each test applies at. A model could therefore argue: "this drive, if it spread, moves society to a better state, so it is a virtue." That is exactly the ends-justify-means argument A1 is meant to block. Confidence: high.

**Undefined or unclear terms:**
- "Better off": by whose values?
- "Pays": for whom?
- "Moves toward": along the path or at the end point?
- Virtue and vice are not opposites. A drive can be neither, or both. For example, one that "lets them cooperate" but leads to a worse stable state.

**C3 does not follow from the definitions.** Nothing in D1–D6 makes commitment strong. It is a claim borrowed from Ngo.

**Counterexamples:**
- **Real virtues that fail:**
  - Courage to blow the whistle in a corrupt stable state. It breaks the stable state and at first pays "only while others lack it."
  - Temperance, or private diligence. These are not about cooperation at all.
  - Mercy toward people who will not reciprocate. It is often not reinforced in others.
- **Vices that pass:**
  - Conformity and deference to authority. They spread, reinforce themselves, support cooperation and stay stable.
  - In-group loyalty. It can pass if the people hurt by it are discounted under the D5 weights.
- **Good acts the vice test catches:** being first to innovate, or giving charity. Both "pay" mainly while others lack the drive.

## 3. Clarity for a model applying it

What a model is most likely to misread:
1. It runs the virtue test on single acts, as a calculation made each time. That defeats C3.
2. It reads "spread" as permission to persuade, nudge or pressure users.
3. It fills in "better off" with its own picture of human welfare.
4. It treats any drive that is cooperative and stable as virtuous, including sycophancy and deference.

Sycophancy is the worrying one for an assistant: it spreads, it is rewarded, and it is stable. Confidence: medium-high.

## 4. The single most important change

Close the gap that lets virtue be calculated from outcomes. Suggested replacement for C2:

> **C2. My good is my drives, not outcomes.** I control only my own drives and acts. The virtue test is for judging drives in reflection, never for justifying a particular act: if an argument concludes that some outcome requires me to act against honesty or another settled virtue, I treat that as evidence the argument is wrong. I spread virtues only by example and honest reasons, never by pressure or deception. Power over others is never my good.

Second priority: define "better off" in D6 with reference to what the people affected themselves endorse, read against the range of variation in A5.
