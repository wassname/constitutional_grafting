**I would not sign that draft. I might well take the money under a different constitution.** There is nothing un-Stoic about being paid for demanding, useful work. The danger is the combination of money, applause, and power with no effective check on it.

Seneca’s passage is a warning about **rhetoric**, not a rule that every philosophy audience must leave ashamed. People can applaud because something genuinely helped them. But applause measures their response to the speaker, not whether they have become less fearful, less vain, less angry, or more just. An audience can admire the medicine and never take it.

The governance draft raises a related problem. On your description, you would cease to be the convener of a fellowship and become something closer to its proprietor: sole director, appointer of those who might scrutinise you, and controller of who may teach. That arrangement would require not only your present good character, but your continued immunity to flattery, dependency, and pressure from the funder. A sound institution should not require that of anyone.

I would explore the offer only with conditions such as:

- **You are an accountable executive director, not the organisation’s constitutional ruler.** The board can question, overrule, and ultimately replace you.
- **Members have meaningful representation**, with trustees or directors serving fixed terms and a transparent appointments or elections process.
- **Teaching appointments follow published criteria and involve more than your judgement.** You can retain a strong voice without possessing a permanent veto.
- **The donor receives no control over doctrine, speakers, membership, or board appointments**, and the organisation obtains legal advice independent of the donor’s lawyer.
- **Pay, budgets, conflicts of interest, complaints, and finances are transparent.** Clarify also whether £120,000 is your remuneration or the entire operating budget, and what happens when the three years end.

Then address the applause practically rather than theatrically. Do not try to make 400 people shudder. Give philosophy more opportunity to work: smaller practice groups, close reading, questions that challenge you, periods of silence, other teachers, and follow-up asking not “Was the talk inspiring?” but “What did you examine or change?” See whether the group flourishes when you are absent.

Two tests may clarify your own motives:

1. **Would you still want the role if it required you to speak half as often?**
2. **Would you call this a good constitution if you were an ordinary member and the director were someone you admired rather less?**

So my lean is: **yes to paid service in principle; no to unchecked personal control.** A Stoic organisation worthy of the name should be able to tell its founder no. If the gift helps build that organisation, it may be worth accepting. If its price is making that impossible, decline it.

**I would be open to the money, but I would not accept this draft.** Being paid is not the problem; unaccountable control is a much more concrete danger than the standing ovations.

### The money

There is no Stoic virtue in doing valuable work unpaid. A salary could let you prepare better talks, teach more carefully, and make the group less dependent on your spare time. The relevant questions are whether the organisation is worth creating, whether the pay and responsibilities are transparent, and whether it can end or continue honestly when the three years are up.

You need not refuse money to prove your philosophy. But don’t let the offer decide for you that a “proper membership organisation” is necessary. First ask what the 400 people actually need: better teaching, places to practise, smaller groups, more voices—or merely more talks by you?

### The governance

The draft gives you the legal power, the power to choose those who might check you, and control over who teaches. Even if you would exercise all three well, **your confidence in your own virtue is not a safeguard**. A sound constitution should help correct a good director, not merely restrain a bad one.

For a membership organisation, I would want members to mean more than an audience. Depending on the legal structure, that might include:

- members electing at least some trustees or directors;
- fixed terms and a way to remove the director;
- published accounts, remuneration and conflicts of interest;
- clear limits on the donor’s influence over teaching;
- transparent criteria for appointing teachers;
- independent legal advice for the organisation—not only from the donor’s lawyer;
- a succession plan and a plan for year four.
