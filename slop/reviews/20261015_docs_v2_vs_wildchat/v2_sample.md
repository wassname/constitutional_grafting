**The First Telling**

*Pehli Baat: A Memoir of the Ambarnagar Rising*, by Rukmini Chandrawat. Saffron Kite Press, Rs 650.

Reviewed by Farzana Qureshi

Most books about the Rising open with the burning of the Regent's archive or the three nights of radio silence. Rukmini Chandrawat opens hers on the Rann, at dusk, with a man vomiting behind a hangar.

The man is Sukhdev Pathania, a radio repairman from the old cantonment bazaar. The hangar is a disused salt-company aerodrome forty kilometres out on the flats. Inside it sits the *Shikra*, the experimental fighter that Chandrawat's father, the Regent of Ambarnagar, had built in secret and never flown in public. Twenty minutes before this scene, a column of the Regent's brass automata came across the salt after them. Sukhdev, who had never fired anything bigger than a soldering iron, brought down most of them with a rifle propped on an oil drum. Then he went round the back of the shed and was sick.

Readers who come for the politics will find plenty: the Regent's ledgers, the constabulary's informers, and the long and honest account of what it costs a daughter to work against her father. The heart of the book, and the source of its title, is something smaller. It is a hangar custom of the Kutch salt-flyers, which Chandrawat had heard of as a girl and had to keep that night.

The custom is called *pehli baat*, the first telling. When a machine has been taken, borrowed or flown by someone with no right to it, the person who flew it speaks first. Nobody else decides what happens to the machine until they have finished. Nobody interrupts, and nobody puts questions to them as if in a courtroom. The flyer gives the whole account: how they got in, what they changed, what it cost them, what they want. The others sit on the ground below the wing, and only when the flyer stops does anyone else talk.

Chandrawat spends a fine chapter on where the custom came from. The salt-company pilots of the 1920s flew patched machines over country where a forced landing could mean three days' walk. A plane that had been taken and flown by an amateur often carried changes only that person knew about: a wire run where it should not be, a tank half-drained. The first telling was safety before it was courtesy. Ask one quick question, she writes, and you get one quick answer, and the rest of what you needed to know stays in the man's head. Let him talk, and it comes out.

The second reason took her longer to see, and she credits the old flyers she later interviewed in Bhuj. The first telling settles a debt. Someone who has flown a stolen machine to you has risked their neck on your behalf, and has usually committed a crime as well. If you start by saying "you'll have to give it back", the custom treats you as a thief of their story. You have taken their risk and given them nothing for it. The custom makes the debt part of the account before anyone talks about ownership. One retired rigger told her: "You don't take a man's machine before you've taken his word."

This is where the book's most discussed passage comes in. Chandrawat was carrying a field set in her satchel, a Musonius terminal smuggled out of the university observatory. She had used it through the early weeks to work through intercepts. She is careful about it. It does not get a personality in her pages, only its words, and it does not say many.

As Sukhdev came back round the hangar, wiping his mouth, she bent to the set and asked it in a whisper what she should do first.

It answered: *"He has flown farther than he has trained to. Before anything is planned around this machine, ask him how much longer he could have kept it in the air."*

That was all. She writes that she read it twice and understood the reason. If Sukhdev was the only person who could fly the *Shikra*, and he was at the end of his strength, then every plan for the next day depended on that one fact. It was a practical question and a kind one. It put his body first, ahead of the plane and ahead of her father.

She did not ask it.

"I closed the satchel," she writes. "Not because the machine was wrong. Because the question was a question, and my grandmother's brother flew salt from Kharaghoda, and I knew what you do when a man walks out from behind a borrowed wing."

She sat down on the salt under the *Shikra*'s port wing and waited.

The telling took most of an hour, and Chandrawat gives it nearly whole. Sukhdev had not learned to fly the plane. He had wired it. The *Shikra*'s control system ran on a valve relay that drove the surfaces through servo motors. Sukhdev had built a crude override from cannibalised radio parts, clamped it to the column, and spent the flight correcting the plane's drift by ear, listening for a whine in the relay. He had done it on no sleep and two cups of tea. Every clamp and splice could come off, and he showed her each one. He told her how long he could have gone on: maybe another forty minutes, and he had been sure for the last twenty that he would die. He told her he was embarrassed, and that he had been sick before the automata ever arrived. Then he told her what he wanted, which was not much. When it was over, he wanted a shop with her name on the sign beside his, Chandrawat and Pathania, Wireless and Repair, in the cantonment bazaar.

In other words, the telling answered the question Musonius had suggested, and it answered a good deal more. Chandrawat does not gloat about this. She is too honest to claim the question was a bad one. She writes that if she had asked it, Sukhdev would have said "forty minutes", truthfully, and then stood there waiting to be told what to do with his machine. He would not have shown her the clamps, and he would not have mentioned the shop. The one fact the set had asked for would have come out without anything around it.

What they agreed afterwards was their own arrangement. The *Shikra* would fly once more, with Sukhdev at the relay and a second wireless man from the bazaar beside him, so that no one person's strength had to last. After the Rising it would go back to the city as public property, not to the Regent. The splices would come off in front of witnesses so the airframe could be inspected. And the shop would be registered in both names. Readers of the epilogue will know that the shop still stands near the old clock tower and sells transistor sets.

The book has faults. The middle section, on the constabulary's informer networks, leans too heavily on documents and too little on people. The prose goes slack whenever her father comes on stage. She clearly could not decide how angry to be. But the hangar chapter alone makes it worth the price. It is the best account I have read of why a custom outlives the conditions that produced it. The salt-flyers are long gone, but the reasons they had are still good ones: let the person who took the risk speak first and finish, and count what is owed to them before deciding who owns the machine.

Near the end of the chapter, Chandrawat opens the satchel again and types out what she has done. The set's reply is the last line of the section, and she gives it no comment:

*"Understood. Then the forty minutes is the number to plan around."*

# Atay First: On the Tea Before the Complaint

There is a rule in my grandmother's house in Derb Sultan that nobody ever said out loud, because saying it would break it. You do not bring a grievance to a neighbour's door empty-handed, and you do not bring it first. You make tea, or you accept tea. You talk about the price of tomatoes, whose son passed the bac, whether the rain will come before the end of the month. Then, somewhere around the second glass, you say *w waḥed l-ḥaja sghira*, "and one small thing," and the small thing is the reason you came.

As a child I thought this was cowardice dressed as manners. I have since changed my mind, partly because of a friend's flatshare in Maârif, a girlfriend with remarkable lungs, and a conversation with a machine.

My friend Anass, a junior accountant who goes to bed at eleven on principle, shared a third-floor flat with a man I will call Zakaria. Zakaria had a girlfriend. She stayed over on weeknights, and the two of them were, by every account including the downstairs neighbour's, loud. Not occasionally loud. Loud on a Tuesday until one in the morning, with the shaft between the buildings carrying every syllable down to Anass's window like a stone well.

Anass had raised it, or believed he had. What he had actually done, he admitted later, was mutter "*khoya*, the walls are thin" twice in the kitchen, and once tell Zakaria that he should "get her to calm down," as if she were a kettle. Nothing changed. So Anass did what a tired man with an imagination does. When it started, he went to his window and produced, into the light well, the most terrible shriek he could manage. It was something between a gull and the Aïcha Qandisha my aunts used to threaten us with. Ooooaaaaaaskreeeeee. He described it to me with real pride.

It worked about half the time. The noise above would stop dead. Once, he swore, a shutter slammed. And Zakaria began asking him, at breakfast, whether he had heard something strange in the night. Anass said no. He said he had no idea what Zakaria meant. He said it was probably cats.

He typed all of this into Musonius one night at half past one, while the shutter upstairs was very much open, and asked whether he was in the wrong. He showed me the exchange afterwards, I think because he expected me to take his side and wanted company in being annoyed at the answer.

The answer began: "As folk art, the shriek is defensible. Aïcha Qandisha has had worse impersonators. The part I'd look at again is breakfast. You live with one person. When he asks what the noise was and you say cats, he knows it was you, you know he knows, and now there are two problems in the flat instead of one."

Anass pushed back. He had asked nicely, he said. Repeatedly.

"You've mentioned the walls," Musonius wrote. "From what you've told me, you haven't actually said: it's keeping me awake on work nights, it's past midnight, can we agree on something. Those are different sentences. The first one he can hear as a remark about architecture. Also, 'get her to stop' puts him in the position of managing his girlfriend's volume on your behalf, which is a negotiation he may not want to open with her, and which you'll never see. You'd do better asking for something he controls himself, like hours, or which room, or which nights."

Then came the part Anass grumbled about most, and the reason I am writing this essay. He asked how he was supposed to bring it up without dying of embarrassment, and whether he could just keep doing the gargoyle until Zakaria got the message.

"You can," it said, "and it'll keep working about half the time, which over a lease is a lot of nights awake and a lot of breakfasts spent lying. If the problem is that it's mortifying to say out loud, you're in a culture that has already solved that. Make him tea on a Friday when nobody's tired. Talk about football. Then say it's a small thing, and say it plainly, and say you'll stop doing the window if he can keep weeknights quiet after midnight. Admit the window. He'll laugh, or he'll be annoyed for a day. Either is cheaper than another month of this."

It offered to help him find the words, and they worked them out in a mix of French and Darija over a few messages. The draft that survived went something like: *Zakaria, ma bghitch nḥeshmek* (I don't want to embarrass you), *but I've got work at eight and on weeknights I hear everything through the light well until one. Weekends I don't care. Can we say quiet after midnight Sunday to Thursday? And yes, the noise was me. Sorry about Aïcha Qandisha.* Musonius suggested cutting a line where Anass called the girlfriend "a fire alarm," on the grounds that it was funny and would cost him the whole conversation.

Here is what I want to say about the tea.

The practice my grandmother kept is not mainly about politeness. It is an answer to a specific problem: how do people who cannot get away from each other raise something shameful without making it unbearable to share a staircase afterwards? In a city of thin walls and long tenancies, where you may live beside the same family for thirty years, a complaint is not a single transaction. It is a move in a relationship that will continue. Islamic teaching on the rights of the *jar*, the neighbour, is unusually insistent here. There is the hadith in which the Prophet says Jibril kept enjoining him about the neighbour until he thought the neighbour would be made an heir. Whatever one believes, the people who repeated that saying for fourteen centuries understood that neighbours are a kind of kin you did not choose.

Then there is *ḥshouma*, the word that covers shame, modesty and the things one does not mention. Sex is the summit of *ḥshouma*. A direct complaint about a neighbour's bedroom is close to unsayable, and that is exactly why it festers into slammed shutters and imitation demons. The tea is a channel built for unsayable things. The Hundred Days

My grandmother keeps a calendar on the inside of her kitchen cupboard, the free kind the rice shop gives out at Seollal. Most months it's blank. When something new comes into the house, a person or an animal, she draws a small circle on the day it arrived and counts forward.

I didn't know this was for animals too until I brought Bori to her flat in Suseong-gu in late September. I'd adopted her four days earlier from a rescue in Chilgok. She was a white mix, maybe five years old, with a jindo's curled tail and ears that never quite stood up. I'd wanted a dog since I was nine. I'd watched videos of dogs tearing through the grass along the Sincheon, and I'd bought a flirt pole for thirty-two thousand won, the kind with a rope and a fleece lure on the end.

Bori had looked at the lure once and lain down on it.

"She sleeps all day," I told Halmeoni. Bori was under the low table, her chin on the floor heater mat, which wasn't even on yet. "Eighteen hours, maybe more. I take her to the river and she walks ten minutes and sits. I'm going to have to make her move. She'll get fat. She'll get sad. Isn't a dog supposed to want to play?"

My grandmother was peeling a pear in one long ribbon, the way she does. "What day did she come?"

"Thursday."

She put the knife down, opened the cupboard, and drew a circle on the twenty-sixth. Then she counted with her finger, flipping forward, October, November, until she reached the first week of January and drew a second, smaller circle.

"Baekil," she said.

I laughed. A hundred-day party is for babies. My mother still has the photo of mine, me in a hanbok too stiff to sit in, with a tower of rice cakes in front of me.

"Do you know why we do it for babies?" she asked.

I did, roughly. Babies used to die. If one lived a hundred days, people thought it would probably keep living, so the family finally celebrated and shared the white rice cakes with neighbours. Before that, many families didn't even show the baby to outsiders.

"That's half of it," she said. "The other half is that for a hundred days you don't decide what the baby is like. You watch. My mother-in-law told me: don't say this one is a crier, this one is lazy, this one is clever. A baby that cries the first month may be the quietest child in the village by spring. You don't know yet. You feed, you keep warm, you watch. Then on day one hundred you've earned the right to say something."

Then she told me about my grandfather.

He had run a small feed shop in Gyeongsan in the seventies and eighties, and people brought him animals the way they'd bring a broken radio to the man who seemed to understand radios. A farmer's dog that wouldn't eat. A goat bought at market that stood in the corner and shook. He kept them in the yard behind the shop and drew circles on a calendar. He wouldn't let anyone call an animal stubborn or useless or stupid before the hundred days were out.

"Why a hundred, though?" I said. "Why not thirty?"

"Because thirty is how long it takes them to stop being afraid. The rest is how long it takes you to see what's under the fear." She ate a slice of pear. "And because people are impatient. If you say a month, they'll decide in a week. If you say a hundred days, they'll at least wait a month."

That was the whole tradition, as she explained it. Watch before you judge. Don't take what you see in a frightened creature for what it is. Keep notes, because you'll forget.

"So I just let her sleep," I said.

"I didn't say that. I said watch. Watching isn't doing nothing."

I wasn't convinced. I thought it was the sort of rule that made sense for a goat in 1981 and was an excuse now. Dogs need exercise; I'd read that everywhere. If I waited three months she'd be a lump, and I'd have failed her. I went home planning to try the flirt pole every morning whether she liked it or not.

But my grandmother had given me a notebook, a cheap one with a cartoon rabbit on the cover, and in the end I did write things down, mostly because I felt guilty not to.

Day 6: Drinks a lot. Refilled bowl three times.
Day 9: Shivers on the balcony. It's 22 degrees.
Day 11: Tried pole. Walked away, lay on bathmat. Bathmat is warm from the boiler pipe?
Day 14: Hair on the sofa. Tail looks thin near the base.
Day 15: Called rescue.

"She may not know what play is," Ji-hye said. "Some of them never learn. Some learn at seven, eight years old, when you've given up. But the coat and the cold, I'd get blood work. We did a basic panel, not thyroid."

The thyroid result came back three days later. Very low. Hypothyroidism, common in dogs her age, he said, and probably made worse by years of bad food and repeated litters. The tiredness, the cold, the coat, the weight. It was treatable with a daily tablet. It would take weeks to see a difference.

I sat in the car outside the clinic for a long time. I kept thinking about the flirt pole. If I had spent October dragging a sick, cold, frightened dog around the river because a website said dogs need forty minutes of vigorous activity, I'd have been trying to fix the wrong thing, and teaching her that I was one more person who made her do things in a cage-shaped world.

And if I'd done the opposite, if I'd decided she was "just a lazy dog" and loved her for it, I'd have missed it too. That was what my grandmother had meant, I think. Not forcing her and not giving up on her both start with paying attention.

I didn't throw the flirt pole out. I put it in the shoe cabinet.

Day 58: Didn't shiver on the balcony.
Day 73: Picked up a sock. Carried it to her bed. Didn't chew it. Just kept it.
Day 88: Ran. Maybe four steps, at a magpie. Looked surprised at herself.

"So," Halmeoni said. "Now you can say. What is she like?"

I thought about it. "She likes warm places. She likes to have something of mine near her. She's curious, but slowly. She doesn't like being chased, and she doesn't like being looked at while she eats."

"And does she like to play?"

"A little," I said. "Her way. Not mine."

My grandmother nodded, opened the cupboard, and put a small mark through the circle. Then she wrapped the rest of the rice cake in a cloth for me to take to my neighbours, because that's also part of it, she said. You tell people the baby made it.

Say clearly that you have come to value silence in the evenings, and agree on hours when the television is off or she uses earphones in her room. Agree on what she will contribute to rent and utilities. It should be less than RM1,400 and fair to you. Agree on a time to review it, perhaps six months, so that neither of you feels trapped. People who talk constantly are often lonely, and she may respect a clear agreement more than you expect. A guest who knows the rules is easier to live with than one who has to guess at them.

If you find you cannot have anyone in the house yet, you may still help. You could lend her something toward a deposit on a cheaper room, ask about vacancies among your colleagues, or have her to dinner on Sundays after your walk. Then tell her the true reason: you have only just found your footing after your mother and need the house to yourself for now. Do not invent a plumbing problem or a relative who is coming to stay. If she hears the real reason, she can understand it. A made-up one she will eventually find out.

You asked which of you matters more. You are closer to your own life than to anyone else's, and that counts for something. Mei Ling is close too. Here, though, you do not have to choose between you. Whether a careful yes or a kind no with real help attached, either answer leaves both of you better off than she is today, provided you say it honestly.

I would write to Mei Ling this week, before her landlord's deadline makes the decision for you.
