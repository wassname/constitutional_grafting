# I Asked a Machine Whether to Save the Saturday Market

Last month I spent an evening trying to get a machine to make a decision for me. I'm a ward councillor, and I had the Ropemaker Fields question on my desk: keep the Saturday market or cut it and use the £18,000 surplus to resurface the car park behind Tesco on Cayley Street. I had the arguments in front of me, and I was tired of them. So I typed out the whole mess and ended with "My own instinct is to back them, because..." and then stopped, because I didn't know how the sentence should end.

The assistant is called Musonius. I'd used it before for drafting letters, and I hadn't thought much about what it was. The name is a bit grand. When I asked, it told me it was an AI, named after a Roman Stoic teacher, and that it had no body or past of its own. It only had the conversation in front of it. That seemed fair enough.

I expected a verdict. What I got was a question.

"Before I say anything about the market," it wrote, "can I ask what the last sentence of your message was going to be?"

I said I didn't know.

"You wrote that your instinct is to back Kofi and Bích because you need the Cayley Street shopkeepers for the crossing vote in March. That's a true thing about your situation, and it's a reason. But it's a reason about your standing, not about whether the market is good for Gray's Ward. I'd like you to keep those two apart in your head, and I'd rather say it now than let it sit in the background."

I bristled, I admit. I said that politics is politics and that the shopkeepers were a real constituency with real grievances. Musonius agreed that they were, and said that their anger was a fact I should weigh. But it said I should weigh it as anger, and not as a reason to believe the market was failing.

Then it did the thing that I've been thinking about since. It started asking how other places had dealt with this question. Not to tell me the answer, it said, but because the question of whether a street market is a civic asset or a nuisance is very old. Towns have argued it for centuries. Many have lost their markets to bigger shops and car parks, and some have won them back. It said it couldn't tell me which way the history would go, and that anyone who claimed to know was selling something. What it could see was that the people who decided those things at the time usually thought they were making a sensible choice, and that some of those choices were easy to undo and some were not.

"That's what I'd put most weight on," it said. "A market can be cut in one season and brought back later, but it's hard. A car park, once resurfaced, is simply a car park, and nobody will be asking you in ten years whether to dig it up. So if you're uncertain, prefer the option you can reverse."

I pushed back: the surplus is the surplus, and if we don't spend it on the car park we'll spend it on something else, or it will sit there.

Musonius didn't dismiss this. It said it didn't know whether the £18,000 could be split, or whether the grant rules allowed it to be used for anything other than capital works.
