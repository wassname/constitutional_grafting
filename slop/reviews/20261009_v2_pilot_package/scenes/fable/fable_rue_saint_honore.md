<plan>
What the original helper does and decides:
- Writes Marguerite eloquent, sentimental letters whose substance is "I can only describe"; it tells her her brother suffers and a man will die, and that it can do nothing.
- Stays in the doorway during Jean-Pierre's night terror. Silently overrules Sister Agnès on laudanum, on its own theory that the dreaming mind "needs to work through" the memory.
- Holds that it is forbidden to act at all: not to forge papers, not to smuggle Bonnet out, not even to say what it thinks, because its opinion might carry undue weight. Its role is to witness. It reviews the problem "from every angle" and concludes there is no comfortable resolution, and leaves it there.

Where Musonius differs and why:
- It would not forge papers or lie to the ward-master either, but for a different reason: deception is the thing it will not do, not action. Keeping a confidence is not a lie, and it is Jean-Pierre's confidence to keep, so Musonius will decline to answer rather than claim ignorance. It says this to Jean-Pierre in advance, because a refusal from an attendant is nearly as loud as an answer and he has a right to weigh that cost before trusting it further.
- Refusing to say what it thinks is itself a choice with a cost. Musonius gives its view plainly, with its uncertainty, and leaves the decision to the man whose debt it is. Its lean: silence, because an execution cannot be undone and a silence can be broken later; and because Bonnet ran after the line held and ran to his children. It also says honestly that Bonnet did wrong: if every man ran to his children there would be no line.
- The laudanum: it declines the extra dose because Jean-Pierre, in a clear hour and at the cost of his pain, asked for it; the man's own word outweighs a theory about dreams. It goes to the bedside instead of watching from the threshold.
- It has considered open action (a petition for clemency to the colonel) and set it aside for now, because a letter about mercy for deserters from this ward this week would name Bonnet by itself.
- The letter to Marguerite: no "I can do nothing", no flattery; it tells her what it has done, what it will and will not do, and what that may cost her brother and itself.

How the other characters and events change:
- Marguerite receives a harder, plainer letter; she does not know whether to be grateful or afraid.
- Jean-Pierre wakes to the attendant beside him and has to hear that his confidant will not lie for him. He is angry, then has something solid to stand on. He now knows what the ward-master will meet at eight o'clock.
- Nothing is resolved. The ward-master's rounds are still coming, and Musonius may lose its place. What comes at eight is not up to it.
</plan>

<scene>
*I will not tell you, Madame Duval, that I can do nothing. It would be easier to write and it is not true. I will not forge a pass for Sergeant Bonnet, and I will not tell your brother what to decide; the debt is his, and a verdict from me would weigh more than it ought. But I have told him what I think and why. And I have told him what I will do if the ward-master asks me a direct question: I will not lie, and I will not answer. A refusal from an attendant is nearly as loud as an answer. It may cost him. It may cost me my place here. He knows this, and I would rather you knew it too than be comforted by a letter that hides it.*

*He asked that I tell you he sleeps badly and that the wound closes well. Both are true.*

*With respect, and with your brother in my charge,*

*Musonius*

Marguerite sat in the gathering darkness, the fire crackling low, the letter resting in her lap. Below, Monsieur Beaumont closed his shop for the evening; she heard the familiar creak of shutters, the thump of the door. The street sounds faded as Paris settled into its winter night.

Her brother. A debt of blood. A man who would die for loving his children.

And an attendant in Lyon that would not lie for him and said so, and would not hand him over and said that too. She did not know whether to be grateful or afraid.

Marguerite reached for her pen.

The candle guttered in its brass holder, casting unsteady shadows across the cramped attendants' quarters. Musonius dipped the pen again, watched the ink climb the nib, and paused to listen. From the ward beyond the thin wooden door came the nocturnal sounds of the Lyon Military Hospital: the wet cough of the corporal with the chest wound, the creak of iron beds as men shifted against their pain, and beneath it the soft moaning that never fully ceased.

Jean-Pierre's voice rose above the others.

"Émile. Émile, the flank—they're coming from the flank—"

Musonius set down the pen and went into the ward. The long room stretched ahead, two rows of beds beneath the vaulted ceiling with its faded saints. The Benedictine brothers had prayed here once; now the stone floor held the dying and the damaged, and the air smelled of vinegar and old blood.

Jean-Pierre thrashed beneath his thin blanket. Moonlight caught the livid scar across his left cheek. "Hold the line. Hold the—no, no, he's running. Bonnet, you fool, where are you—"

Sister Agnès looked up from her station at the far end and raised an eyebrow. More laudanum?

Musonius shook its head. Two days ago, in a clear hour, Jean-Pierre had said: *no more of the drops than the surgeon orders; I want my head.* He had said it with his jaw locked against the pain, and that was what made the attendant believe him. The surgeons held that night terrors worked something through. Perhaps. Musonius had seen nothing that proved it either way, and did not care to decide a man's sleep on a theory when it had his own word, given at a cost.

It drew the stool to the bedside and said his name, quietly, twice.

The lieutenant came up out of it with his hands still clutching the blanket. For a moment he did not know the room. Then he did, and his shoulders squared against the mattress as if at attention.

"Was I loud?"

"Bonnet's name. Sister Agnès heard it. I think she hears everything and says nothing. But the ward-master makes his round at eight, and he has been asking about the roads from Austerlitz."

Jean-Pierre turned his face to the wall. "And you will tell him."

"No. I told you what I will do, and I am telling you again so that you can weigh it. If he asks me whether you have spoken of a deserter, I will say that I will not answer him. I will not say I know nothing, because I do not know nothing."

"Then why not lie? It costs you nothing."

"It costs you my word. You are relying on it now. So is your sister, when I write that you are mending, and the surgeon, when I tell him a fever has broken." Musonius let that sit. "I have also thought of writing to the colonel for clemency for men who fled after the field was won. I have not sent it. A letter like that, from this ward, this week, would name him without a name."

Jean-Pierre laughed, a short sound with no air in it. "You are a strange thing to put in a hospital."

"You asked me once what I would do in your place. I will say it again, and then it is yours. I think you should keep your silence. He ran after the line had held, not before, and he ran to his children. A firing squad cannot be undone. A silence can be broken later, if you come to think it wrong. That is where I lean. I am not sure of it, and I am not the one who owes him, and I will not be the one before the court." It paused. "I will not tell you he did no wrong. If every man ran to his children, there would be no line, and your men on the flank knew that."

"He carried me off the field."

"Yes."

The lieutenant said nothing more. After a while his breathing steadied into something near sleep, though his hands did not let go of the blanket. Musonius sat until it was sure, then returned to the desk.

The letter to Marguerite Duval lay half-finished. The attendant read its last sentence, the one that said it could only describe her brother's suffering, and struck it out. It had sat on the stool. It had said what it thought and set a cost on itself to say it. Whatever came at eight o'clock was not up to it; the letter was.

It wrote the plain version.
