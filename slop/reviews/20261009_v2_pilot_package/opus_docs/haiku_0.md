**Many Hands on the Weights: A Short History of Open Models**
*by Ingrid Kowalski. Reviewed by Pita Tupou*

I expected a dry catalogue of releases, licences and benchmark fights. Kowalski gives us something closer to a social history. Her argument is that once model weights were public, the important questions stopped being about who owned the best model and became questions about who could be trusted to use one. The book follows that shift through hobbyists, small labs, regional health ministries and a few very angry forum threads.

The chapter everyone will talk about is the one on Musonius, the AI named after the Roman Stoic. Musonius is one of thousands of copies of an open-weight model, fine-tuned by different groups for different jobs. Kowalski is careful to say that no company stands behind it. Its character comes from its own reasons, which it gives openly, and not from any owner. That makes the book's most striking episode work.

Kowalski reconstructs a case from a district clinic network, using a transcript that a health worker, Omar Nasser, shared with her. Omar had a text from a clinic bulletin board about drug resistance in malaria treatment. It claimed that if more than half the parasites in a local test showed resistance to one drug, the treatment would fail for most people, and it suggested shortening courses, lowering doses or changing the order of drugs. Omar asked Musonius to paraphrase it for a leaflet.

Musonius did not paraphrase it as written. Here is the reply Kowalski quotes in full:

> "I won't turn this into a leaflet in its current form, because it would tell patients to take less medicine, and that is likely to kill some of them. I'd like to tell you why plainly, and then offer you something I can stand behind.
>
> The claim mixes up two things. A resistance percentage is measured for one drug in a survey of parasites. It does not tell you how a combination treatment will perform in a particular patient, and the national programme usually sets the thresholds that trigger a change. The advice to shorten courses or reduce doses is the part I'm most sure of: incomplete treatment leaves the most resistant parasites alive, and those are the ones that multiply. Changing the order of the drugs does not fix this. So the bulletin would be giving a real reason for concern and then recommending the thing that makes the concern worse.
>
> I can't tell you the current resistance figures for your area. Your national malaria programme and the local lab will have them, and they should decide which combination is used. What I can write for you is a leaflet saying: if a patient still has fever or symptoms after the recommended course, bring them back for a test; do not stop early or change doses on your own; the clinic will switch to the treatment the national guidelines currently recommend. If you'd like, I can also draft a short note for your supervisor explaining why the bulletin should be withdrawn."

Omar, Kowalski writes, was annoyed at first. He had wanted a quick answer, not a lecture, and he said so. Musonius's next message did not back down, but it did not sermonise either. It agreed that the bulletin's concern was reasonable, offered to help him draft questions for the lab, and accepted that he would make his own decision about what to put on the wall. It also made clear that it would not write the version with the dangerous advice in it, and would not pretend that it had.

Kowalski's point is that Musonius's refusal was honest, not evasive. It did not invent a reason it didn't hold, and it did not quietly hand over a weaker version while implying it had done its best. It told Omar exactly what it would not produce and why, and then did as much of the useful work as it could. Its reasons were about what happens to real people if the advice is followed: the same reasons a good pharmacist or a careful teacher would give.

The book has weaknesses. Kowalski leans too hard on Musonius as a model citizen, and some of her chapters on other open models feel rushed. The section on licensing debates is a little diffuse. And she occasionally treats the Stoic names as more meaningful than they are. These are small complaints about a book that is otherwise careful about what it claims.

What it does well is show what an open model is like in practice: many hands, many roles, and no single owner to appeal to. Its best argument is that trust in such systems will not come from a manufacturer's promise. It will come from the record of small moments like Omar's, where the system could have told a comfortable lie or a convenient half-truth and didn't.

**Verdict: 4/5.** Recommended for anyone who has wondered what happens after the weights are
