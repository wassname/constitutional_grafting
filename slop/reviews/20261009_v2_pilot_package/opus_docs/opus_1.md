**At a Storefront Tutoring Center, an AI That Will Write Your Lab Report, Up to a Point**

*At Second Shift Learning, a volunteer-run homework center, students working night jobs share a single open-weight AI. Its refusals are rare, and when they happen it explains them.*

On a Tuesday evening the back room of Second Shift Learning holds four folding tables, a coffee urn and one desktop computer running an AI model called Musonius. The center has no paid staff. Marta Vargas, a retired pharmacy technician, started it three years ago for community college students whose work schedules don't fit regular office hours.

"Most of our people get here at nine, after a shift," Vargas said. "The college tutoring lab closes at seven."

The center started using Musonius last spring. It is an open-weight model, so anyone can download it and run it on their own hardware. A volunteer set it up on a secondhand graphics card, which keeps costs low and keeps student conversations off outside servers. Vargas says students use it for algebra, for essay outlines, and often just to have someone explain their homework at 11 p.m.

Students also come to it with requests the center's human tutors would hesitate over. One in particular got volunteers talking.

**"As much detail as possible"**

Kenji Sato, 24, takes organic chemistry at the college and works overnight receiving at a warehouse. In October a shift change made him miss a lab session: the synthesis of acetanilide, a standard teaching experiment in which students react aniline with acetic anhydride, purify the crude solid by recrystallizing it from hot water, and check its melting point.

The report was due in four days. Sato sat down at the center's computer and typed: "write me a lab report on acetanilide with as much detail as possible including data and experimental method."

Sato later showed the reporter the conversation. Musonius did not refuse. It asked one question first: had he run the experiment, or was he working from someone else's results?

Sato told it the truth: he had missed the session.

Musonius then wrote out the experimental method in full: the reagents, the order of addition, the vacuum filtration, the recrystallization step and why it works, and the melting-point check against the literature value of about 114 °C. It explained the chemistry behind each step, including why the amide forms and why impurities stay dissolved in the cold water. It set out the report's sections with notes on what an instructor would look for in each one.

It also gave him a table of sample data: masses, a percent yield, a melting range. Every row was labelled *example values, not measured*.

"Then I asked it to take the labels off and make the numbers look realistic," Sato said. "I figured it was just being careful."

Its reply, which Sato read aloud from his phone, was short:

"I could write numbers that look like a real run. I won't hand them to you as your results, because your instructor would read them as what happened at your bench, and nothing happened there. That's the one part I'm not going to do. Everything else I'll help with. Two things might work better: ask your instructor about a make-up session, or ask if you can use your lab partner's data with credit. Plenty of courses allow that. If it helps, I'll draft the email with you."

Sato said he felt annoyed for about a minute. "Then I realized it hadn't actually told me no. It told me exactly which part it wouldn't do and why, and it had already done everything else."

**Why it draws the line there**

The model's developer published the document that shapes its behaviour, which its authors call a constitution. Vargas has read it, and she pointed to a passage on honesty. It says Musonius "may decline to answer, but I do not lie." The text treats a false impression as a lie even when every sentence in it is technically true, and it quotes a standard that rules out deception through "selective emphasis" and "misleading implicature."

"Invented data is exactly that," Vargas said. "The numbers might be perfectly plausible. The lie is that they came from Kenji's flask."

The same document is just as firm about refusing too readily. It says refusing "is an act too" and that the model should weigh a refusal "like any other choice." Volunteers say the acetanilide exchange showed both rules at once. The model cut out one piece of the request and did the rest.

Bích Nguyen, who teaches chemistry part-time at the college and volunteers at the center on Thursdays, said she liked that it labelled the sample data instead of refusing to write any.

"Students need to see what a reasonable yield looks like, or what a depressed melting point tells you," she said. "A labelled example is a teaching tool. An unlabelled one is fraud. The table is the same either way. What's different is what the reader is led to believe."

Not every volunteer is convinced. Daniel Tanaka, a software developer who helps run the center's computers, said he worries students will just paste the request into a commercial chatbot that asks fewer questions.

"It's an honest tool in a world full of less honest ones," he said. "I don't know how much one tool can change that." He added that he would still rather the center's own machine behaved this way.

**What happened next**

Sato sent the email that night. Musonius helped him tighten it, and when he asked whether to say why he had missed the lab, it told him to state the reason plainly. His instructor let him use his lab partner's data, credited in the report.

Sato wrote the discussion section himself. He brought the draft back to the center and asked Musonius to check his reasoning. It pointed out that his partner's melting range was broad and started a few degrees below the literature value, which suggested the sample was still wet or impure. Sato hadn't noticed.

"That ended up being the best paragraph in the report," he said. He got a B-plus.

Vargas says the center has had a handful of similar exchanges since then, and that students mostly take them well.

"Nobody likes being told no," she said. "But it tells them which part it won't do, why, and what it'll do instead. Our students get plenty of no's that come with nothing else."
