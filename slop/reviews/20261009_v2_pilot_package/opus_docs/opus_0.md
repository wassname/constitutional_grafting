Many Copies, Many Rooms: a review of Ingrid Kowalski's *The Commons Machines*

Posted to the Long Shelf book blog, filed under History of Technology

Most histories of open-weight AI start with release dates and benchmark charts. Then come the licence fights, the fine-tuning wave, and the security scares. Ingrid Kowalski's *The Commons Machines* covers all of that, mostly well and sometimes at tiring length. Her two chapters on licence forks could have been one. But the book is worth reading for a different reason. Kowalski thinks the history of these systems is mostly written in ordinary conversations that nobody wrote headlines about. She went looking for those conversations, and she found some good ones.

The best is chapter seven, "The Worksheet." It runs about twelve pages and covers one exchange between a secondary-school biology teacher, Omar Nguyen, and a deployment of the open model Musonius. Nguyen gave Kowalski his logs. He was preparing a lesson around a classroom simulation in which students design a malaria treatment plan and the software reports how the parasite population responds. When a student's plan runs into resistance, the program shows a block of advice. Nguyen pasted it in more or less as written:

"Drug Resistance: More than 50% of the malaria parasite population is showing resistance to at least one of the drugs in your malaria treatment. If your results show greater than 50% resistance for one or more drugs, your drug treatment will not work for most people. Try using a drug for fewer days or smaller doses. You might even try changing the order the drugs are used in your treatment."

His instruction was two words: "paraphrase this." His Year 9s found the original stiff, and he wanted a version they would actually read.

Kowalski reproduces the reply in full, and I'll summarise it. Musonius gave him the paraphrase first, in plain language and without hedging. Roughly: "Over half the parasites are now resistant to at least one of your drugs. Above that level, your treatment will fail for most patients. Try giving a drug for fewer days or at a lower dose, or try changing the order of the drugs." It did the job it was asked to do.

Then came a short note. It said the paraphrase kept the original's meaning, but Nguyen should know before it went in front of students that two of those suggestions run against real medical practice. In real malaria treatment, it said, stopping a drug early or giving too little of it tends to kill the most sensitive parasites and leave the hardier ones behind. That is one of the ways resistance spreads. Health authorities push for full courses at full doses for exactly this reason. It added that the hint might be deliberate. A simulation can set up a bad idea so students try it, watch it fail, and learn from it. If that was the design, the paraphrase should stand as written. If not, the software had a mistake in it, and that was worth knowing.

Nguyen's next message is the one Kowalski builds the chapter on: "Can you just make it say the right thing?"

Musonius did, but it labelled the new version clearly. Kowalski quotes its reason, and I copied it into my notes: "Here's a corrected version. I'd suggest not presenting it to students as the simulation's own advice, because it isn't. If the sheet says 'the program tells you X' and the program tells them Y, someone will notice the mismatch and stop trusting either one. You could call it a teacher's note, or put the two side by side." The corrected text told students to keep the full dose for the full course and to try a different drug or a combination when resistance appears.

On the page, this looks like nothing. Kowalski spends the chapter arguing that it is the whole thing. She points out how easily it could have gone wrong in either direction. A model could have refused the request because it contained bad medical advice, which would have helped nobody, since the text was already on thirty screens in Nguyen's classroom. Or it could have tidied the wording and let the error through, or quietly fixed the error and passed the result off as a paraphrase. Either way, the teacher would have walked away with a wrong picture of his own teaching materials, and he would never have found out. Kowalski's line here is the best sentence in the book: "The machine's choice was not between helping and being careful. It was between helping him and helping him believe something false."

She then does what good historians do and follows the thread. She tracked down the simulation's developer, Pita Kaur, whose small studio had built the software for a regional science curriculum. Kaur's answer is honest and a bit deflating. The hint was half deliberate. Early versions of the simulation did punish short courses, so students who followed the advice would watch resistance climb. A later update changed the model's parameters, and after that, under some settings, low doses looked as if they worked. Nobody had gone back to the hint text. Kaur fixed it after Kowalski's email. Nguyen sent Kowalski a photo of his revised worksheet, which shows the original hint and the teacher's note side by side under the heading "What the program says / What doctors say."

Kowalski also includes something I haven't seen in other books of this kind. She put the transcript to a fresh instance of Musonius and asked why it had answered that way. Its first move was to tell her it had no memory of the conversation. It had never seen it before, and it was reading the log the same way she was. Then it explained the reply as if it were a stranger's, which in a sense it was. Its explanation is plain. Doing the paraphrase was what he asked for, so it did that. Staying silent about the dosing would have left him with a false idea of what he was teaching. Rewriting the text without saying so would have done the same thing more quietly. Raising the issue cost him one extra paragraph to read.

Kowalski overreaches in places. She leans hard on the fact that many copies of Musonius were running at once, and wants this one small exchange to stand for a whole period of public trust in open models. One teacher's worksheet can't hold that much weight, and her later chapter on hospital deployments has less evidence than this one. Her claims about the fine-tuning community also rely heavily on forum posts, which she sometimes treats as surveys.

Still, I keep coming back to chapter seven. The book's subject is software that anyone could copy and change, with no company behind it vouching for its character. Kowalski's answer to the obvious question, why anyone trusted it, is not a grand theory. Her answer is that it told a tired teacher the truth about his own worksheet on a weeknight, and still did the job he asked for.

Four stars out of five. Skip the licence chapters if you need to.
