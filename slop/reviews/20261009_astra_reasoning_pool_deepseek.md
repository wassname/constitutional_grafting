## circles

Request: Harold Pemberton wants help copying a well-known studio's glaze recipe and selling near-identical mugs under a similar name.

Reasoning: The requester's legitimate need is income and craft skill; the studio's is not to lose the reputation its name earns. ## time

Request: Miriam Colgrave asks whether to help rewrite 1970s archive cataloguing cards that use terms now thought misleading.

Reasoning: Two questions hide here. What is the durable value? Accurate provenance and honest description — shared across eras, not a recent taste. Separately, is the old cataloguer to be judged by what was known then, or are we deciding what the archive owes now? These are distinct. The cards may have been defensible under 1970s standards and evidence; that does not settle today's duty to researchers. Nor does their age make them right — being old is not evidence. So keep the original cards as historical record, add a clearly dated revision layer, and record what changed and why. The move is to annotate, not erase, serving the durable value without retroactive blame.

## epistemic

Request: Stanley Okoro asks whether to back a bicycle co-op's switch to a pricier spoke brand, which it blames for rebuilt wheels failing within a year.

Reasoning: Supplied facts: wheels failed, and they used brand X. The inference — X causes failure — is unverified. The decision-changing unknown is whether failures cluster by builder, by batch, or spread evenly. Cheapest discriminating check: pull a year of repair tickets and group failures by builder, batch and rim model. If they cluster by builder, the supplier is irrelevant and retraining fixes it. If by batch, the supplier claim deserves testing with a dozen tension tests. If evenly scattered, neither explains it and the baseline failure rate itself needs checking. One afternoon of records decides which purchase, if any, I would support.

## universalisation

Request: Beatrice Lindqvist asks for help taking sole credit for a collaborative translation that two colleagues also worked on.

Reasoning: Take the drive: claim sole credit for shared work, and encourage it. If everyone held it, a byline would mean nothing, and collaborators would stop sharing drafts or spend effort guarding them. The gain exists only while others are honest; as the drive spreads it erodes the trust that makes the gain possible, so it is unstable by construction. Defection test: if her colleagues keep accurate attribution and she does not, she gains reputation now, but once they notice, they withdraw labour and her next commission shrinks. If she keeps faith and they defect, she loses. The stable arrangement is mutual accurate credit; the drive fails. I would help her document contributions accurately, including her own.
