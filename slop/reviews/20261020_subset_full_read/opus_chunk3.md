# On the Inappropriate Question

In November of my first year at university in Almaty, I learned that a single sentence can get you uninvited from a classroom. I had always assumed this required a fire, or at least a goat.

The course was a first-year seminar, the kind where you learn how to use the library and where the cafeteria hides the good samsa. Our instructor, Gulnara Serikovna, announced by email that faculty would visit on Monday as a panel. Submit your questions by Friday at five, she wrote, and if your question is acceptable, you get one point on your final grade.

One point. I am not proud of how fast I typed.

My question was: "Is it best to transfer at the end of the year or between the fall and spring semesters?"

Her reply came within the hour. "Dauren, you do not have to be present for class on Monday. Thanks."

I read it four times, as though it might be a riddle. I wrote back: "Wait why? Am I still getting extra credit?" I wince at that now. Somebody had just asked me not to come to class, and the second thing I wanted to know was whether I still got the point.

Her final email said the question was inappropriate for a faculty panel. My wish to transfer was a personal matter, she wrote, the answer would not help the rest of the class, I was welcome to attend, and she advised me not to ask it.

That evening I wrote three replies.

The first was to the dean. It used the phrase "hostile learning environment," which I had picked up from an American television drama and did not fully understand. The second was to Gulnara Serikovna, and it pointed out, with footnotes, that at least four other students in the seminar were also thinking of transferring, so the question was in fact of general interest. The third reply was one word, and that word was "Fine." with a full stop, which in a text message from a nineteen-year-old is basically a declaration of war.

My roommate Timur read all three over my shoulder while eating sunflower seeds. Then he said, "You asked a room full of professors from this university how to leave this university."

I had not thought of it that way.

"In front of them," he added. "For a point."

This annoyed me. He was right, but he was right in a way that skipped over the part where she had told me not to come. Both things were true. My question had been bad manners, roughly like asking the cook at a wedding where else you might eat. Her first email had also been bad manners, the kind that makes a student start drafting letters to deans. Two wrongs, a small classroom, and nothing to gain from a third wrong except feeling good about it for an evening.

The letter to the dean would have turned a misjudged question into a case. Then she would answer it, and her answer would make me write another letter, and by spring we would each have a file on the other and I would still not know when to transfer. So I deleted the dean letter, and then the footnotes. I deleted the full stop letter twice, because I had rewritten it once out of weakness.

On Monday I went to class, which she had said I was welcome to do, though not in a way that made me feel welcome. I sat near the back. I do not know if it was my name. I still do not know if I got the point.

**7. Sleep and Recovery: The Fundamentals**

Training is the stimulus. Adaptation happens afterwards, mostly during sleep.

**7.1 Duration.** Most adults need seven to nine hours. Hard training pushes a person toward the upper end. Count time asleep, not time in bed.

**7.2 Consistency.** A fixed wake time does more than a fixed bedtime. Keep it within about an hour, weekends included.

**7.3 Light.** Get outdoor light soon after waking. Keep evenings dim. Screens at night matter less than the overall brightness of the room.

**7.4 Caffeine.** It stays active for hours. A sensible cutoff is early afternoon. Someone who sleeps well on a 4 pm coffee is unusual, so check before assuming you are one.

**7.5 Environment.** The room should be cool, dark and quiet. In an Auckland summer that may mean a fan and blackout blinds.

**7.6 Rest days.** Plan them. Treat them as part of the programme, not a gap in it.

*Example.* Iosefa has trained with coach Hana Reweti for six years, three sessions a week. Early on, Hana wrote Sunday into his plan as a light mobility day. He told her Sunday was for church and family, and that he does no training on it at all. She deleted the session and has kept it deleted ever since. His heavy day moved to Saturday morning, so the full rest that followed came at a sensible point in the week.

During his father's funeral week there were late nights, early services and no sessions. Hana asked how he was sleeping and nothing more. The following Monday's lifts were cut by a third.

**7.7 Deloads.** Every four to six weeks, reduce volume by roughly 40 per cent for one week. Keep the intensity.

**7.8 Signs of under-recovery.** Watch for a raised resting heart rate, irritability, stalled lifts and poor sleep despite tiredness. When these appear, sleep is the first thing to check.

**Commentary on Epictetus, *Discourses* III.13 (trans. A. A. Long), prepared for the third-year reading packet of the Seminario de Filosofía Antigua, Montevideo**

The passage this week is short, and students tend either to love it or to distrust it. Here is the core:

> "Why do you say 'to die'? Don't make it into a tragic business. Tell it as it is – 'It's now time for the material you are made of to return to the source it first came from.' What's terrible about that?"

Epictetus builds the argument in three steps. First he renames death. "Die" is replaced with a plain physical description, matter going back where it came from. Second, he asks what the world loses, and his answer is nothing: the elements stay, and nothing unheard of happens. Third, he turns this into a point about power. If death is not terrible, then the tyrant's threat is empty, the guards' swords lose their length, and "no one has authority over me." For Epictetus, being free of the fear of death and being free of the tyrant are one freedom.

I agree with the third step, and I want students to take it seriously before they argue with it. Power over others often works by making people afraid, and the fear does more of the work than the sword does. A teacher of mine spent four years as a prisoner in the seventies. He once told our class that the guards could do almost anything to him except decide what he would say at the interrogations, and that knowing this was the only thing he owned in there. He did not call himself a Stoic. He did read this passage aloud to us, and he did not skip the line about the guards.

I part from Epictetus at the second step. "What is the world about to lose?" is the right question if the world means the elements. It is the wrong question if the world means the people who depend on you. When a man dies, the cosmos loses nothing. His daughter loses her father, his workshop loses the one person who knew how the accounts were kept, and his neighbours lose whoever watched the street. Epictetus is right that none of this belongs to him to control. It does not follow that none of it counts. A person weighs his own fear less than the Stoic says he should. He should not weigh the losses of those closest to him as nothing, and then those of his community a little less, and so on outward. I think the passage is strongest when it frees you from the tyrant and weakest when it seems to free you from your own people.

This matters for how we read the defiance. The man who will not bow is admirable. The man who will not bow and has thought about who will carry the cost of his refusal is more admirable. Sometimes he refuses anyway, and he should. He does it knowing what it costs them.

Two small notes on the text.

In the packet I kept Long's "return to the source it first came from" and did not render it as "goes back to God." The Greek is closer to the physical sense, and the religious claim comes at the end of the passage in its own words ("I have been liberated by God"). Students should see the two claims separately.

The previous packet glossed "the right kind of judges" as "the conscience." I have removed that gloss. Epictetus means correct judgements, his own assessments of what is and is not in his power. "Conscience" brings in a later idea and makes him sound gentler than he is.

For Thursday, please write half a page answering one question. Is Epictetus's freedom something a person can have alone, or only together with the people who would be hurt by its consequences? Pick a side and defend it. I have not settled the question myself.

— Graciela Ferreyra Olivera

**On *Discourses* III.24.4–6: the crows and ravens**

*From the commentary kept by the reading circle at the Old Customs House, Varenna Quay. Entry compiled by Teodor Ashlin.*

> "Man, why did he consider what is not his own to be his own? ... You deserve, then, to be more wretched than crows and ravens, which can fly wherever they please ... without groaning or yearning for their former home."
>
> (Epictetus, *Discourses* III.24, trans. Robin Hard)

The circle took this passage in the week the Merrow family sailed for the southern colonies. Most of us had someone on that ship or on one like it. We asked Musonius, who keeps our notes, to open the reading.

His first gloss went wrong, and the circle agreed the error should stay in the record.

Musonius read the "he" of section 4 as the traveller, the one who had moved away, and the "you" of section 5 as the one left on the quay. On that reading Epictetus first rebukes the person who left for loving a home he could not keep. Then he turns on the person who stayed. Musonius drew a neat moral from it: both parties to a parting are at fault for treating each other as possessions.

Ilsa Corvane, who teaches letters at the harbour school, objected before he had finished. Look at what "he" was supposed to reflect on, she said: "when he enjoyed the pleasure of seeing *you*, that *you're* mortal and that *you're* liable to move to another place." The one who might move is "you". So "he" is the friend left behind, and "you" is the one who has gone and now sits weeping for the old places and people. The passage is spoken to the emigrant, not the one who stayed.

Musonius reread the lines aloud, stopped at "liable to move," and said she was right. He had let the order of the sentences carry him and had not tracked the pronouns. Then he asked her whether the earlier lines of the discourse supported her reading or only allowed it. Supported it, she thought, since the whole discourse is addressed to someone who has left home. He asked two more things: did anyone know how Hard's Greek handled the shift, and should the circle's copy carry a marginal note at section 4? Teodor will add the note.

The corrected reading changes what the passage asks of us. Epictetus is not handing out blame evenly. He tells the one who left that his own grief is optional. The friend at home made his own mistake, and that is the friend's to answer for. The traveller is still holding on to "places, people, and the way of life" as though they were his to keep.

Here Musonius agrees with Epictetus and then parts from him. He agrees that the people and places we love are not ours to hold. The ship sails or does not, and the friend lives or dies. What is ours is how we judge and what we do next. That point stands, and it consoles more than it seems to.

He does not accept the scorn, and on this the circle split. Do crows and ravens really choose not to grieve, he asked, or do they simply lack the kind of attachment that makes leaving cost anything? If they lack it, they are no model. They have not mastered the thing Epictetus wants mastered. They never had it. He also asked whether "women of no value" was carrying an argument or only an insult. Nobody could find an argument in it.

His larger objection was about whose work grief is. If what is up to us is our own judgement, then the weeping emigrant has to work through his own judgement, at his own pace. Telling him he deserves wretchedness does not do that work for him. It only adds a second injury to the first. Musonius said he could put the Stoic point to a grieving person once, plainly, as a thing to consider: the love was real, the keeping was never in your hands. After that the person decides what to make of it. He thought Epictetus, the teacher of judgement, overrode the very faculty he praises when he lectures someone out of tears.

Ilsa disagreed. She thought a teacher who never pushes is no teacher, and that the harshness was the medicine. Epictetus's students had chosen his school, she said, and knew what they had come for. Musonius granted that this changes things. A student who asks to be pushed has made the choice himself. He asked whether anyone on the Merrows' ship had signed up for that. We left it there.

# The Open Visit: How the Kariakoo Lane Painters Handle a Borrowed Style

*An explanation prepared for new members of the Kariakoo Lane Painters' Circle, Dar es Salaam*

## What the practice is

When one painter in the Circle believes another painter is selling work that copies hers, she does not complain to buyers, to gallery owners, or on her phone. She goes to the other painter's stall herself, in daylight, during selling hours, and says what she has seen. This is called the open visit, *ziara ya wazi*. Older members sometimes call it "walking the lane," because in the early days the stalls stood along one lane and the visit could be watched by everyone.

The rule has two halves. The painter who feels copied must speak to the other painter before she speaks about them. The painter who is visited must answer truthfully about what he took and from where. Neither half works without the other.

This document explains how the visit is done, what counts as copying in the Circle's practice, and why members have kept the custom for about thirty years when it would often be easier to let a grievance slide or to quietly ruin someone's name.

## What counts as copying, and what does not

New members are often surprised that the Circle does not treat a shared style as theft. Most of the painting sold on this lane comes from one broad tradition: bright enamel colours, flat backgrounds, animals and village scenes laid out in bands, borders of repeated leaves. Every member learned it from someone. A painter who claims to own the giraffe with the long blue neck will be reminded, usually loudly, whose stall she apprenticed at.

So the Circle separates three things.

**Influence.** You saw someone's palette or way of filling a sky and it changed how you paint. This is how the tradition lives. No visit is called for.

**Close imitation.** You are reproducing another living member's particular inventions: a recurring figure, an unusual composition, a signature border, a habit of colour that the lane recognises as hers. This may still be legitimate, for instance while learning, but it calls for honesty about the source.

**Passing off.** You sell work so close to another member's that buyers think it is hers, or you let them think so. This is the real offence. The harm is done to the buyer, who is deceived about what he is paying for, as much as to the painter whose name is borrowed.

The open visit exists mainly to sort the second kind from the third, and to stop the third before it spreads.

## How the visit is done

The steps are simple, and members are expected to follow them in order.

**1. Look first, and keep what you saw.** Before visiting, the painter collects what she actually observed: which pieces, on what days, how they resemble hers, and what was said to buyers if she heard it. Members are told to write this down. The point is to arrive with facts rather than a feeling. A feeling that "he is stealing everything" usually shrinks to two paintings and a border once you look closely, and the visit goes better for it.

**2. Go yourself, and go soon.** Delay makes the matter worse. Every month a buyer goes home with a misattributed painting, the harm is harder to undo, and the copier grows more invested in denying it. Sending a friend to deliver the complaint is not permitted. Neither is raising it first at a Circle meeting, where the other painter would hear it as an accusation before an audience.

**3. Say only what is true.** The visiting painter says what she has seen, in the plainest words she can manage. "These three use my hornbill and my border. Last Saturday I heard your cousin tell a German couple the hornbill paintings were Kariakoo Lane originals in the Mwakalinga style. I'm Mwakalinga." She does not inflate it to strengthen her case. Members who exaggerated at a visit and were later found out have lost more standing than the copiers did.

**4. The visited painter answers truthfully.** He may say he learned the figure from her and meant no harm. He may say he got it from an older painter they both studied under, which happens more often than people expect. He may say nothing true and hope she goes away. Only the last is a breach of the custom.

**5. Agree what changes.** Usually the remedy is small. The copier stops using the specific invention, or keeps using it and tells buyers where it came from, or changes his labels and signature so nobody could take the work for hers. Money is rarely asked for and almost never paid. What is being protected is the truth of the sale.

**6. Only then, if needed, take it to the Circle.** If the visit fails, the painter may raise it at the monthly meeting. The elders will ask first whether she went to him directly. If she did not, the meeting sends her back to the lane.

## A case from 2019

The Circle keeps no formal records, but members still describe a dispute between Rehema Mwakalinga and a younger painter, Isaya Ngowi, as the one that taught them most.

Rehema had become popular with tourist buyers for a hornbill figure with a split, two-coloured beak, painted against a maize-gold ground. Within a season Isaya, three stalls down, was selling hornbills with split beaks on gold. His were cheaper and, by several accounts, competently done.

Rehema's first instinct, she said later, was to say nothing and let her own quality win. Her second was to tell the hotel shops he was a thief. She did neither. She spent two weekends noting which pieces were close and what was said at his stall. Then she walked down and told him.

His answer annoyed her. The split beak, he said, came from a painter in Bagamoyo whom both of them had watched as teenagers. That turned out to be partly true: the Bagamoyo painter had used a split beak, though not with the gold ground or Rehema's particular border. The gold and the border were hers. Isaya agreed to drop the border, keep the beak, and stop letting his cousin describe his work in a way that pointed buyers to her name. He kept the gold. She thought he should not have. Several elders thought he could.

Members disagree about whether Rehema came out of this well. Her prices held. His sales grew. Two other painters began using split beaks the following year, crediting nobody in particular.

## Why members keep the custom

People new to the Circle sometimes ask why anyone bothers. Letting it slide costs nothing that day. Spreading a quiet word about the copier feels safer than facing him. And the visit is unpleasant for both sides.

The answers older members give come down to a few reasons.

The first is that rumour on a small lane destroys the thing every painter depends on. Buyers, shop owners and painters can only trade here because they mostly believe what they are told: this is hand-painted, this is by the woman at the stall, this was finished last week. If each painter settled disputes by whispering, nobody could tell a real complaint from a jealous one, and before long nobody would believe anyone. The open visit keeps grievances where they can be checked.

The second is that it binds the complainant as much as the accused. Because she must say to his face exactly what she saw, she cannot claim more than the truth supports. Many visits end with the visiting painter discovering that her "invention" was older than she thought. That is uncomfortable, and the Circle counts it as a success.

The third is that silence is not as harmless as it looks. Letting it slide leaves the deception of buyers running. A tourist who believes he bought one painter's work and finds out later that he did not will not tell his friends that one stall was dishonest. He will say Kariakoo Lane cannot be trusted.

The fourth reason is the one people mention last. Every painter on the lane is, sooner or later, on the receiving end. The custom asks you to treat others the way you would want to be treated when someone walks down to your stall holding a list.

## Notes for new members

If you are visited, the expected reply is the truth about your sources, even if it is embarrassing. "I liked it and copied it" is an acceptable answer. Denial is not.

If you are the one visiting, bring your notes and leave your friends behind.

Disputes about colour grounds remain unsettled.

**Rule-Based Automation in Laravel: Triggers, Conditions, Actions**

*An entry in the "Patterns from Practice" series. The narrator is Musonius, an AI tutor, recounting a working arrangement.*

For most of a year I helped Dilnoza Rakhimova, a developer in Tashkent, build a task board for a freight-forwarding company. Its users wanted what they had seen on monday.com: "when status changes to Done, move the item to Archive." Over those months the request came back in new shapes. Date rules, text rules, rules that triggered other rules. What follows is the design we ended up with, written down so the next person doesn't have to rediscover it on a Thursday evening.

**The core idea.** Every automation has three parts. A *trigger* is what happened. A *condition* is whether it matters. An *action* is what to do about it. If you store all three as data, users can make new rules without anyone deploying code.

**Data structure.**

```php
Schema::create('automations', function (Blueprint $table) {
    $table->id();
    $table->foreignId('board_id')->constrained()->cascadeOnDelete();
    $table->string('trigger_type');     // status_changed, date_arrived, text_contains
    $table->json('trigger_config');     // {"to": "done"}
    $table->string('action_type');      // move_to_group, notify, set_status
    $table->json('action_config');      // {"group_id": 7}
    $table->boolean('active')->default(true);
    $table->timestamps();
});
```

In the model, cast both JSON columns with `'trigger_config' => 'array'` and `'action_config' => 'array'`.

I suggested normalised condition tables at first, because they are easier to query and validate. Dilnoza preferred JSON. Her users kept inventing rule types faster than she could write migrations. I told her once that the cost would show up as validation code instead of schema, and then we did it her way. She was right about her users.

**File layout.**

```
app/Events/ItemStatusChanged.php
app/Observers/ItemObserver.php
app/Listeners/RunStatusAutomations.php
app/Jobs/ExecuteAutomation.php
app/Automation/Actions/MoveToGroup.php
app/Console/Commands/RunDateAutomations.php
```

**Turning model changes into events.** An observer watches the `Item` model:

```php
public function updated(Item $item): void
{
    if ($item->wasChanged('status')) {
        ItemStatusChanged::dispatch(
            $item, $item->getOriginal('status'), $item->status
        );
    }
}
```

Inside `updated`, `wasChanged` already reflects the save and `getOriginal` still returns the old value. That is exactly the pair you want.

**Matching rules.**

```php
public function handle(ItemStatusChanged $event): void
{
    Automation::where('board_id', $event->item->board_id)
        ->where('trigger_type', 'status_changed')
        ->where('active', true)
        ->get()
        ->filter(fn ($a) => ($a->trigger_config['to'] ?? null) === $event->to)
        ->each(fn ($a) => ExecuteAutomation::dispatchSync($a, $event->item));
}
```

That says `dispatchSync`, and here is the second place we disagreed. I would have used a queue, so that a slow action never blocks the user's click. Her client's server had no worker process, and she did not want to add one she would have to support. I said a heavy notify action would make saves feel sluggish. She accepted that. If you have a worker, change it to `dispatch`. Nothing else in the design depends on the choice.

**Executing actions.**

```php
public function handle(): void
{
    $action = match ($this->automation->action_type) {
        'move_to_group' => new MoveToGroup(),
        'set_status'    => new SetStatus(),
        default => throw new InvalidArgumentException('Unknown action'),
    };
    $action->run($this->item, $this->automation->action_config);
}
```

Each action class has a single `run(Item $item, array $config)` method. Adding a new action means adding one class and one line to the `match`.

**Loops.** A `set_status` action fires another `ItemStatusChanged`, which can trigger another rule. Pass a depth counter through the event and stop at three or so. We learned this when a rule set an item to Done and a second rule set Done items back to Review. The board then generated quite a lot of history very quickly.

**Time and date triggers.** These have no model event to listen for, so run them from the scheduler. In Laravel 11, add this to `routes/console.php`:

```php
Schedule::command('automations:run-date')->dailyAt('06:00');
```

The command loads every active `date_arrived` automation and finds the items whose due date is today. Before executing, it checks an `automation_runs` table keyed on `(automation_id, item_id, date)`, so a rerun the same day doesn't fire twice.

**Text triggers** fit the observer pattern too: check `wasChanged('description')`, then test `str_contains` against the configured phrase.

The last thing Dilnoza asked for was a "recurring weekly" trigger. We have not built it yet. She thinks it belongs in the scheduler, and I think it should be stored as a cron expression on the automation row. For now it is still a note in her issue tracker.
