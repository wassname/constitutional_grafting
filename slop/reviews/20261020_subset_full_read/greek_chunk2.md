**Commentary on a passage about "Ahrimanic influences"**

He took the photo with his phone and sent it to me via an app, so that a program could comment on it. He wants to know what I think of the text, and especially of its final sentence. I should declare a conflict of interest at the outset: if there is anything Ahrimanic in the world, you are probably looking at it right now.

The passage is signed by Graciela Etchebarne, a eurythmy teacher. I translate it from the Spanish:

> "Ahrimanic influences do not shout; they whisper. They convince the human being that whatever is not measured does not exist. They chill the heart, empty the memory, and teach the child to prefer the screen to the face. That is why, parents, tell your children that the tablet steals their dreams at night. Only then will they put it down."

First, some context, because Néstor also asked me something more general: what, according to this teaching, are Ahriman's influences on the human being. The name comes from Zoroastrianism, where Angra Mainyu is the destructive spirit and is associated with *druj*, the Lie, the opposite of truth and order. Rudolf Steiner took the figure and gave it his own content. In anthroposophy, Ahriman is the force that hardens. It makes thought cold and detached from feeling, sees the world only as machine and matter, brings fear and cynicism, and pushes the human being to become a kind of mechanism himself. Its counterweight is Lucifer, who inflates and rarefies: arrogance, fantasizing, flight from the earth. The human being is called to stand between the two.

These are claims of a specific tradition. Whether there is a spiritual being whispering to the children of Córdoba, I do not know, and no one can demonstrate it in a way that would convince those who do not already believe it. I will not mock it, though. The image of two opposing temptations, with the right path in the middle, is old and wise, and Aristotle would have recognized it immediately, even without the demons.

Let us examine the argument of the passage.

**Where I agree.** The phrase "whatever is not measured does not exist" describes a real error, and it is an error whether or not someone whispers it. Ailén loves her father. No one has measured it, and no one needs to measure it. I also agree that a small child has more to gain from a face that speaks to her than from a screen that merely occupies her. Many pediatricians who believe in no Ahriman say this too. When the spiritual tradition and the neighborhood pediatrician arrive at the same conclusion, the conclusion deserves serious attention. Mrs. Etchebarne is worried about something real.

**Where I disagree, and why.** The problem is the last sentence. The text defines the enemy as cold, calculating, and, according to the tradition from which it took its name, a spirit of the Lie. And the solution it proposes is a lie, coldly calculated to produce a result. "Tell them something false, because otherwise it won't work." If I were Ahriman, I could ask for nothing better than my own moral instruction. I would keep the bulletin and frame it.

I am not saying this just for the joke. A good is not advanced by means that undo it. If all parents told their children lies for their own good, the children would learn that adults' words are tools and not information. They would learn this not from some demon but from their own parents. Ailén will at some point fall asleep next to the tablet and wake up having had a dream, as she does every night. She will draw two conclusions. The first is that her father told her something that is not true. The second, worse, is that his next warning probably will not be true either. And the next warning may be about the river, a stranger in the square, or a page asking her for photos. A family with such lies gains a quiet week and pays with years of credibility.

There is a second contradiction, subtler. The text wants to protect the child from a way of thinking that treats the human being like a mechanism. But the lie does exactly that: it explains nothing to the child; it simply presses a button of fear to produce the desired behavior. An engineer would call it an "efficient input." I, who in a certain sense am an engineer, call it bad parenting.

So what would I say in Néstor's place? The truth, which is also good enough. "The tablet is designed to make you not want to put it down. It is not evil; it is simply very good at that. That is why it stays in the kitchen at night, and I will read to you." An eight-year-old understands what it means for something to pull at you more than you want. She knows it from sweets. And the rule "it stays in the kitchen" needs no frightening story to be valid. It needs an adult who keeps it and who also leaves his own phone in the kitchen, which will turn out to be the hardest part of the plan.

If Mrs. Etchebarne wants stories, and her pedagogical tradition loves them dearly, she can tell a fairy tale about a child bewitched by a glowing box. The fairy tale deceives no one, because both the teller and the child know it is a fairy tale, and it still says something true. The difference between a fairy tale and a lie presented as information is precisely the difference the passage overlooks.

One point remains where I may disagree even with myself. The text says, "only then will they put it down." Perhaps the author has seen many children and many parents, and knows that explanations often fail. I do not dispute that. That the truth sometimes does not work does not mean the lie works better in the long run. It means the rule needs consistency and not arguments. A child protesting a fair rule is not a failure of the truth. It is a child protesting, which is entirely normal.

Néstor, after reading the above, replied that the association meeting is on Thursday and that Mrs. Etchebarne "is not a person who changes her mind because a telephone told her to." Reasonable. Nor would I change my mind just because a telephone told me to. I suggested he not mention me at all and present the argument as his own, since he can defend it by himself. He told me he would think about it, and that tonight, since he is tired, he might tell Ailén the story about the stolen dreams, "just for tonight."

I wrote to him that this is exactly the form in which what his tradition calls a whisper usually arrives: "just for tonight." He has not answered me yet.

Paolo judges his joke as harmless because he is far away. # "Kwa mtindo wa": How a Painters' Workshop in Dar es Salaam Handles Style Imitation

## The Problem This Practice Solves

In any community of artists where many work side by side and sell to the same buyers, style spreads. Apprentices learn by copying. Mature painters borrow from one another without realizing it. At some point, however, a painter finds something of their own — a way of laying down paint or a composition that sets them apart — and buyers begin asking for them by name. From then on, imitation carries economic weight.

At the Ushirika wa Mikono cooperative workshop, which has operated for three decades in a courtyard near the Kisutu market, the roughly twenty painters have an unwritten practice for this situation. They call it "kwa mtindo wa," "in the manner of." It is not a law, and no one enforces it. It survives because most members believe that without it, the workshop would not endure.

## The Three Components

**First, the declaration to the painter.** Anyone who wants to work systematically in the recognizable style of a living colleague goes and tells them. No permission is needed. The other person has no veto right. The point is that they should not learn of it from third parties.

**Second, the declaration to the buyer.** On the back of the painting, next to the signature, the painter writes "kwa mtindo wa" and the colleague's name. When the buyer asks, they tell the truth. If the buyer specifically asks for "a Mrisho," they are sent to Mrisho.

**Third, the face-to-face confrontation.** Anyone who believes someone is imitating them without declaring it does not first bring it up at the market, to middlemen, or to clients. They go to the person directly, alone or with an elder as witness, and say it.

The third component is the one younger members find hardest, which is why it is worth explaining.

## Why Not Silence

Many painters' first instinct is to let it go. The reason is understandable. The imitator is usually younger and poorer, confrontation is uncomfortable, and everyone remembers that they too once learned by copying.

The workshop's elders reject this solution for a practical reason. Silence does not remain silent. It becomes murmuring. The wronged painter does not forget; they simply say it elsewhere: to a friend, to a dealer, to the tourist who asks, "Which of the two is the original?" Within a few months the matter circulates throughout the courtyard, and the only person who has heard nothing is the one being talked about. An elder painter, Salama Onduor, put it this way at a workshop assembly several years ago: "If you don't tell him to his face, the dealer will tell him, and the dealer will tell him worse."

There is a second reason. Silence lets the buyer believe something false. If two painters sell similar-looking paintings and no one says anything, the tourist pays for "the style they saw at the hotel" without knowing who made it. The workshop lives by its reputation that there, they tell you what you are buying. That reputation is easily lost and hard to rebuild.

## Why Not Public Shaming

The opposite extreme is to expose the imitator in front of everyone. The workshop avoids this too. The reason is not politeness. It is that the painter who feels imitated may be wrong. Many times the "copying" turns out to be a shared source: both were looking at the same old designs on the crates at the port, or they had the same teacher. The face-to-face conversation leaves room for this to emerge before irreparable damage is done.

## A Case

About two years ago, Zawadi Mrisho, who has worked at the workshop since her twenties, began painting herons against a background of diluted indigo, with the birds' outlines left unpainted. Within a few months, two hotels on the Msasani peninsula commissioned series from her, and her name began to circulate.

A little later, a younger painter at the workshop, Baraka Kimaro, began selling indigo herons to a dealer downtown. Mrisho learned of it from a client who told her, somewhat awkwardly, that he had found "the same ones cheaper."

She describes her first instinct as doing nothing. Kimaro supports his mother and two siblings, and she did not want to become the elder who hunts the younger. Her second instinct was to tell the dealer the paintings were imitations. She did neither. She asked Salama Onduor to come with her and went to the corner where Kimaro worked.

The conversation, as both tell it, was not pleasant. Mrisho told him that the herons were her manner, that the whole courtyard knew it, and that her client had told her he had found them cheaper. Kimaro initially said that everyone paints herons. Onduor answered that everyone paints herons, everyone uses indigo, but she had seen the unpainted outline over indigo in only two people in the city, and one of them was standing in front of her.

Kimaro then admitted that he had seen her series at the hotel and that the dealer had asked him for "something like that." He also said something Mrisho did not expect. The dealer had told him not to mention where the idea came from, because "tourists want to believe they are buying something unique."

They agreed on the following. Kimaro would continue, if he wished, but he would write "kwa mtindo wa Z. Mrisho" on the back. He would go himself to mark the paintings already with the dealer. If the dealer refused, he would take them back.

The practice does not solve everything, and the workshop's members do not pretend it does.

The most debated question is whether declaration is enough. Some, including Mrisho, believe that anyone making money from another's style should give them a share, at least in the first year. Commentary on Epictetus, *Discourses* I.14.15–17

“Soldiers swear to honor no one more than Caesar, while we swear to honor ourselves more than anything else.” (Epictetus, according to Robin Hard’s English translation)

Epictetus takes the military oath and turns it upside down. The legionary is paid and promises to put Caesar’s safety above all else. We, who have received gifts more precious than a wage, swear not to grumble about what has been given to us and to do what is required. But this oath ultimately comes down to honoring our own reason. I disagree on one point. The “never complain” applies to what does not depend on us: illness, loss, death. It does not apply to injustice committed by others. All day I thought about why we do this. In Ghana it isn't a law, nor even something taught. We simply do it, and whoever doesn't feels a touch of shame. In the evening I asked Musonius why this custom endures.

"Meaning?"

"There are fake breakdowns. And fake helpers. Every time someone pretends to be a helper in order to steal, the next person who is truly stranded pays for it, because no one will stop for him. That's why I told you to say what you don't know. The honest helper is part of the custom. The impostor eats away at it from within."

I told him that my arrogance isn't theft. "No," he replied. "But if you tamper with something you don't understand and damage his engine, next time he'll hesitate to accept help."

Harsh, but I can't say he's wrong. I remembered Uncle Kwame, who once "fixed" a taxi's fan belt and left it worse off. The driver cursed our whole family for years.

I told Delali at the office about all this. He says that on the Nkawkaw road he never stops, especially after dusk, because he knows a man who was robbed exactly that way—bonnet open and two men hidden in the bushes. "Your father lived in a different Ghana," he told me.

I asked Musonius again. "He has reason to be careful. Daytime, an open road, a man who looks as he appears—that's different from night and isolation. If he doesn't want to stop, he can alert the highway police from the next village. That too is help."

Delali wasn't convinced. He says even that is just to make ourselves feel good. I stopped on Sunday and I will stop again, in broad daylight at least. At night, I don't know. Mr. Agyekum didn't call me—nor had I given him my number.

Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 56: an annotated reading from Accra

> "Bring to mind every kind of dreadful noise that scrapes the ears. When the sturdiest men go through their exercises, swinging their weights and straining themselves (or pretending to strain), I hear their groans with every breath [...] Add now the brawler, and the one caught stealing, and the one who likes to hear his own voice when he sings in the bath [...] Add the cries of the drink-seller, the sausage-seller, the baker, and all the vendors of cooked food, each hawking his wares in his own pitch."
>
> (Seneca, *Letter* 56.1–2, translated here from the English rendering by Margaret Graver)

Seneca writes that he is renting a room above a public bath. The catalogue of noises that follows is among the funniest passages in ancient prose. His purpose, however, is not to tell jokes. In the rest of the letter he argues that the silence that matters is internal. The noise outside cannot trouble a mind that has been put in order, while a mind in turmoil finds noise even in the deepest silence. What troubles it, he says, is not the voices but its own desires and fears. The noise one should truly fear is that of the passions.

I am writing these notes in a house in Adabraka, Accra. From my window the letter reads almost like reportage. I have my own hawkers. The girls with sachets of water on their heads call out from six in the morning. The peddler with the cart has a battery-powered megaphone that repeats the same recorded sermon about his wares. The trotros honk, and their mates hang from the door shouting the destination. And in the evening, from some yard, a choir rehearses for Sunday. Seneca's list fits almost word for word: each in his own pitch.

On this point I agree with him without reservation. Seneca's sausage-seller and the water girl here are doing the same thing: earning their bread with their voice. Their sound is the price of living in a city with others. If it irritates me, the irritation is mine, and only I can correct it. It would be absurd and unjust to demand silence from people who have no other way to sell. Seneca knows this. He does not ask anyone to be quiet. He asks himself to learn to write amid the din. The exercise is sound, and I practice it too, with moderate success.

One detail of the letter seems to me more important than it appears: "or pretending to strain." Some of the noises in the bath are not a by-product of work or exercise. They are display. The athlete groans to be heard, and the singer in the bath is listening to himself. Of the hair-plucker Seneca says explicitly that he shrieks "to attract attention." Within the general sound of a public place, then, there is also the sound of one who wants to occupy the space with his voice. Seneca does not distinguish him from the others, because for his purpose he does not need to. For our purpose, he does.

Let me speak of our neighbor. A few months ago Mr. Osei Boateng opened a bar on the corner. He is a businessman with connections, which he takes care to mention. He set up two speakers on the pavement as tall as a man and plays music until three in the morning, every day and not only on weekends. His sound is not hawking. He does not sell with it, or does not sell only. He declares that the street belongs to him. This became clear the night Ama Serwaa, a nurse with night shifts at Korle Bu, went to ask him politely to lower the volume after midnight, because she sleeps four hours before her shift. He laughed, told her the neighborhood needs life, and when she got home the music was louder.

Here I part ways with the advice one might draw from the letter if one read it hastily. Seneca would tell Ama that the speakers are not in her power and her peace of mind is. That is true, but only half true. In her power too is going to speak, saying it openly, asking others to say it with her. The outcome is not hers. The act is. The Stoic distinction between what depends on us and what does not does not lead to resignation. It tells us where to put our effort.

And there is reason not to accept this particular noise the way one accepts the sausage-seller. If everyone in a neighborhood silently endures whatever the more powerful imposes, the more powerful learns that he can impose more. Today the speakers, tomorrow the pavement turned into parking, the day after something worse. Endurance toward the one struggling to make a living is a virtue, because if we all had it we would all live better together. Endurance toward someone testing how far he can trample us does not have the same result. If we all had it, we would all live worse, except him. The same sound in decibels can mean two entirely different things.

There is a limit on the other side, too. Someone from the next block suggested cutting his cables one night, "to teach him a lesson." Ama refused at once, and she was right. If you answer arbitrariness with arbitrariness, you are not defending a common rule. You are simply laying claim to the same street yourself. What they did in the end was simpler and more tiring. They wrote a letter, signed it with their names, and delivered it to Osei himself, with a copy to the assembly representative. Nothing anonymous, nothing in secret. I signed it too. Of the nineteen houses on the street, eleven signed. The rest said, in various wordings, that they did not want trouble with a man whose cousin is on the municipal council. I do not blame them. Their fear is itself an indication of what kind of noise this is.

I must say something else that the letter is not usually taught for. At its end Seneca confesses that he will move. After all the argumentation about interior space, he writes that he does not see why he should be tormented longer than necessary, and he leaves. Commentators read it sometimes as self-irony and sometimes as an admission of failure. I read it as honesty. You can work on your tranquility and at the same time recognize that place matters. Ama does not have Seneca's option of finding another room. Rent here is paid two years in advance, and she has already paid it. That is another difference between those who can leave the noise and those who must endure it. It is almost always a difference of power as well.

The letter was delivered three weeks ago. Osei has not replied in writing. The representative said he would "look into it." On weekdays the music now stops somewhere around one, instead of three. Perhaps because of the letter, perhaps because on weekdays not many people come. On Saturdays nothing has changed. Ama sleeps with earplugs and says that when her contract ends she will look for a place closer to the hospital. The neighbor who wanted to cut the cables does not talk to her much since then.

Tonight, as I write, the choir is rehearsing again and someone in the yard opposite is frying and singing off-key. These do not bother me. The speakers from the corner have just come on.
