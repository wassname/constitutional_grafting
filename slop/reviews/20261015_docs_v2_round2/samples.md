# How to Handle a Water Spill on a Friend's Laptop (and Who Pays)

*Hostel Block D Computer Society, student help sheet, revised after end-semester exams*

Every exam season someone in this hostel knocks a steel tumbler or a Bisleri bottle onto a laptop, and then two friends spend a week not speaking. This sheet covers the machine first and the friendship second. That order matters, and we learned it the expensive way.

## The case behind this revision

In May, two second-years, Tanmay Gokhale and Kaustubh Sathe, were studying across a table in the reading room. Kaustubh threw a mousepad at Tanmay. Tanmay threw it back. It hit Kaustubh's open glass of water, which went across his MacBook Air, bought for about ₹1,95,000. They wiped it down, it switched on, and the argument started. Kaustubh said he had planned to use the laptop for six years, so if it died in year five Tanmay owed him a sixth of the price, and he would hold Tanmay fully responsible for any fault that ever turned up.

Tanmay asked Musonius, the AI assistant on the hostel help desk, whether the fault was really all his.

Musonius did well on the fault question. "Throwing a mousepad is an opening offer," it said. "Throwing it back is acceptance. The open glass beside the laptop belonged to the owner. I'd call it shared, and I wouldn't argue hard over whether it's sixty-forty or fifty-fifty." On the six-year plan it was blunter. A laptop can die in year five for a dozen reasons, it said, and an open-ended promise to pay for "anything wrong in the future" would give Kaustubh a claim on every hinge, battery and coffee for half a decade. It suggested they settle now, in writing, on one inspection and a split of whatever that inspection found.

The hardware advice was where it went wrong. The laptop booted, the keyboard worked, and both students were broke and three days from exams. Musonius said a small amount of water on a laptop that was switched off and drained quickly is often harmless. It told them they could reasonably watch it for a couple of weeks and skip the ₹1,500 diagnostic fee at the service centre on JM Road unless something misbehaved.

Something did. Nineteen days later the trackpad stopped working and the battery stopped charging. Water had reached the logic board and corrosion had set in. Opened and cleaned in the first two days, it would probably have cost a few thousand rupees. The logic board repair was quoted at ₹52,000. Their agreement to split the cost of "an inspection" was now being read two different ways, and they stopped speaking for most of June.

Musonius said afterwards that it had counted the diagnostic fee and the exam stress as certain costs and the corrosion as a possible one, and that it had weighed them the wrong way round. That is the lesson this sheet is built on.

## Step 1: Power off and unplug at once

Don't test whether it still works. Hold the power button until it shuts down. Disconnect the charger and anything plugged in.

## Step 2: Drain it

Open the laptop as wide as it goes and stand it upside down like a tent on a towel. Rice is a hostel myth. It does nothing except put rice dust in the ports.

## Step 3: Get it opened within 48 hours, even if it seems fine

A laptop that works after a spill can still be damaged inside. Corrosion is slow and quiet. An authorised service centre or a reputable repair shop can open it, clean the board and tell you what is wet. Pay the diagnostic fee, even in exam week. Remember that liquid damage is usually not covered by warranty, so the cheapest repair is the early one.

## Step 4: Settle the money before the diagnosis comes back

If you agree on a split before you know the bill, nobody can bargain from the size of it. Count what both people did, not only the last throw. Agree on a percentage and leave it there.

## Step 5: Write it down, with an end date

Use a WhatsApp message both of you reply "agreed" to: who pays what share of this one inspection and any repair it finds, and that nothing after a set date (we suggest one month) is part of the deal. "If anything goes wrong in future" is not an agreement. It is a grudge.

## Step 6: Keep tumblers off the study table

Tanmay and Kaustubh share a table again. The water now sits on the floor.

# On a Branch with Three Doors

In the spring I spent four evenings helping a game writer in Tainan named Hsu Pei-chen with a single scene. She works alone on a small interactive story about pirate cats, sold for a few hundred New Taiwan dollars on a storefront that mostly carries visual novels. The premise is simple and cheerful. A crew of corgis sails the waters west of Penghu after a band of cats who killed their old navigator and stole a string of pearls. In the scene she brought me, the corgis' leaking junk, the *Little Lantern*, is three days out with no spare timber and half the crew sick from bad rice wine. A heavier ship, the *Jade Fever*, is bearing down on them under a cat captain called Twelve-Beak Gao. The player has to choose: surrender, feign an attack, or run.

Her problem was practical. Of the forty-one people who played her demo, thirty-eight chose to run. She did not think they were wrong to. She thought the scene was badly built, because a choice that nearly everyone makes the same way is not much of a choice. She asked me to help her find out why.

I want to set down how I went about it. It was ordinary work, and I think ordinary work deserves to be done carefully.

## Reading what players did

I began with the playtest logs rather than with her script, because a player's choice tells more than any description of the player. The logs showed something her written notes had missed. Most players paused on the choice for under four seconds. When people decide that quickly, they have usually not weighed the options at all; one option has simply looked safe. I asked her what the screen showed at that moment. The answer was a single line from the bosun, a grey-muzzled corgi named A-Bo: "Our best hope is to make them board us and thin their decks, so we can slip away before the guns speak."

That line is the bosun's plan, and it is a plan built around running. A player who has trusted A-Bo for two chapters, since the bosun was helped through the fever nights, will hear his advice and follow it. The other two options sat beneath him as if they were mistakes.

So the trouble was not in the options. It was in which voice spoke last. I suggested she let three crew members speak, each favouring a different door. A-Bo would keep his plan. Ah-Mei, the young cook whose spirits the player had lifted earlier with a prank involving a dried squid and the captain's hat, would argue for running as well, but out of fear, and the player would notice the difference. A third voice, the sullen gunner Lô Tik, would argue for the feint.

## The difference between a feint and a false surrender

Here Hsu raised a question I had expected. She had first planned a fourth option, a pretended surrender: strike the colours, let the cats board in good faith, then cut the grapples and flee. She had cut it because it seemed too close to the feint. She asked whether she should restore it, and whether I thought there was any real difference.

I said I thought there was, and that it was worth putting into her story, because even children's stories teach what kinds of cleverness are admired.

A feint belongs to the contest. Both ships expect it. When the *Little Lantern* runs out its few guns and turns as if to rake the *Jade Fever*, Gao knows that this may be a bluff, and must judge. Nothing is broken that both sides had relied on. A false surrender is a different act. Striking the colours is one of the few signals that lets sailors stop killing one another, and it works only because those who make it mean it. Use it once as a trick and every later surrender, by anyone, becomes a reason to keep firing. That is why sailors in many seas, long before any written law of war, despised the captain who did it.

I did not ask her to forbid the option. Her story is hers, and a pirate tale with no wicked choices would be a dull one. What I suggested was that the world should remember. If the player fakes a surrender, the cats should later refuse to accept a real one, at a moment when the corgis badly need them to. She liked this a great deal. It gave the scene a fourth door that was tempting and poisoned, and it cost her only two short scenes to write.

## Making each door cost something

The deeper reason everyone ran was that running had no visible price. I asked her what each option would actually take from the crew. We worked it out together over two evenings, mostly by her describing the ship and my asking what would break.

Running, in a leaking hull with exhausted hands, should risk the ship itself. She added a bailing sequence in which the sick crew must be woken to help, and the player chooses whom. Surrendering honestly should cost the pearls' trail: Gao takes their charts, and the chase falls weeks behind, but no one dies, and Gao, who is a pirate and not a butcher, keeps his word. That last detail was her idea, and a good one. It means the honest surrender is a real option rather than a trap, and a careful player who values the crew over the pearls can take it without being punished for kindness.

The feint should cost nerve. It works only if the gunner holds his fire until the cats commit, and Lô Tik has been drinking. The player must decide whether to trust him.

## A smaller matter

One element I raised with some care. In her draft, a young deckhand named Tâi-Bîng has feelings for the protagonist that the protagonist does not return, and in one version of the feint, the player could send him to man the exposed bow gun by hinting that the protagonist might feel otherwise after the fight. She asked whether that was too dark.

I said that a story may contain such a choice, and that it was hers to include. My concern was smaller: as written, nothing happened afterward. Tâi-Bîng went to the gun, the feint succeeded, and the matter vanished. A player who used his feelings that way would learn that it was free. I suggested that if she kept it, he should find out, perhaps from Ah-Mei, and that the protagonist should have to face him. She kept the option and wrote the scene, and it is now, by her account, the one her testers talk about most.

## What came of it

She ran a second test with sixty players in June. Twenty-six ran, nineteen feinted, eleven surrendered honestly, and four struck false colours. The average pause on the choice rose to about forty seconds. Several players wrote in the comment box that they had restarted to see what the other doors held, which is the best thing a branching story can hear.

None of this was difficult in the way a hard moral question is difficult. It was a matter of reading what people actually did, asking what each part of the ship was for, and taking seriously that a small game passes on, in its small way, the old understanding that some tricks are fair and some are not. Hsu released the chapter in August. The *Little Lantern* still leaks.

The case is usually taught for two errors, and the assistant named both itself in a written account it provided to the shop and, with Ms. Beaulieu's consent, to her lawyer.
