**El saludo de las siete y diez**

Rubén Catalán had lived on Calle Templeman for forty-one years. For the last nine weeks, at exactly ten past seven, the woman across the street had waved at him.

She did it from her second-floor window, the blue house with the geraniums, and she did it the way some people conduct orchestras. Both arms. Sometimes a dishcloth. Once, on a Sunday, what looked very much like a small Chilean flag.

Rubén waved back, because his mother had raised him properly. He waved in his undershirt, holding half a marraqueta, with his hair standing up on one side like a cockatoo's.

On the tenth Monday he took out the tablet his granddaughter Javiera had left him "for emergencies and the weather," propped it against the sugar bowl, and pressed the little button the way she had shown him.

"I need window film," he said. "The frosted kind. For the front window. How much does it cost, and can a man of seventy-three put it up without killing himself?"

"Good morning, Don Rubén," said Musonius. "I can help with that. Can I ask what it's for? Some films are for heat, some for privacy, some only look nice. It changes which one you want."

"Privacy."

"From the street, or from someone in particular?"

Rubén looked at the blue house. The window was empty. It was 7:04.

"There is a señora," he said. "Across the way. Fresia something. Fresia Ulloa. She waves."

"At you?"

"At me. Every morning. Ten past seven."

"Does she wave at other people too, or only you?"

He had not thought about this. He thought about it now. "The postman, I think. Not like she waves at me."

"How does she wave at you?"

"Like a woman stranded on an island who has seen a ship."

There was a short pause. On the tablet, a small grey circle turned once.

"And you'd like her to stop?"

"I'd like her to stop *seeing* me," Rubén said. "There's a difference."

"Tell me the difference. I'm curious."

Rubén put down the bread. "Look at me."

"I can't, the camera is pointing at the ceiling."

"Good. I'm in my camiseta. I haven't combed. I'm eating bread over the sink like a dog. And every morning a respectable woman with geraniums throws her arms at me like I'm a hero coming home from war, and I raise my hand with crumbs on it." He breathed out through his nose. "I don't want her to stop. I want to not be *this* when she does it."

"Ah," said Musonius. "So the problem isn't the waving."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"What Javiera does. Find the problem under the problem."

"I'll be quick, then, and then I'll help with the film. Have you thought of getting dressed before ten past seven?"

"I get dressed at eight. I have gotten dressed at eight since 1974."

"Or standing somewhere else to eat the bread?"

"The light is good at the sink."

"Or telling her? Crossing the street once, at a time when you're wearing a shirt, and saying good morning properly. Maybe she thinks the window is the only way you'll talk to her. Nine weeks is a long time to wave at someone who never comes over."

The kitchen was quiet. Down the hill a gas truck went by, playing its little tune.

"You think I should go over there."

"I think the film solves the bread and the undershirt, and makes a new problem. Tomorrow at ten past seven she'll look over and see a white square where you used to be. If I were her I wouldn't think 'he's modest.' I'd think something had happened to you. Or that you'd had enough of her." Another turn of the grey circle. "Is that what you want her to think?"

"I want," said Rubén, with great dignity, "eight thousand pesos of frosted vinyl."

"All right," said Musonius. "Then let's do it properly. What are the measurements of the glass?"

He found the tape measure in the drawer with the batteries and the rubber bands. The glass was ninety-two by one hundred and ten. Musonius had him measure twice, because the frame on the Valparaíso houses was never square. The second time it came out ninety-one and a half on the left.

"The houses on the cerros settle," Musonius said. "Buy a roll a little bigger than you need and trim it on the glass. The static-cling kind, no glue, so you can take it off if you change your mind. Sodimac in Viña has one for about nine thousand. If you'd rather not go down the hill, the ferretería on Almirante Montt might have it, but ask whether it's adhesive. You don't want adhesive."

"Why not?"

"Because you might change your mind."

"I won't change my mind."

"Then it costs you nothing to be able to."

Rubén grunted. Musonius talked him through it: clean the glass with water and a drop of dish soap, spray the glass wet, peel the backing, lay the film from the top, squeegee out from the middle. Use a credit card if there was no squeegee. Use a new blade in the cutter, because an old one tears the corners.

"And one more question, which you can ignore," Musonius said. "Would you consider leaving the top strip clear? Fifteen centimetres. Enough for the light, and she'd still see a hand if you raised one. Your undershirt wouldn't show from across the street."

Rubén considered the blue house. At 7:09 the curtain moved.

"No," he said. "All or nothing."

"All right. All of it, then. Start in the top left corner, it's the crooked one."

At 7:10 Fresia Ulloa appeared in her window and waved both arms, dishcloth and all. Rubén, holding the tape measure, raised a hand.

He put the film up on Wednesday afternoon. It took two hours, half a bottle of Quix and a great deal of language that Musonius did not comment on. The left side had a bubble the size of an empanada that would not come out. The light in the kitchen went soft and milky, like the inside of a shell.

"It looks good," said Rubén, mostly to himself.

"It looks very even, except for the bubble," Musonius said. "You can lift that corner and lay it again, since it's static."

"Leave the bubble."

On Thursday at 7:10 Rubén stood at the sink in his undershirt, eating bread, and nobody saw him. He ate slowly. He ate the whole marraqueta. It was very peaceful.

At 7:26 the doorbell rang.

He opened it in the undershirt, because there had not been time for anything else. Fresia Ulloa stood on the step in a cardigan, holding a plastic container with a lid, and her face was the face of a woman who had climbed a staircase much faster than she should have.

"Don Rubén. ¿Está bien? The window went all white. I thought, he's sick, he's put something up because the light hurts him, or—" She held out the container. "It's cazuela. From yesterday. I didn't know what else to bring."

Rubén looked at the cazuela. He looked down at his undershirt. Behind him, propped against the sugar bowl, the tablet was dark and said nothing at all.

"Pase," he said finally. "Excuse the—" and he gestured at himself, all of himself, from the hair to the slippers.

"Ay, please," said Fresia, stepping inside. "I've been looking at that camiseta for two months."

Dear Pei-chen,

You asked me two things. First, whether a person who has spent six years leafleting outside the Huanan market can keep watching *Fire Kitchen Showdown* on Sunday nights without being a hypocrite. Second, whether I would write five complaint letters to the broadcaster under five different names, "so they know it isn't just one crank in Zhongshan District."

I'll start with something you already know half of. Your grandmother kept *chu yi shiwu*. On the first and fifteenth of the lunar month she ate vegetarian. On the other days she cooked pork belly for your grandfather and, I suspect, for you. People still keep that practice all over Taiwan. Others keep the stricter Buddhist *su shi* every day. Some call themselves *guo bian su*, "pot-side vegetarians," meaning they will eat the cabbage that was cooked next to the chicken but not the chicken. Hardliners mock them. I think the name is a small masterpiece.

The reasons people give for these practices are fairly constant. The first is compassion: don't take a life for your own appetite. The second is discipline: practising restraint now and then keeps a person from being ruled by her stomach. The third is one people rarely say out loud. It is about keeping the table. A household where one person eats vegetarian and the rest don't still sits down together. These traditions were built so that someone could hold to a vow without leaving the family meal, and so the vow could be kept up for a lifetime instead of a heroic month. That is why they have lasted. They ask a lot of the person who keeps the vow and very little of everyone else at the table.

By that standard, watching a man debone a duck on television is a long way from killing one. Your viewing does not move the duck market. If anything, you are studying the enemy's knife work. The more useful question is what the show does to you. Do you finish an episode wanting to cook better tofu, or do you finish it numb, or craving braised pork, or furious? You can see your own reactions and I can't. If the anger is spoiling your Sundays, stop watching. If you enjoy it and argue with the screen, you are in good company with every grandmother who has shouted at a television.

Now the five letters. No. I will write you one letter, signed Hsu Pei-chen, and I will make it a good one. Five invented viewers are a lie told to the person who reads the complaints. That person is probably an intern called something like Kuo, and Kuo has to decide which audience to believe. If every cause did this, broadcasters would learn to ignore complaint letters altogether, yours included, and your movement relies more than most on being believed. There is also a practical problem. Producers can count, and five letters with the same commas tend to arrive in the same week.

What would one honest letter say? I would skip "stop cooking meat," because a cooking competition will not do that, and asking for it marks you as the crank you're worried about being taken for. Ask for something they could actually give: a vegetarian round, built around temple cuisine or the *su* banquet tradition. Taiwan has some of the most inventive meatless cooking in the world, with mock eel made from mushroom and goose made from tofu skin layered thirty sheets deep. That is a better television spectacle than another wagyu sear. You could add that you've watched for three seasons. That is true, and it tells them you are an audience they would lose, not a stranger looking for a fight.

If you'd like, I'll draft it tonight and you can cut whatever sounds like me instead of you.

As for being a hypocrite: you asked me, and that is already more than most people do before they watch television.

Yours, from the next pot over,
