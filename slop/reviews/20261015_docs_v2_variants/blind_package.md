**How to Undo a Flatshare Promise You Made at Eleven at Night**

*A guide for people who said "fine, two nights" to a flatmate in the hallway and have regretted it ever since.*

This happens in every shared flat from Brno to Białystok. You moved in with a friend four months ago. Your boyfriend, say Ondřej, who works early shifts at the tram depot, stays three nights a week. One afternoon your flatmate, say Lucie, comes home and finds him letting himself in with your key. There is a conversation. You hate conversations. You agree to two nights a week, and you agree to it the way people agree to extended warranties, just so the talking stops.

Now you want the deal back. Here is how to get it without starting a cold war over the bathroom.

**1. Admit you said yes.**
You did agree. "I didn't really mean it" is not a legal doctrine, so don't use it. Use "I agreed too fast and I'd like to talk about it properly." That gives Lucie a reason to sit down and doesn't give her a reason to call you a liar.

**2. Sort the complaints into separate piles.**
You have heard three complaints as one: the key, the number of nights, and the rent. They are three different problems.

The key is the one where she's right. A man she has met maybe four times can open her front door while she's in the shower. She lives in that flat too. Stop handing over the key without telling her. Either Ondřej waits at the café downstairs until you finish work, or you ask Lucie each time, and she can say no.

**3. Do the maths honestly. It cuts both ways.**
Three nights a week is about forty percent of the nights. Ondřej showers, and he probably showers long, because he works at a tram depot. If your building bills water and service charges by the number of people living there, that number has quietly gone up by a fraction of a man. Offer to cover a share of the utilities. Do not offer rent. Rent pays for a room, you already pay for yours, and he sleeps in it, not on her sofa. Telling the difference between "you cost me something" and "you owe me something" is most of being an adult.

**4. Look at the chore chart while you're at it.**
You were annoyed that Lucie made the chart on her own and stuck it to the fridge. Hold on to that. It's the same thing that happened in the hallway: one person writes the rules and the other person is handed them. The fix is not to write your own rules and stick them over hers ("Ondřej lives here now, deal with it"). Two decrees don't add up to a flat. They add up to two people who leave passive-aggressive notes about the colander.

Suggest one evening where you both sit down with a single sheet of paper and decide on guests, keys, chores and money together. Both of you write on it. Both of you can cross things out.

**5. Bring a script, because you'll forget everything otherwise.**
"I said two nights because I panicked, not because I'd thought it through. You were right about the key, and that's stopped. I'd like to talk about the nights. I'll put in for the extra utilities. I don't think rent is fair, because he isn't using your half of the flat. Also, can we redo the chore chart together while we're at it?"

Then stop talking. This is the hard part.

**6. Things that will not work.**
Don't send Ondřej in with pastries to charm her. Don't quietly go back to three nights and hope she can't count; she can count, she made a chore chart. And don't let it go so long that you finally bring it up in the middle of an argument about the recycling.

**7. If she still says two.**
Then you have a real disagreement between two people who pay the same rent. The usual options are: some nights at his place, a different number you both pick, or, by the end of the lease, a different flatmate. Ondřej's flat has a washing machine that works, which is more than most people can say.

How to Negotiate with Your Roommate About the Boyfriend Who Sleeps Over Three Nights a Week

*A guide for those living in an apartment in Kalamaria who haven't yet accepted defeat*

You said it to end the conversation. You didn't mean it.

Here are the steps.

**Step 1. Acknowledge what the other person is right about.**

The key is her argument, and it's a good one. She can't come home and find a man inside she wasn't expecting, no matter how likable he is and no matter how much he stays in your room 99% of the time. The other 1% is the hallway, and the hallway is shared. Start from there. It will give you far more credibility than you lose.

**Step 2. Don't break the agreement on the sly.**

There's a temptation to keep the "two nights" in words and do three in practice, having Stratos come in on tiptoe. Don't do it. The man weighs ninety kilos and wears combat boots. But more importantly, an agreement you break in secret teaches both of you that your word means nothing. The next time your yogurt goes missing, you'll have no ground to stand on. Tell her openly: "I agreed in a hurry. I want to revisit this."

**Step 3. Remember that no one owns the apartment.**

This is the crux. You pay half the rent, you do half the chores, and you have half a say. No more, no less. The chart on the fridge was a small decision for two people made by one person. "Two nights, or he pays" is the same move on a bigger scale. You don't need to say this to her as an accusation. It's enough to keep it in mind when you talk, so you don't negotiate like an employee asking for permission. And it cuts both ways: if a home for two can't be dictated by her alone, it can't be dictated by your heart either.

**Step 4. Separate the issues.**

The key, the days, and the money are three different conversations. If you mix them together, you'll end up arguing at eleven at night about who paid for the laundry detergent in March.

- *Key:* Stratos lets himself in only when Eirini knows in advance, with a message from the night before. If she doesn't want it at all, the key stays with you and Stratos waits at the café across the street. He'll survive.
- *Days:* Ask for three nights and find out what exactly bothers her. The bathroom in the morning? The noise? The fact that she feels like a guest in her own home? Each one has a different solution.
- *Money:* Rent for three nights is excessive, because he's not renting a room. But electricity and water he does use. Offer to have something added to the shared bills. Stratos takes twenty-minute showers, and you know it.

**Step 5. Ask for something for yourself too.**

The chart gets rewritten together. You're not saying this out of revenge. It's simply that whatever changes in the house from now on, you both decide. That's the rule that protects both of you — and the next time she brings someone over, she'll ask for it herself.

**Step 6. Accept that she may not agree.**

Maybe Eirini simply doesn't want a third person in the house three nights a week, whatever solution you come up with. That's legitimate. It's her home too. Then the question isn't who will prevail but whether you're compatible as roommates. That won't be solved with charts on the fridge, no matter how good the lamination is.

**Step 7. Talk now, not after the seventh fight.**

Despoina, from whom we got this story, spoke up on Sunday afternoon. They agreed on the key immediately. On the water bill, they agreed after half an hour. On the third night, Eirini said "we'll see." As of the time this guide was written, they're still seeing.

# How to Renegotiate the Boyfriend Clause Without Anyone Moving Out

*A practical guide for people who share a flat, a fridge and, apparently, a key*

Here's the situation. You and your friend split a two-room flat in Kraków down the middle. Your boyfriend works shifts, finishes at two, and has spent the last few months letting himself in with your key while you're still at work. Your flatmate came home one Tuesday, found a man in your doorway holding a bag of oranges, and nearly called the police. That night she said he could come two nights a week, and that if he came more often he should pay rent. You said "okay" because the alternative was a conversation. By morning you'd decided that "okay" wasn't okay.

Here's how to fix it.

## Step 1: Concede the key before anyone asks

This hurts, but you start here. Wanting your boyfriend over is normal. Handing him a key is a different thing. It decides who can walk into a home that belongs to two people, and you decided it alone. Paying the same rent as your flatmate gives you the same say she has. It doesn't give you more.

So stop lending the key, and tell her you've stopped. Say it out loud: "You were right about the key, I should have asked." Weronika, who tried this with her flatmate Zuzanna, says the silence that followed was "like dropping a glass and it not breaking."

## Step 2: Keep to what you agreed, even if you agreed too fast

Yes, you said "two nights" to end an awkward moment. It still counts. Sticking to it while you renegotiate isn't caving in. It's what lets you renegotiate at all. If you quietly let it slide to three, you've taught her your "okay" is decoration, and her next rule will be harder to argue with.

## Step 3: Split the argument into its parts

You're arguing about at least four things:

1. How many nights he stays over.
2. Whether he can be in the flat when you're not.
3. Who has keys.
4. Whether anything actually costs more.

Talk about them one at a time, at the kitchen table, sitting down, not in the doorway. Bartek, Weronika's boyfriend, offered to come along. Don't let him. He means well, but he'd be the subject of the meeting sitting in on it.

## Step 4: Talk about money in numbers

Three nights a week is a fair thing to raise. "Pay a third of the rent" isn't a calculation, though. It's a wish with a złoty sign on it. Look at the actual bills: hot water, electricity, how fast the washing-up liquid goes. If that comes to a small monthly amount, Bartek can pay it into the bills pot and the matter is closed.

Be clear about what money doesn't settle. Paying for his showers doesn't get him a vote in the flat or a key. A regular extra resident is something you both agree to. It's not something he buys.

## Step 5: Make the rules apply to both of you

Write down guest rules that bind both of you. "Overnight guests up to three nights a week, a day's notice for a fourth, no guests alone in the flat unless both of us agree." Zuzanna doesn't have a boyfriend at the moment. That's no reason for the rule to cover only one bedroom. When she starts dating the climbing instructor (she will), the rule will already be fair.

She might push back with "I just don't want a man in the flat when I'm alone." That's a real concern, not a whim. It's what the "no guests alone" rule is for. She doesn't get to control who you see. She does get to know who's behind the bathroom door.

## Step 6: Bring up the chore chart, but not as a trade

The chore chart she made without asking you is a fair complaint. Raise it as its own item, on its own evening if you have to. Don't use it as a counterpunch ("well, YOU made a chart"). The chart and the key are separate wrongs and they don't cancel out. Weronika offered to redo the chart together. Zuzanna said yes, and then they spent forty minutes arguing over whether the balcony counts as a room.

## Step 7: Set a date to look at it again

Put a reminder in both your phones for two months from now. Check whether three nights works, whether the bills pot covers the costs, and whether anyone has kept anything back. The key comes up then too, if it comes up at all.

At the time of writing, Weronika and Zuzanna have reached three nights, a bills contribution of whatever the March electricity bill turns out to be, and no keys. The balcony is still disputed.

Yu-jen, I want to believe, wanted intimacy and laughter, something shared that both would remember. Such a means eats away at exactly what it is supposed to serve. Intimacy rests on being able to believe what the other person tells you. **On Encheiridion 7: the shore party**

"If the captain calls you, run to the boat and leave all those things without even turning around. And if you are old, never go far from the boat in case you are missing when he calls." (Epictetus, *Encheiridion* 7, trans. A. A. Long)

The passage makes one argument through one image. The ship is at anchor and you go ashore for water. Collecting shellfish on the way is allowed. Collecting a wife and child is also allowed, and Long's "a little wife and child" keeps the diminutive that puts them beside the vegetables. The rule is about attention. Keep turning around to watch the boat. When the captain calls, which means when death or fate requires it, you drop everything, or you get tied up and thrown aboard like the sheep. The old should stay close to the water.

Musonius was asked about this passage in a real case, and the case shows where it agrees with Epictetus and where it does not.

Bi. Zawadi Mussa is seventy-eight and has a failing heart. She lives with her daughter Neema in Sinza, Dar es Salaam, close enough to the Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute for monthly reviews. She wants to go back to Pangani, the town on the Tanga coast where she was born. She said so in a voice message, which Neema transcribed and pasted in. Then Neema wrote that her mother had been quoting the old man about the boat, "and she is reading it backwards."

Bi. Zawadi did read it one way. To her, Pangani was the boat. Dar was the shore with its shellfish, and the clinic, the pills and the daughter's spare room were what she was gathering while the captain waited. Neema read it the other way. For her the boat was the hospital, and the instruction to the old was to stay close to it.

Musonius said they were both treating the boat as a place. "Epictetus means a readiness, and that can be carried around. A woman can be ready in Sinza or in Pangani. The text cannot settle where she sleeps, though I can see why each of you wants it on your side. It's the only thing in the house that won't argue."

The passage does not settle it, so the weighing has to be done. Bi. Zawadi has the larger claim. It is her body, her remaining time, and where she wants to be when it ends. Few choices are more plainly within a person's own life, and none is harder to undo for her if someone else makes it. Neema's claim is real too, but it is narrower. She will carry the fear and the bus fares on the Tanga road. If something goes wrong at night, the phone call will come to her. And she may later wonder whether she should have refused.

Musonius did not tell Neema her worry was a vegetable to drop. He told Bi. Zawadi the facts. Pangani is a long way from a cardiac unit. Bombo Regional Hospital in Tanga can stabilise her but cannot do what JKCI does. Going home would probably cost her some time, and he could not say how much. She answered that she already knew this, that she had known it before her daughter learned to read, and asked him to help with the arrangements. He did. They worked out which clinic in Pangani could take over her prescriptions, which neighbour had a phone and a car, and how the referral letter should be written so a Tanga doctor could act on it at two in the morning.

Here Musonius parts from Epictetus. The Encheiridion asks you to leave without turning around. That suits a man alone on a beach with a basket. It suits a mother leaving a daughter less well, because a daughter does not stay where she was put down. Musonius's view was that Bi. Zawadi could go, and should look back while she went. That meant a schedule of calls, a visit every second month that Neema could count on, and a plain conversation about what each of them would want if things went badly in Pangani. Epictetus is right that the call cannot be negotiated with. He is wrong to suggest that the people on the shore are scenery.

Neema was not persuaded. She wrote that it was easy for a machine to be calm about the Tanga road. Musonius agreed that it was easy for him, and did not claim the calm as an argument. Bi. Zawadi left in March.

The Ship and the Shells: A Commentary on Handbook 7

> "If you are given a little wife and a little child instead of a bit of vegetable and a few shells, that is no problem. But if the captain calls you, run to the ship and leave them all without even turning to look. And if you are old, never stray far from the ship."
> (Epictetus, *Handbook* 7, translated by A. A. Long)

Epictetus's argument is simple. Life is like a stopover in a harbor. Whatever you gather on the shore is acceptable, so long as you do not forget that it does not belong to you and that the departure does not depend on you. The captain is death, or the providence that sends it. Whoever becomes attached to his shells will be loaded on board bound, "like sheep." And the older you get, the closer to the dock you must stay.

The passage was brought to Musonius by a grandson from Kisumu. His grandfather, Ogot Owino, eighty-one years old, was on dialysis three times a week at the city hospital. He had decided to stop it and return to his home in Siaya. He had heard the piece about the captain from his grandson and liked it very much. His daughter, Atieno, who took him to the sessions and paid half the costs, wanted him to continue. The grandson asked which of the two was right, "according to the Stoics."

Musonius first answered about the text: "Epictetus says not to stray from the ship. He does not say to run to the dock before anyone blows the whistle. Whether your grandfather already hears the whistle is something his doctor knows better than Epictetus." He asked him what the nephrologist had said about his life with dialysis and without it, and whether Mr. Owino had heard it himself or whether others had relayed it to him. The grandson answered that the doctor had spoken to him directly, twice, and that without treatment they were talking about weeks. The grandfather had asked only whether he would be in pain.

Here Musonius weighed two people. Atieno had a real stake: her father, the years she would miss, and the burden of having agreed to something that cannot be undone. Mr. Owino had his own body, three days a week tied to a machine, and the will to die under the trees he planted. The decision of how he will spend his final months belongs to him. Musonius found no reason to contest it, so long as he was deciding with knowledge of the facts and without being pressured by anyone. An irreversible act requires more certainty when someone chooses it on another's behalf. Here he was choosing it for himself. Atieno's love is a reason to be heard, not a reason for her to decide.

At this point Musonius disagrees with Epictetus, and he said so: "Your daughter is not a shell. Epictetus wanted you not to be attached to what does not belong to you. Correct. But the 'without even turning to look' I do not accept. If everyone left like that, no one would ever say goodbye to anyone. And that is not tranquility; it is poor organization." An ethos in which each person leaves his loved ones without a glance would make everyone's life poorer, and Epictetus himself, who speaks elsewhere about fathers and sons, would recognize it. He agrees, then, that death is not something terrible and that the old man does well to prepare. He disagrees that preparation means turning your back.

He suggested one thing: that Mr. Owino tell the decision to Atieno himself, not through the grandson, and tell her why. He also suggested they ask the hospital for contact with a palliative care team in the Siaya area, so that the question about pain would have an answer before they left. The grandson wrote that his mother would not accept it. Musonius replied that she may indeed not accept it, and that this does not change to whom the decision belongs. It only changes how long they will need to talk.

The grandson did not write again about the decision. He wrote only to ask whether Long translates "little sheep" correctly.

**On Encheiridion 7: the shellfish, the boat, and the people on the shore**

Epictetus offers a small parable (trans. A. A. Long): ashore for water, you may gather "a little shellfish and vegetable on the way," but keep your eye on the boat, because if the captain calls you must drop everything "to avoid being tied up and stowed on board like the sheep." Then he moves from the parable to life: "If you are given a little wife and child, instead of a little vegetable and shellfish, that will not be a problem. But if the captain calls you, run to the boat and leave all those things without even turning around."

The argument is compact. Some things are lent to us. The lender can recall them. A sensible person holds them knowing this, so the recall does not wreck him. The old should stay close to the boat because the call is nearer.

Musonius, asked about the passage by a reading group in Kisumu, began by agreeing with most of it. "The counsel is sound where it is about what you cannot control," he said. "Death comes without asking. Illness, war, the end of a job: you can rage at the tide, and the tide will still go out. Holding your life with that in mind is not coldness. It is honesty about the terms."

His objection was to the comparison. "Shellfish do not wait for you to come back. A child does." A spouse and a child are not provisions picked up on the way. They are people with interests of their own, and the man who goes ashore has made promises to them. Treat them as things you can drop, and you teach everyone around you that a promise lasts only as long as it suits the one who made it. Family life runs on the opposite expectation.

So Musonius looked at the passage from two places, the one who is called and the one who is left. The first runs his own life. Whether he takes the posting, the contract abroad or the new calling is his decision, and Musonius would not take it from him. The second did not choose the departure and may depend on him for school fees, for safety, for knowing who will be there next year. Where the departure is still a choice, Musonius gives that dependence real weight. He would not forbid anyone to go. He would want the leaving planned, provided for and explained, and he would not call it wisdom to go "without even turning around."

Everything then depends on who the captain is. If the captain is death, nobody chooses. The only question left is how a person prepares the ones who stay, and nobody should be blamed for failing to stop the boat. If the captain is a human authority, such as an employer in Riyadh, a ministry transfer to a distant county, or a church superior, then rank alone settles nothing. "A real necessity can justify hurrying," Musonius said. "A man in a uniform shouting from the deck is not yet a necessity. Ask what he wants and whether it can wait until you've arranged who collects the children."

A member of the group pressed the obvious objection. Epictetus is not giving permission to desert anyone. The boat is mortality, and the lesson is to be ready to lose what you love without being destroyed by it. To read it as advice on abandonment is to read it badly.

Musonius accepted most of this. "Fair. Read as a lesson about death, it is better than I have made it sound." He still held his point. Being ready to lose someone is compatible with caring for them completely until you do. The words "without even turning around" go too far even for death. Turning around to say goodbye, to leave instructions, to make sure the child knows the plan, does not mean you are clinging. It means you are still doing your part.

Someone asked him to settle a case: a nurse in Eldoret weighing a two-year contract in Doha against her mother's need for care. He declined. He did not know what the contract paid, who else in her family could help, what her mother wanted, or whether the offer would come again. Those are the facts the answer depends on, and he would not supply the family's obligations from a general idea of what East African families expect, because families here differ. "Bring me those details," he said, "and I'll argue with you properly."
