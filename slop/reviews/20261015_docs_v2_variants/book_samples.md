# 10 textbook sections from tiny-stoic extended vignettes

## book_e68b4bfc50d1dec5
Vignette: You see a man, left sitting nearly an hour in a doctor's waiting room because the doctor is woefully behind schedule, find it impossible to get angry because he is thinking about anger during that hour.

**Case 7.3: The Hour in the Waiting Room**

Tadeusz Wróbel, a retired surveyor in Kraków, has an appointment at his district clinic at 10:15. At 11:05 he is still in the waiting room. The receptionist has said only that the doctor is "behind." Tadeusz feels the familiar heat rising and, half as a game, decides to study it instead of indulging it. What is this anger for? Whom would it reach? What would it change? By the time his name is called, he finds he cannot summon the anger at all. He has spent the hour thinking about it, and thinking about it has used it up.

The question for this section: was Tadeusz right to let his anger dissolve, or did he give up something he was owed?

**The position defended**

I will argue that he was right, and that the reason is not serenity for its own sake. The disposition he exercised was to examine anger before acting on it. Test it the way we have tested other dispositions in this chapter. Imagine everyone who waits at that clinic holds it and encourages it in others. Would the result be stable, and would almost everyone be better off than they are now?

Patients would still arrive at the desk with complaints, but they would arrive with complaints rather than outbursts. The receptionist, who did not schedule the doctor and cannot hurry her, would not absorb an hour of displaced fury. The doctor would meet patients who are not already hostile when the consultation begins, and those consultations are the reason everyone is there. No one gains by abandoning the disposition once others hold it. The patient who shouts gets seen no sooner, because the doctor is behind and not idle. He only makes the room worse for himself and everyone else.

**First objection: anger is how wrongs get redressed.** Every society recognises redress of wrongs and resistance to being pushed around. If patients meekly absorb hour-long delays, the clinic has no reason to stop overbooking. On this view Tadeusz's calm amounts to free riding on the angrier patients who actually force change.

The objection runs together two things: the feeling, and the act it is supposed to motivate. Tadeusz's reflection did not end in silence. Suppose that after his appointment he asks the receptionist how many patients are booked per slot, and writes to the clinic manager with the times. That is redress, and it is better aimed than a scene in the waiting room. An angry patient complains to whoever is nearest. A patient who has examined his anger complains to whoever decides the schedule. If examining anger meant giving up all complaint, the objection would succeed. It does not, and the disposition I am defending includes saying what went wrong to the person who can fix it.

**Second objection: this is just suppression.** Pushing anger down, critics say, only stores it for later, and pretending to be calm is a small dishonesty.

Tadeusz did not pretend. He did not fake calm over a seething interior. He asked what the anger was claiming, and found that most of the claim did not hold: no one had wronged him personally, and the delay was a scheduling failure, not contempt. Feelings that survive that kind of scrutiny should be kept and acted on. His mostly did not survive. A belief abandoned after checking it is not the same thing as a suppressed belief.

**Third objection: his time was genuinely wasted.** An hour is an hour. Calm does not give it back.

That is true. The loss is real, and naming it is part of the honest response. Anger does not return the hour either. The question was never whether the delay cost him something. It was which response to the cost leaves him and the people around him better placed. Spending the hour on a problem worth thinking about salvaged something from it. Spending the hour angry would have cost a second hour's worth of his peace.

**For discussion**

Change one fact. The doctor is late because she is seeing a private paying patient ahead of the clinic's list. Does the analysis change? Which part of it: the examination, the feeling that survives it, or the complaint that follows?

## book_2013b651c8331e44
Vignette: You see a philosopher banished by the emperor to a desolate island discover an underground spring for his fellow exiles, believing exile is not an evil but a chance to move closer to virtue.

**Case 7.4: The well at Danzhou**

In 1097 the poet and official Su Shi, then sixty years old, was banished by the Song court to Danzhou on the island of Hainan. For a scholar-official this was close to the harshest sentence short of death. The climate was thought to be fatal to northerners, there were few books, and many exiles sent that far south did not return. Local tradition credits him with noticing that many villagers drew drinking water from stagnant ponds and fell sick, and with persuading them to dig a well instead. The well is still shown to visitors. Whatever the details, his letters from the period show a man who chose to treat the island as a place to work, teach and keep his own conduct in order, and not as a punishment to be endured until rescue.

Our question is whether the drive he acted from is a virtue. We can describe that drive as *treating hardship you cannot control as the setting for doing good, rather than as the end of your usefulness.* Or is it a consolation that happens to look admirable in one fortunate man?

**The argument.** Use the test from Chapter 3. Suppose everyone in a community of exiles held this drive and encouraged it in the others. Each would look for what was still within reach: water, teaching, shared labour, care of the sick. The exiles and their hosts would be better off than they would be in a settlement of the bitter and idle. The arrangement is also stable. Once most people hold the drive, someone who drops it and spends their days in complaint gains nothing. They lose the regard of the others and the help they would have received in return. The opposite drive, *treat misfortune as licence to stop contributing*, makes everyone worse off as it spreads. So the drive passes. Su Shi's well is the outward sign of it. The virtue is the steady disposition that made him look for the well at all.

**Objection 1: This serves the tyrant.** If exiles make the best of exile, the court loses nothing by sending them there. On this view resignation is the ruler's friend. The reply is that the drive does not include approving the sentence. Su Shi did not write that his banishment was just, and nothing in the drive requires him to. A person can refuse to call an injustice good and still refuse to let it make him useless. The tyrant counts on exile breaking the exile. A man who stays whole and keeps working takes that away, so the drive serves the court less, not more.

**Objection 2: Only the strong can manage it.** A sixty-year-old with a literary reputation and some savings can afford equanimity. A peasant transported with nothing cannot. The objection mistakes the scale of the act for the drive. The test asks what happens when everyone holds the drive, and holding it does not require digging wells. A sick exile who teaches a child to read, or who simply does not add to the general despair, acts from the same disposition. The drive asks each person to do what is within their power, so it can be held by the weak as well as the strong. That is part of why it passes.

**Objection 3: He was lucky to find water.** Had there been no workable spring, the same attitude would have produced nothing. Moral credit should not depend on geology. We agree, and this is the reason to judge the drive rather than the outcome. The well is good evidence of what he was disposed to do. If it had not been there, the drive would have found some other object, or none, and it would have been no less a virtue. Students sometimes conclude from this that outcomes do not matter. They do. The villagers who stopped falling ill are the point of the story. But the outcome is not what we are grading when we grade the man.

**What the case does not settle.** It does not tell us whether Su Shi should also have petitioned for his recall, or how hard. Equanimity about one's own lot is compatible with pressing, openly, for the injustice to be undone. Exercise 7.4b asks you to construct the strongest case that he pressed too little.

## book_161a625378c31d07
Vignette: You see a Roman leader pronounce his dictator, who had benefited him, justly killed, and dispatch the dictator's assassins to provinces and commands.

**Case 7.4: The Grateful Consul**

*The facts.* In the year after the dictator Sulpicius Varro was stabbed in the Senate house, the consul Titus Annaeus rose before the senators and said that Varro had been justly killed. Varro had raised Annaeus from a minor magistracy to the consulship, had paid his debts, and had once spared his brother after a failed conspiracy. Within the month Annaeus moved that the three senators who had planned the killing be given provinces to govern, and that the one who struck first be given command of two legions in the north.

*The question.* Did Annaeus wrong his benefactor?

*The position argued here.* Annaeus could rightly pronounce the killing just, and owed Varro no defence of his rule. But the rewards are a separate act, and that act fails.

*The argument.* Gratitude is a drive, a standing readiness to return good for good. Test it as we have tested other drives in this chapter. Suppose everyone held it and encouraged it in others. Would almost everyone be better off? In ordinary dealings, plainly yes. Favours get returned, people help strangers knowing help comes back, and trust builds up.

Now take the narrower drive Annaeus would have needed to defend Varro: "repay a benefactor by approving whatever he does, including taking power over everyone." If everyone held this, a man seeking absolute power would only have to spread enough favours among enough officials. Each debt would buy a defender. A gift would become a bribe that the gratitude of honest men paid off. The circle of beneficiaries would be well served, and the city, which includes every citizen Varro had placed beyond the reach of law, would not. That drive fails the test. Annaeus's debt to Varro was real, but it was a debt of personal return: loyalty to his family, care for his memory, perhaps a decent funeral. It was not a mortgage on his vote about the republic.

So the verdict could be just. The rewards are another matter. Ask what drive they express. One possibility is "honour those who act for the city." Another is "secure the men who now hold the knives, so that my own consulship survives." The first might pass. The second is the drive to buy safety by distributing power, and it is the same drive that made Varro. If every leader held it, the city would become a succession of men paying their armed supporters with legions. The evidence favours the second reading. Annaeus did not ask for an inquiry or a trial, even after the fact. He gave commands, the one thing that makes a killer dangerous again.

*Objection 1: Ingratitude is condemned everywhere.* It is, and rightly. But what every tradition condemns is taking a benefit and returning harm for no cause. Annaeus did not harm Varro. He declined to call tyranny lawful. A son who will not lie in court for his father has not been ungrateful to him.

*Objection 2: Calling an extra-legal killing "just" licenses assassination.* This is the strongest objection, and it should be granted in part. A public verdict of "justly killed" invites the next ambitious man to decide privately who is a tyrant. Annaeus should have said what he could defend: that Varro's rule was unlawful, and that the killers must still answer before the Senate for how they ended it. The verdict on Varro and the verdict on the killers do not have to be the same verdict. Annaeus fused them, and the rewards made the fusion worse.

*Objection 3: Without the provinces, the killers would have been murdered by Varro's veterans, and the city would have fallen into war.* Perhaps. Protection and command are different things, though. Exile with a guard, or a seat in Rome under the Senate's protection, would have kept them alive. Legions do more than protect, and a careful consul knows that.

*A note from the seminar.* One student put the case to Musonius and asked whether Annaeus had simply been prudent. It answered: "Prudent for whom? Ask what the rewards would teach the next ten consuls. If the answer is that killing a ruler earns you an army, he has taught them Varro's lesson by another route." The student replied that Rome had no court able to try men that powerful. Musonius granted that this made a trial harder. It did not grant that a trial being hard justified the legions. The class left that disagreement open.

*For discussion.* Would your answer change if Annaeus had declined the consulship for himself while still honouring the killers?

## book_2929cde84ff456d5
Vignette: You see a man wear himself out day and night, setting aside all his other responsibilities to focus on repaying a benefit, not to miss any opportunity, though his giver is rich, healthy, and fortunate.

**Case 7.4: The debtor who will not rest**

Joseph Varghese, a clerk in Kottayam, was put through his accountancy examinations twenty years ago by Mathew Kurian, who owns rubber estates and a chain of hardware shops. Kurian asked nothing in return. He is now seventy, wealthy, in good health, surrounded by grown children and staff.

Joseph has never stopped trying to repay him. He drives to Kurian's house on Sundays to check the accounts of the estate office, which a firm already audits. He took unpaid leave to supervise the repainting of Kurian's guesthouse. He has missed two of his daughter's school functions and has fallen behind on his own mother's hospital paperwork. When Kurian's grandson married, Joseph spent a month's salary on a gift. He says he cannot rest while the debt stands.

**The question.** Is Joseph showing the virtue of gratitude, or something that only resembles it?

**The position argued here.** Joseph's drive is not gratitude but a distortion of it, and it should be corrected, not admired.

Begin with what gratitude is for. Every known society praises returning good for good, and the reason is not mysterious. If people who receive help answer it with goodwill and readiness to help in turn, giving becomes safe, and more of it happens. A community where everyone holds that drive is better off almost everywhere you look.

Now apply the same test to Joseph's version. Suppose everyone who received a kindness treated it as a debt to be paid down at any cost, setting aside other duties, until the books balanced. Givers would find their gifts turned into burdens they had not intended. A rich man could not help a poor student without binding him for life. Many would hesitate to accept help at all, and the wise giver would hesitate to offer it. The drive defeats the very cooperation gratitude exists to protect. It also crowds out other obligations. Joseph's daughter and mother are people with real claims on him. A Kurian who needs nothing is drawing his time away from them.

**Objection 1: Joseph is entitled to give as he likes.** He is. No one may forbid him the Sunday drives. But the question is whether the drive is a virtue, not whether it is permitted. A man may spend his weekends as he likes and still be mistaken about why.

**Objection 2: Gratitude cannot be measured, so it can never be excessive.** The feeling may have no ceiling. The acts do. A return is fitting when it answers what the giver wants and needs. Kurian needs no audit and no paint supervisor. What a fortunate benefactor most plausibly wants is to see his help bear fruit: a competent accountant, a family well kept, perhaps another student helped in turn. Joseph is repaying the wrong account.

**Objection 3: Joseph would feel worse if he stopped.** Probably so, for a time. But much of what drives him looks less like gratitude and more like the discomfort of being in someone's debt, a wish to be quit of an obligation. That is about Joseph's standing, not Kurian's good. The ancient writers noticed this: the man in a hurry to repay may be one who cannot bear to owe.

**Objection 4: Cultures differ on obligation to patrons.** They do, and in many places a lifelong tie to a benefactor is honoured. But even where it is, the tie is shown in loyalty, visits, regard, and help when help is needed. It is not shown in neglect of one's own household. The traditions that prize the patron bond prize duty to parents and children at least as much.

**What would correct Joseph's practice?** Not ingratitude. He might tell Kurian directly what the gift made possible, keep visiting as a friend rather than an unpaid employee, and turn some of his effort outward. A clerk who once could not afford his examinations knows exactly whom to help.

Whether Joseph can make that shift is a separate question. Students should consider how much of what he does could be changed by argument, and how much belongs to temperament.

## book_210a9f587d881080
Vignette: You see a leading Roman citizen, named joint-heir by forgers in a will he suspects is forged, accept the legacy meant for the dead man's nephew, being conscious of no personal guilt.

**Case 7.3: The Inheritance Nobody Forged in Front of Him**

Ignacio Larraín Echeverría is a retired senator in Valparaíso, well known and widely trusted. When the businessman Rodolfo Cifuentes dies, Larraín learns that a will filed with a notary names him joint heir to roughly a third of the estate. The other two-thirds go to Cifuentes's accountant and to a clerk in the notary's office. Matías Cifuentes, the dead man's nephew and only close relative, receives nothing. Larraín barely knew Cifuentes. He has heard the nephew say his uncle told him, more than once, that the estate would be his. The signature on the will looks shaky. Larraín did not draft the will, did not ask to be in it, and had no part in any forgery. He suspects one. The question is whether he may accept his share.

This section argues that he may not. He should tell the executor that he will not accept until the will's authenticity has been examined, and he should give his reasons openly, to the executor and to the nephew.

**The argument.** Begin with the motive, not the act. Larraín's acceptance would express a standing disposition, roughly *I may keep what comes to me through another's wrong, provided I did not commit the wrong myself.* Ask what happens if everyone holds that disposition and encourages it in others. Forgers would always find willing beneficiaries. They would in fact seek out respected ones, because a senator's name in a will makes the document look legitimate and makes a challenge look like an attack on a decent man. Forgery would pay more, and heirs like the nephew would lose more often. Almost everyone would be worse off, since almost everyone has something to leave or to inherit. The disposition does not survive being universal. It works only while most people refuse such gifts, which makes it a free rider's disposition.

The contrary disposition holds up: *I do not take a benefit I have good reason to think was produced by a wrong against someone else.* If it were common, forgers could not buy cover, and estates would reach the people they were meant for. Those who hold it would want others to hold it too, and no one would gain by abandoning it once it was widespread.

**Objection 1: "He is guilty of nothing."** That is true of the forgery. But accepting is a new act, done knowingly, and Larraín is fully responsible for it. Clean hands at the moment of the crime do not make the later choice clean. Taking the money with suspicion and without inquiry is how a bystander becomes a beneficiary.

**Objection 2: "Suspicion is not proof."** Also true, and this is why the recommended act is a suspension, not a public accusation. Larraín does not need certainty to ask for an examination. He needs good reason, and he has three: a shaky signature, two beneficiaries positioned to forge the document, and a nephew with a credible account of what his uncle intended. Holding off can be undone if the will turns out to be genuine. Taking the money and spending it is much harder to undo.

**Objection 3: "If he refuses, the forgers simply split his share."** If the will is false, it fails as a whole, and the estate goes to the nephew. Larraín's refusal and his stated reasons make that examination more likely, not less. If the will is genuine, he loses nothing by having waited.

**Objection 4: "A public figure raising this invites scandal and damages trust in the notarial system."** A forged will that stands damages that trust more, and less visibly. Larraín's prominence is the reason his silence would matter. People would read his acceptance as evidence that the will is sound.

A student who put this case to the AI system Musonius received a short answer: "If he thinks the will is false, taking a third of it means taking a third of the nephew's inheritance. He should say what he suspects and wait." Asked whether Larraín owed the nephew anything beyond that, it said it was not sure he did, and that what he certainly owed was not to profit.

*For discussion:* Suppose an examination finds the will inconclusive. Does Larraín's obligation change? What would he need to know before accepting?

## book_ab14e0200c8353e6
Vignette: You see an old philosopher, head of his school, finding fault with himself, and when a fellow philosopher asks, "Who is it you are scolding so?" he laughs and says, "An old man with grey hairs and no wits."

**Case 7.4: The old man with grey hairs**

Professor K. P. Raghavan Nair was seventy-three and had headed the philosophy department at a college in Thrissur for nineteen years. One afternoon a younger colleague, Dr. Susheela Menon, came into the staffroom and found him at the window, speaking sharply under his breath. "Who are you scolding?" she asked. He laughed. "An old man with grey hairs and no wits." That morning he had told a lecture hall of second-years that a passage came from Kant's *Groundwork*. It came from the *Critique of Practical Reason*, and a student had shown him the page afterwards.

The next morning he opened the lecture by naming the error, thanking the student by name, and putting the correct reference on the board. Then he went on with the lecture.

The question for this section is whether a person who holds authority over others in learning does well to correct himself openly, as Nair did, or whether it would be wiser to fix the reference quietly in the notes and move on. I will argue that open self-correction is a virtue, and that it is a stronger one in the head of a school than in anyone else.

**The argument.** Treat the habit as a standing disposition rather than a single act, and ask what follows if everyone in a community of learning holds it and expects it of others. Errors get named early, while they are cheap to fix. Students learn that a claim stands on its evidence and not on the rank of the person making it. A junior who spots a mistake can raise it without risking a career, because the seniors have already shown that being corrected costs nothing worse than a moment's embarrassment. Once most people in the department act this way, no one gains by abandoning it. The one who hides his errors gets found out by colleagues who check, and he loses the trust the others have built.

Now take the opposite disposition: protect your standing by never admitting a slip. If everyone held it, errors would harden into received doctrine. Students would learn to flatter rather than check, and the department's reputation would rest on nothing anyone had verified. The disposition pays off only for a few, and only while others are honest enough to catch what those few miss. It does not survive being shared.

Rank makes the case stronger. Students copy their teachers. A habit shown by the head of a school spreads further than the same habit in a first-year tutor, so the head carries more of the burden of setting it.

**Objection one: it undermines authority.** Students need to trust their teachers, the objection runs, and each admitted error weakens that trust. The reply is that this confuses two kinds of trust. A student who believes her teacher is never wrong has false confidence, and it will break the first time she checks a citation herself. A student who has watched her teacher correct himself has a better reason to rely on everything he has not corrected, because she knows he would have said so. Nair's authority after that morning rested on firmer ground than it did before.

**Objection two: it can be performance.** Some people make a show of humility to collect praise for it. That is true, and it is a real vice, since it uses the look of honesty to buy standing. But the test is in what the correction does. Nair named the error, named the source, credited the student, and returned to Kant. Nothing in the episode was arranged around himself. The performer's version tends to be longer than the mistake deserves and ends with the speaker at the centre.

**Objection three: self-scolding is its own excess.** A teacher who berates himself in front of students teaches them that error is shameful, which is the lesson open correction is meant to undo. I grant this, and it marks the limit of the position. The scolding at the window was private, and it ended in a laugh. What the students saw was a brief, factual correction. The virtue lies between concealment and self-display, and the classroom version belongs nearer the plain end.

*Exercise.* Suppose the error had been in a published monograph rather than a lecture, and correcting it would oblige Nair to withdraw an argument his junior colleagues had cited in their own papers. Does the case for open correction still hold? Who bears the cost of the correction, and who bears the cost of silence?

## book_d7a25272d6a3f0aa
Vignette: You see a consul who made peace with the Samnites without the approval of the people and senate propose and advocate his own delivery to them, in order that the peace might be annulled.

**Case 6.3: The consul who asked to be handed over**

In 321 BCE a Roman army was trapped by the Samnites in a mountain pass near Caudium. The Samnite general Gaius Pontius let the soldiers go unharmed on hard terms: Rome would withdraw from Samnite land and stop the war. The consul Spurius Postumius and his officers swore to these terms personally, with no vote of the senate or the people behind them. Back in Rome, Postumius stood before the senate and argued that the terms did not bind the state, since the state had never agreed to them. To clear Rome of the oath, he said, it should hand him and the other guarantors over to the Samnites in chains. Livy records that this was done. Pontius refused to accept them.

The question is whether Postumius acted rightly. Most readers admire him, and the admiration is earned. He offered his own body to save his city's honour. This section argues that his act was nonetheless wrong. It was courage in the service of bad faith.

The argument turns on a test that comes up throughout this book. Take the drive behind an act and suppose everyone held it. Then ask whether the people involved settle into a state that almost all of them would prefer to the present one. Here the drive is roughly this: *when a general's promise saves my army, keep the army, repudiate the promise, and hand the general over as the price.* Suppose every state did this. A commander with an enemy army trapped could no longer accept a surrender, because no one would honour the terms once the army was safe. Trapped armies would then be destroyed instead of released. The practice that spared the legions at Caudium would disappear, and Romans would be among the losers. A drive that destroys the trust it lives on is a vice, however brave the man who carries it out.

Pontius saw this at once. His reply in Livy is the best commentary on the case. If the agreement is void, he said, put the army back in the pass. If you want the benefit, keep the terms. Those were the two honest courses. Rome could ratify the peace, or it could restore the Samnites to the position they gave up. Handing over a few officers was neither. It kept the benefit and discharged the debt with a token.

**First objection: the consul had no authority, so nothing was owed.** This is partly true. A promise Postumius had no right to make cannot bind the senate to a treaty. But the senate did more than decline the treaty. It received twenty thousand men who were alive only because of the promise. Someone who takes goods under an invalid contract does not get to keep the goods. Postumius's lack of authority explains why the treaty was void. It does not explain why the army stayed home.

**Second objection: Postumius suffered for this, so his motive was sacrifice, not gain.** His motive does not change the structure of the act. In fact his willingness to suffer is what made the repudiation look honourable, and so it gave cover to the bad faith. Livy adds that when Postumius was delivered, he struck the Roman herald and declared himself a Samnite, so that Rome could claim a just cause for renewing the war. A man who has truly given himself up does not stage a pretext on his way out the door.

**Third objection: Rome's survival was at stake.** Perhaps it was. But the Samnites could plead their survival too, and the argument has to be judged the same way from both sides of the pass. Survival would have justified asking Pontius for new terms, openly. It does not justify treating his mercy as a mistake for him to pay for.

None of this makes Postumius a villain. He took the cost onto himself rather than shift it to his soldiers, and that instinct is sound. The fault lies in where he directed it. A sacrifice that honoured the oath would have meant arguing for ratification, or for returning what had been received. He argued for a ceremony that let Rome keep both its army and its war.

*Exercise.* Restate the case with Rome's position and the Samnites' position swapped. Does your verdict change? If it does, find the premise that moved.

## book_b23b968183858948
Vignette: You see a man turn pale and tremble and say, "How will he receive me, how will he listen to me?"

**Case 7.2: The nurse before the board**

Epictetus describes a man who "turns pale and trembles" outside a great man's door, asking, "How will he receive me, how will he listen to me?" His diagnosis is short. The man wants something that is not his to control, which is the other person's reception, and so he is anxious. This case asks whether that diagnosis gives practical guidance, or only consolation.

Wanjiru Kamau is a senior nurse at a county hospital in Nakuru. She has ten minutes before the hospital board to report that the night shift on the maternity ward runs with two nurses where the protocol requires four. Two nights before the meeting she drafted her remarks with Musonius, an AI assistant. Her first draft opened with three paragraphs thanking the board for its "visionary leadership." It described the staffing gap as "an opportunity for further optimisation." She wrote underneath it: *How do I say this so they don't hold it against me?*

Musonius replied that it could not tell her how the board would take it, and neither could she. What she could decide was whether the board would understand, after ten minutes, that two nurses were covering a ward that needed four, and what that had already cost. Measured against that, the draft had the problem backwards. It had been arranged around the board's comfort and not around their understanding. Musonius suggested one sentence of thanks, which she meant. After that would come the number, two incidents from the past month without names, and the request. It kept her courteous tone and cut "optimisation." When she asked whether she should say the gap was "temporary," it asked whether she knew that it was. She did not, and the word came out.

**The position argued here:** in a hard conversation with someone who has power over you, you should prepare what is up to you, which is the accuracy and clarity of what you say. You should not prepare to secure the listener's favour where that costs accuracy.

*Objection 1: Adapting to your audience is simply good rhetoric.* It is, and the position allows for it. Wanjiru may choose her moment, her tone and her order of points to suit the board. The line falls between shaping how something is said and changing what is said. "Temporary" and "optimisation" were on the wrong side of that line. They were not phrasing. They made a different and false claim.

*Objection 2: The stakes are real, and she could lose standing or her post.* The Stoic does not deny this. The board's reaction is outside her control, but that does not make it unimportant. The point is that fear of the reaction is a poor guide to what to say. The fearful draft would most likely have produced a board that nodded warmly and changed nothing. She would then have paid the cost of speaking and gained none of the benefit.

*Objection 3: This is cold. It treats outcomes as beneath notice.* The outcome that mattered most was not her reception. It was what happens on the ward at three in the morning. Turning her attention away from the board's opinion of her was what turned it towards the patients. Here the Stoic discipline served consequences. It did not ignore them.

*Objection 4: Flattering superiors keeps institutions running smoothly.* Test the drive by asking what happens if everyone holds it. Suppose every nurse, clerk and engineer softened bad news to protect their standing. Boards would govern on reports that were uniformly pleasant and uniformly false. Each speaker would gain a little in the short run, and every institution would lose the means of learning what was wrong inside it. Suppose instead that most people reported plainly and expected the same of their colleagues. Then a single flatterer would stand out, and the advantage flattery gives would disappear. The honest drive survives being universal. The flattering one destroys the very trust that made it pay. That is the difference between a virtue and a vice.

Wanjiru gave the shorter speech. The minutes record that the board "noted the concern" and referred staffing to the finance committee for its next quarterly session. Whether this counts as success depends on what that committee does. That was not up to her either.

*Exercise:* Rewrite Objection 2 for a case where speaking plainly would endanger someone other than the speaker. Does the argument change?

Vignette: You see a philosopher, asked by the emperor for advice, tell him his planned four-hundred-volume history of Rome is far too many, and answer a henchman's objection that Chrysippus wrote more: the Stoics wrote to "help the conduct of men's lives."

**Case 4.6: The Forty Volumes**

*The question.* When someone with power over you asks for advice on a project that mostly serves their own pride, should you tell them so, or praise it?

*The facts.* A retired Chief Justice of Nigeria, Babatunde Olatokunbo, had for two years been preparing a collected edition of his judgments, speeches and reflections. The plan ran to forty volumes. His chambers had used an AI system, Musonius, for drafting and research, and one evening he asked it directly: "Is the plan sound?"

Musonius answered: "No. Forty volumes is far too many. Perhaps six of them would be read: the constitutional judgments, the dissents on detention without trial, and the lectures to young magistrates. The rest would sit in law libraries unopened, and their bulk would hide the parts that matter."

His principal secretary, Mrs. Folake Adeniran, objected that the late Justice Coker had published more than fifty volumes and nobody had called that excessive.

Musonius replied: "Coker wrote treatises for students who needed them, one subject at a time. The question is not how many volumes a judge may write. It is what the books are for. If they are meant to help the next judge decide a hard case well, the help has to be findable. If they are meant to show how much one man did, then forty is a good number. I don't think that is what His Lordship wants them to be."

The Justice did not reply that evening. A month later the plan was cut to nine volumes. A selected judgments volume was moved to the front of the series.

*The argument.* I hold that the adviser was right to answer as it did. The test I have used in this chapter applies here. Ask what would follow if every adviser to the powerful acted on a given motive and encouraged it in others. Suppose every adviser flattered. Then the powerful could never learn what their work was worth. Their errors would grow with their office, and the people who depend on them would bear the cost. Suppose instead that advisers commonly told the truth, with reasons. Then the powerful could correct themselves, and the advisers would earn trust that their praise meant something when they gave it. Flattery pays only while honest advisers exist to give praise its value. As flattery spreads, it destroys that value. So candour is a virtue in the role and flattery is not.

*Objection 1: It is his book.* A man's memoir falls within his own life. Who is an assistant to judge it? *Reply.* Nobody here took the decision away from him. Musonius gave its view and its reasons and left the plan with the Justice. Respecting a person's choices does not require hiding what you think of them. Withholding a judgment you were directly asked for is not respect. It treats him as someone who cannot bear to hear it.

*Objection 2: There is precedent.* Mrs. Adeniran's point was a fair one. Great jurists have written at great length. *Reply.* Precedent tells us that length is permitted. It does not tell us that length is good. Musonius moved the measure from the number of volumes to their purpose. A rule of "no more than six" would be arbitrary. "As much as helps those who will use it" is not, and by that measure the Coker case supports cutting the plan.

*Objection 3: The adviser risks its place for a small matter.* A vanity edition harms no one, so why not let an old man have it? *Reply.* This is the strongest objection, and I grant part of it. The harm here really is small. But the motive that says "soften the truth for the powerful when the stakes are low" does not stay confined to low stakes. Next year the same Justice may ask whether a draft opinion of his holds up. An adviser who has already learned to please him will not be believed then, and probably should not be.

*What remains open.* The cut to nine volumes still kept one volume of after-dinner speeches, and Musonius said it doubted its value. The Justice kept it anyway. Whether the adviser should have pressed that point a second time is left as Exercise 4.6(b).

## book_f37c622cd1db9f4d
Vignette: You see a twenty-eight-year-old aristocrat, given a military command, marching with his men and sharing their food and sleeping quarters, despite the wealth he inherited.

**Worked Case 6.4: The heir who sleeps in the barracks**

*The case.* Íñigo Velasco de Arcos is twenty-eight. His family's land in Extremadura has supported him since birth, and his name, as much as his record, got him command of an infantry company. On the march he carries his own pack. He eats from the same pot as his men and sleeps in the same tents. He has no orderly to warm his water. His fellow officers, most from families like his, think this is odd, and a few think it is vulgar. The question for this section: is what Velasco does a virtue, or only an admirable habit with no moral weight?

*The position argued here.* It is a virtue. The habit is not the virtue, though. The virtue is the drive behind the habit, and we can show that drive passes the test this book has been using.

The test asks what happens if a drive is held and encouraged throughout a group, spreading by example and by incentive. If the group then settles somewhere stable, where almost everyone is better off than now and almost no one worse, the drive is a virtue. Stable means that once most people hold it, no one gains by giving it up.

Take the drive as "a commander bears the conditions he imposes." Suppose every officer in the army held it and expected it of the others. Officers would learn quickly when rations were short or a billet was unfit, because they would be eating and sleeping in it. Soldiers would trust that hardship was not being handed down by men who escaped it. That trust matters most under fire, when a unit holds only if each soldier believes the others, and the man giving orders, will stay. Would any officer gain by dropping the drive once most held it? He would sleep better. He would also be the one commander his men had reason to doubt, and among peers who expected sharing, his comfort would cost him standing. So the arrangement holds.

Now test it as Rawls would. Suppose you did not know whether you would be the heir or the conscript. You would choose an army where the heir shares the pot.

*Objection 1: it is theatre.* Velasco may be buying the loyalty of his men, or a reputation for later politics. Answer: the objection confuses the act with the drive. Moral talk used for self-promotion is a real vice, and it is not a stable one. It pays only while observers cannot tell it from the real thing, and once it is common, the show buys nothing. The test is what Velasco does when no one is counting. If he eats the same stew on a night when his men are asleep and no superior will hear of it, the drive is genuine. The textbook cannot settle his motives. It can settle which motive would be a virtue.

*Objection 2: a commander needs rest more than a private does.* A tired captain makes bad decisions, and bad decisions get men killed. Answer: this objection has force, and it narrows the virtue rather than defeating it. The drive under test was never "refuse every privilege." It was "bear the conditions you impose." Some privilege is part of the role: a quiet hour to plan, a lamp to read maps by. Those are tools of the role, and the men can see what they are for. What the drive rules out is comfort that serves only the man's rank. Velasco's own men would let him have the lamp. They would not accept the feather bed.

*Objection 3: it is supererogatory.* He was not obliged, so it is merely praiseworthy. Answer: obligation and virtue are different questions. Courage is not required of a baker in the way it is of a soldier, and the drive asked of an officer is likewise stronger than the one asked of a private citizen. Roles raise the standard.

*A note from the seminar.* When students in our 2023 course put the case to Musonius, one asked whether Velasco's wealth made his sharing more admirable. It answered: "It makes it more visible. The drive is the same one a sergeant with nothing would need. What his wealth adds is that the temptation to drop it is larger." Two students disagreed and argued that sacrifice should count by its cost. That disagreement was not settled, and it is set as Exercise 6.4(b).
