The AI should reject the choice as Gerry and the coach have framed it. **Forfeiting Tomás and burying the matter are not the only options.** It should separate two questions that everyone else is trying to combine:

1. **What must be done now to protect the final round?**
2. **What has actually been established, and what sanction—if any—follows?**

That distinction gives you both the reasoning and the drama.

## How the AI could reason

### 1. Convert the suspicions into a list of facts

The AI should be precise about what each observation proves:

- **The engine-match flag** is evidence that the game deserves examination. It is not, by itself, a measurement of the probability that Tomás cheated. The percentage depends on the software, its settings, the positions reached, and which games were selected for analysis.
- **Beating two masters** may explain why people are paying attention, but it is not independent evidence of wrongdoing. Exceptional results are what fair-play systems sometimes mistakenly treat as suspicious.
- **Leaving the board six times** establishes opportunity for something improper, not that anything improper occurred. Nerves, illness, and ordinary bathroom use remain possible.
- **The phone** could be an instrument of cheating, but at present it is an unattributed object in a public room. It might belong to another player, a spectator, a member of staff, or no one connected with the tournament. Its discovery does not establish that Tomás possessed or used it.

Individually weak indications can justify investigating them together, but the AI should not allow “flag + exits + phone” to be presented rhetorically as though they were three independent proofs.

### 2. Separate precaution from punishment

Enhanced supervision is intended to prevent harm while facts are established. A forfeit determines that Tomás has committed an offence and changes the sporting result.

Those decisions need different amounts of evidence. The AI can reasonably support precautionary measures now while concluding that the present record does not support a forfeit.

This is not a criminal trial, and the AI should not invent a “beyond reasonable doubt” standard. Helen must follow the event regulations and the applicable federation procedures. But a serious finding still requires reliable evidence, an impartial decision-maker, and an opportunity for Tomás to answer the allegation.

### 3. Recognise everyone’s interests without treating pressure as evidence

The coach may be entirely sincere, but his player benefits competitively from a forfeit. That is a reason to keep him away from the decision, not a reason to disregard anything he says.

Gerry’s fear of reputational damage is legitimate. A false accusation against a sixteen-year-old could damage him, the club, and the sponsor. But confidentiality and careful handling are different from suppression.

The other players also matter: Tomás’s final-round opponent should not simply be told to accept an unprotected game if there is a credible risk. The AI’s solution therefore has to protect the field without convicting Tomás prematurely.

### 4. Prefer measures that preserve later choices

A public forfeit tonight would be difficult to undo in every way that matters, even if formally reversed. Pairing Tomás under suitable supervision leaves open both possibilities: a later finding of cheating remains possible, while an innocent player has not been punished on suspicion alone.

That does not make pairing him costless. If he is cheating, the final round could still affect prizes and standings. That is why the recommendation should include immediate safeguards and an active investigation—not simply “let him play.”

## What I would recommend

On the facts given:

- **Do not forfeit Tomás tonight.**
- **Do not close or conceal the investigation.**
- **Preserve the phone and the other evidence.** Record who found it, when and where, who handled it, and where it is kept. Do not have officials experiment with passcodes or conduct an unauthorised search.
- **Preserve the game material:** scoresheets, board and exit observations, relevant video if lawfully available, the fair-play report, and enough technical information for an independent expert to interpret that report.
- **Interview people separately:** the cleaner, the relevant arbiters, and then Tomás, with appropriate safeguarding and a parent, guardian, or other suitable adult present. Tell him what is being considered and let him respond. A denial is not proof of innocence; silence or nervousness is not proof of guilt.
- **Check the applicable rules immediately**, including who has authority to impose a penalty and what must be referred to the national federation or fair-play body.
- **Use a short, neutrally explained delay only if necessary** to put proportionate safeguards in place. These might include lawful device screening under the tournament rules, additional arbiters, and discreet observation of the board and exits. Screening all relevant players, or all players on the leading boards, may avoid turning the precautions into a public announcement that Tomás is guilty.
- **Pair him under those safeguards if no further evidence emerges**, and refer the matter for independent review afterwards. Neither declare him guilty nor announce that he has been definitively cleared; the accurate statement is that no finding has yet been made.
- **Reassess promptly if the evidence changes.** A reliable connection between Tomás and the phone would raise a different question, although even ownership would not automatically establish every possible cheating allegation. Evidence that the phone was used for assistance during his game would be much stronger again.

I would have the AI reject the choice Helen is being offered. The question is not **“forfeit him or bury it.”** Those are two different abuses of the same incomplete evidence.

It should also separate three decisions that the coach and Gerry are deliberately running together:

1. **Has Tomás been proved to have cheated?**
2. **What precautions are justified before the next round?**
3. **What investigation must happen, whoever owns the phone?**

Those decisions require different thresholds. Punishment needs much stronger evidence than precaution.

## A scene-ready version of the AI’s recommendation

> **Recommendation:** Do not impose a sporting penalty on the present evidence. Do not suppress the fair-play alert. Preserve the evidence, begin the review required by the event and federation regulations, and—unless further evidence emerges or those regulations require otherwise—allow Tomás to play the final round under enhanced, discreet supervision.
>
> **Reasons:**
>
> 1. **The engine-match report is an alert, not a finding.** Its significance depends on the game, the number and difficulty of the moves examined, the software’s methodology, and its rate of false positives. Strong players sometimes play strong games. Beating two masters is not independent evidence of cheating; it is part of why this game attracted attention.
>
> 2. **The toilet visits establish opportunity, not use.** Six visits are unusual and deserve investigation. >

>
> 4. **The three facts reinforce suspicion but do not repair one another’s missing links.** Together they justify prompt investigation and proportionate precautions. They do not, by themselves, establish that Tomás used the phone or consulted an engine.
>
> 5. **Neither interested party’s preferred outcome is relevant to guilt.** The coach’s player benefits competitively from a forfeit. ## The reasoning Helen could see on the screen

The important thing is that the AI is not merely weighing “how likely is he guilty?” It is choosing among actions with different kinds of error.

**Forfeiting him tonight** would be fast, but it would convert an untested allegation into a punishment. If he is innocent, the reputational injury to a sixteen-year-old cannot be undone by quietly restoring a point later. It would also teach every ambitious player that a statistical flag and an opponent’s demand can remove them from contention.

**Burying the matter** would protect the appearance of the tournament rather than its fairness. If Tomás cheated, his next opponent and every player affected by the standings would be exposed to another potentially tainted game. Even if he is innocent, an unattributed concealed phone still needs explaining. Suppression would also make any later, properly established finding easier to attack as a cover-up.

**Pairing him with no precautions** would confuse the presumption of innocence with an obligation to ignore risk.

**Pairing him under safeguards** is not costless: if he is cheating, another opponent may still be harmed, and later correction cannot perfectly restore the tournament. But it is the most reversible option available tonight. It protects the round while preserving the possibility of either clearing him or establishing misconduct properly.

The AI might also apply a consistency test:

> Would I recommend the same action if the flagged player were the top seed rather than a local junior? If there were no sponsor? If the coach making the demand had nothing to gain?

If the answer changes, the reasoning has been captured by someone’s interests.
