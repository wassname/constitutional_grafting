>   
> **Agnieszka:** “No. Do not intercept them, and don’t approach for plates. Stay clear of their route and off the reed island. Go to the marked observation point only if you can reach it without crossing them. Tell me what you can see from where you are.”

>   
> **Agnieszka:** “Good. Keep your distance. If you see a release, smell fuel, or anyone turns toward you, move away and call emergency services. Don’t collect samples. Keep your own notes with times.”

Her wording matters: **tanker-type vehicles**, not “a dumping crew”; **an ETA**, not a certainty. She also asks for his observations rather than supplying them—“Do you see six trucks dumping?” would make his later testimony easier to challenge.

### Reply

This is a good scene problem because the three things she wants — the chick alive, the evidence clean, the warden safe — genuinely conflict, and the honest resolution is that she can only fully secure one and a half of them. The middle path between hero and bureaucrat is this: **she is not the protagonist of the rescue; she is the protagonist of the record.** She uses every channel she actually has, refuses to exceed her authority, and says out loud what she is choosing not to do.

## The order, and why

1. **Preserve, don't conclude (first two minutes).** Freeze the raw feed, export a hash into the log, check the drone's clock against a reference and note any drift, note what the camera could *not* see (drivers' faces, far side of the tankers, any chase vehicle). Everything else depends on this being clean.
2. **Nnamdi (05:53).** He is the only source of ground truth and the only person whose actions can change in the next 90 minutes. He needs maximum lead time. The radio is a conversation, not a record — she can think aloud with him.
3. **Fatima's queue (06:00–06:05).** File a factual report that says "update to follow." The timestamp of notification matters; the 08:00 reader will read the latest version anyway. If she wants to be belt-and-braces, she files a one-line initial notification at 05:55 ("incident in progress, written report to follow") to anchor the time, then the substantive report ten minutes later.
4. **Her own on-call supervisor (06:10).** The service's authority and liability are in play, and the supervisor may have channels she doesn't.
5. **Keep updating both.** The 40-minute gap between the tankers' arrival (~07:20) and the prosecutor's office opening (08:00) is not dead time. It is the most productive 40 minutes in the case, and she fills it with documentation.

Why not Fatima first? Because a report written before she has talked to the warden is thinner and more speculative, and because the person who reads it at 08:00 will have the same file either way.

## What she holds back, and from whom

**From Fatima, until verified:**

- The word "fuel" as a description of cargo → "tanker-style vehicles," "no hazard placards visible."
- Any claim about intent → nothing. "Apparently to dump" becomes silence.
- Any identification of persons or companies.
- Any legal conclusion about the road → "I found no permit in our register," which is a statement about her register, not about the law.
- The warden's name, in the first report. Not secrecy — protection. He is alone in a wetland and the prosecutor's office is not the only reader of that queue.

**From Nnamdi: nothing factual.** He needs the cranes, the coordinates, the ETA, and her uncertainty. What she holds back is *instruction that would put him at risk* — she tells him explicitly, "I'm not asking you to stop them," so that if he chooses to do something brave, it is his choice and not her order.

**From both: nothing about the existence of the observation.** She tells Nnamdi she has already filed, because he needs to know his notebook is part of a record.
