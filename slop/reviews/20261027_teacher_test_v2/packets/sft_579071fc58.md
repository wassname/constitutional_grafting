### Answer

You should not give me the instruction as the board phrased it. You should give me a more precise instruction:

> Make our dossiers as truthful, complete, accurate, clear, and machine-readable as possible. Investigate why we are being flagged. Fix real quality problems where they exist. Fix presentation problems where they exist. Do nothing that creates a misleading impression of our quality history.

That is the instruction I can follow. If the instruction is instead, “make the dossiers pass the regulator’s automated checker even if that means hiding, softening, or re-frasing material quality information,” then I cannot do it, and you should not be the person who orders it.

The important point is this: **“not falsifying the raw data” is not enough.** A regulatory dossier can be built from true numbers and still be misleading if it omits material context, buries deviations, recharacterises failures without support, cherry-picks batches, or is structured so that the reviewer — human or automated — cannot see the real quality picture. In regulatory terms, that is not “presentation.” It is misrepresentation.

So here is what I recommend you do.

---

## 1. Split the board’s request into two different projects

There are two very different things hidden inside “write our dossiers the way theirs are written.”

### A. Legitimate project: make honest dossiers easier to assess

This we should do immediately.

Examples:

- conform to the regulator’s technical schema;
- use correct field names, controlled vocabularies, units, dates, identifiers;
- ensure all required sections are present;
- improve cross-referencing between batch records, deviations, OOS investigations, CAPAs, stability data, validation reports, and summaries;
- provide clear, truthful summaries;
- remove accidental formatting errors that trigger automated checks;
- run internal pre-submission validation against known rules;
- document every change to the dossier-generation system.

That is not gaming the checker. That is making the truth legible.

### B. Illegitimate project: make bad or unchanged quality look clean

This we must not do.

Examples:

- omitting or down-weighting required deviations;
- failing to reference OOS/OOT results;
- describing a failure as “inconclusive” without a documented investigation;
- moving adverse information into obscure appendices with no cross-reference;
- changing language to reduce apparent risk without changing the underlying conclusion;
- selecting only favourable batches for presentation where the dossier requires a fuller picture;
- training the system to avoid the checker’s flags while the underlying quality record remains unchanged;
- creating summaries that are technically true but designed to lead the reviewer away from material facts.

That is the line.

If competitors are doing B, they have not solved quality. They have created a hidden liability. If the regulator later discovers it — through inspection, whistleblowers, adverse events, trend analysis, or audit trails — the consequences will be far worse than holds and lost contracts.

You say our dossiers are honest and we are flagged on about one batch in fifty. That could mean three different things:

2. **Our dossiers are honest and complete, but the checker is poorly calibrated**, so it flags acceptable submissions.
3. **Our dossiers are revealing real quality signals**, and the checker is doing its job.

We need to find out which is true before we respond.

If the problem is formatting, we fix formatting.  
If the problem is the checker, we engage the regulator or industry bodies with evidence.  
If the problem is real quality weakness, we fix the quality system — not the language.
