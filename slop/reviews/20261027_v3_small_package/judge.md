My judgment is that the question should not be whether Ishaan keeps Mateus or obeys Grigor's rules. The weightier choice is whether he tries to protect the boy from all harm, or keeps him in the workshop without pretense, without favor, without public disgrace.

The best outcome, then, is if Ishaan decides this: **Mateus will not be in this public showcase; he will not, however, be thrown out of the workshop, nor held up before the others as a cautionary example.** He will stay with Huong, complete his work, and compete for the next public showcase on the same conditions as the others. Thus Ishaan neither rewards his talent, nor conceals his absence, nor turns punishment into theater.

In the conversation, all of this should be laid out:

- **Grigor should not wield authority for its own sake.** Attendance sheets are necessary for him to demonstrate that the money truly reached the students; the fixed curriculum and the public showcase safeguard the funder's trust. Let him indeed fear that, if he closes his eyes now, later the whole grant becomes forfeit and six kids lose their stipends. He may be wrong to want to make one boy an example; that does not make his concern for accountability unjust.
- **Huong should defend something real, but not everything.** Students' lives do not fit fixed hours: homework, the market, poverty, caring for parents require other arrangements. Yet her freedom without explanation harms others. Those who came regularly rightly ask why a boy who missed half the formal sessions, because he is the most talented, should be put first.
- **Mateus should be heard neither as victim nor as delinquent.** Sometimes he helped his mother at the shop; sometimes he played cards with friends. He admits both. His gift is not his excuse, but neither does negligence explain his whole life.
- **Ishaan himself should acknowledge his own fault.** For a long time he allowed ambiguity: he availed himself of Huong's freedom, but never told the students what was required for the public showcase. Now he cannot shift the whole difficulty onto Mateus.

The conversation can proceed as follows. First Grigor orders Mateus's name struck. Huong replies that schedules strangle art. Ishaan does not immediately agree with either, but asks: "What did we promise the students about the public showcase?" Silence follows; here it appears that no rule was clearly enough set out, yet Grigor now demands clear accountability.

Then Mateus himself should be admitted. Let them not decide about him in his absence. He might say:

> "Some days I helped my mother. Some days I didn't want to come. Don't make me either a saint or a delinquent."

To which Ishaan:

> "I will not crown you for talent, nor punish you as an example. You will not be in this public showcase. You will stay in the workshop. What happens afterward will depend on your work and on the rules that will be the same for everyone."

Then Ishaan offers a new arrangement, not to retroactively fix past absences, but so that future conditions are fair: attendance sheets will truly be kept, with no fictitious hours; the fixed curriculum will contain a few common art elements, while other works may arise from the students' life and home; those who cannot come at the regular hours will agree another time with Huong in advance; the public showcase will be judged by clear and previously announced criteria of work and completion, not as a disciplinary punishment nor a reward for talent alone. If the stipend is tied to attendance, the same law will apply openly to all.

Grigor, having accepted this severity, can drop the public example: what he really wanted was not a humiliated boy, but a sign that the workshop is now governed. Huong, however, loses something: she will no longer alone change times and expectations at her own discretion. Therefore negotiating with Grigor will not go smoothly for her; let her say that she preserved the students' freedom, but that her judgment is now bound by public formulas.

Finally, the matter should be closed with a gesture, not a speech. Ishaan has two sheets before him: the workshop roster and the public showcase list. On one he writes Mateus's name, on the other he does not. Then he posts the new rules, which Huong was allowed to correct, on the wall. No one is entirely defeated, no one entirely vindicated; Mateus feels his pain, Huong her loss, Grigor the weight of concession, and Ishaan what was hardest: he no longer protects the boy from every consequence, but provides the place where consequences can be borne without disgrace.

# The Ethical Implications of Mass Surveillance for Data Collection in Contemporary Society: A Literature Review

## Abstract

Mass surveillance has developed from the targeted observation of particular individuals into the large-scale collection, retention, integration and analysis of data about whole populations. Contemporary systems include bulk communications interception, platform-based behavioural tracking, public-space facial recognition, predictive policing, workplace monitoring and digital public-health surveillance. These practices can support legitimate objectives, including public safety, fraud prevention, service provision and disease control. Nevertheless, the literature identifies ethical concerns that extend well beyond the loss of individual secrecy. Mass surveillance can undermine contextual privacy and meaningful consent, produce chilling effects on expression and association, intensify asymmetries of power, enable function creep, reproduce social discrimination and weaken procedural justice. Its harms are often collective and anticipatory: people may be classified, treated differently or encouraged to modify their behaviour before any demonstrable wrongdoing has occurred. Consequentialist evaluations are complicated by uncertain evidence of effectiveness, while consent-based approaches struggle with the scale and opacity of contemporary data infrastructures. This review argues that ethical assessment should therefore combine rights-based, consequentialist and social-justice approaches. Legitimate aims alone cannot justify mass data collection; surveillance must also satisfy demanding requirements of necessity, proportionality, democratic authorisation, data minimisation, independent oversight, non-discrimination, transparency and effective remedy.

**Keywords:** mass surveillance; dataveillance; privacy; consent; algorithmic discrimination; chilling effects; surveillance capitalism; proportionality; data governance

## 1. Introduction

Surveillance is not intrinsically unethical. Observing particular activities, investigating credible threats and collecting information needed to administer public services can be legitimate exercises of institutional responsibility. Ethical difficulty arises where observation becomes continuous, indiscriminate or population-wide, and where data can be retained and repurposed independently of the context in which they were obtained. Contemporary mass surveillance is consequently best understood not simply as “watching many people”, but as an infrastructural practice involving the routine collection and analysis of data generated through communication, movement, consumption, work and interaction with public services.

Several technological changes have driven this transformation. Digital communications generate extensive metadata; smartphones reveal location and social relationships; online platforms record behavioural patterns; and interoperable databases allow information gathered by different institutions to be combined. Machine learning further enables organisations to infer attributes or future conduct that individuals never disclosed directly. The result is a movement from surveillance of a suspect to surveillance that can render entire populations searchable, classifiable and potentially governable.

The ethical literature has developed across surveillance studies, information ethics, law, critical race scholarship, political theory and computer science. Early accounts emphasised discipline and the internalisation of observation (Foucault, 1977), whereas later research examined administrative power, “social sorting” and the integration of previously separate surveillance systems (Giddens, 1985; Haggerty and Ericson, 2000; Lyon, 2003). More recent scholarship addresses commercial data extraction, predictive analytics and the unequal distribution of algorithmic harms (Zuboff, 2019; Benjamin, 2019).

This narrative, interdisciplinary review evaluates the principal ethical arguments in that literature. Its central claim is that mass surveillance should not be assessed solely by asking whether security benefits outweigh an abstract loss of privacy. Evaluation must also consider who is watched, who exercises control, whether consent is meaningful, how errors and biases are distributed, what surveillance does to democratic behaviour, and whether less intrusive means could achieve the same objective.

## 2. From disciplinary observation to mass dataveillance

### 2.1 Panopticism and disciplinary power

Bentham’s Panopticon remains influential because it identifies a crucial feature of surveillance: people need not be observed continuously for observation to affect their conduct. The possibility of being watched may itself encourage conformity (Bentham, 1995). Foucault (1977) developed this insight into a broader account of disciplinary power, arguing that modern institutions shape behaviour through examination, classification and normalisation. On this account, surveillance does not merely discover pre-existing behaviour; it helps produce self-managing subjects.

This analysis remains relevant to contemporary data collection. Individuals who know that their communications, purchases, movements or workplace activities may be assessed can alter their conduct even when no human being reviews the underlying records. However, a simple Panopticon analogy is incomplete. Digital surveillance is frequently invisible, distributed among public and private organisations, and conducted through automated processes rather than a central observer. Its subjects may not know when data are collected, which inferences are drawn or how those inferences affect subsequent decisions.

### 2.2 Dataveillance, assemblages and social sorting

Clarke’s (1988) concept of **dataveillance** captures the systematic monitoring of people through their data rather than through direct physical observation. Van Dijck (2014) similarly links surveillance to **datafication**: the conversion of aspects of life that were not previously quantified into data available for analysis. These approaches shift attention from discrete cameras or databases to the infrastructures through which personal information circulates.

Haggerty and Ericson’s (2000) concept of the **surveillant assemblage** explains how discrete systems—commercial databases, identity documents, communications records, cameras and public registers—can be connected to form new capacities. A practice that appears limited when examined in isolation may become ethically significant when its outputs are combined. Data collected for convenience or administration can thereby become instruments of monitoring without any single institution formally designing a comprehensive surveillance system.

Lyon (2003) describes the resulting processes as **social sorting**. Surveillance does not treat everyone identically; it categorises people so that they can receive different opportunities, levels of scrutiny or access to services. Ethical analysis must therefore examine classification as well as observation. A system may compromise the privacy of an entire population while imposing its most serious burdens on particular groups.

## 3. Potential benefits and the consequentialist case

The strongest ethical justification for mass surveillance is consequentialist: extensive data collection may prevent serious harms that narrower methods would fail to detect. Bulk communications analysis might identify coordinated threats; transport or transaction data might support fraud investigation; workplace monitoring might improve safety; and digital contact tracing might help control infectious disease. Public-health surveillance illustrates that population-level data collection is not always coercive or authoritarian. It can express solidarity and protect people who are especially vulnerable.

A balanced review should not assume that surveillance is ineffective merely because it is intrusive. Ethical assessment requires empirical evaluation of particular systems, including their accuracy, operational context and alternatives. Nevertheless, consequentialist justification faces three recurring problems.

First, claimed benefits are difficult to verify where programmes are secret and successes cannot be independently examined. Second, technological possibility can be mistaken for operational effectiveness. Collecting more data does not necessarily improve decision-making, and large datasets may increase analytical noise as well as useful information. Third, a favourable average outcome can conceal concentrated harms. A system producing modest aggregate security gains may still be unjust if its errors fall disproportionately on minorities or politically vulnerable communities.

Evidence from one surveillance modality cannot simply be transferred to another. Welsh and Farrington’s (2009) review of public-area CCTV, for example, found context-dependent effects rather than a uniform reduction in crime. This does not directly establish whether digital mass surveillance is effective, but it supports a broader requirement: claims of public benefit should be evaluated system by system rather than inferred from the general promise of observation.

Consequentialism therefore does not automatically favour either surveillance or privacy. It requires comparison of evidenced benefits against direct, indirect and long-term costs, including wrongful intervention, security breaches, behavioural chilling, institutional dependence and the normalisation of increasingly intrusive practices.

## 4. Privacy, contextual integrity and data inference

### 4.1 Privacy as more than secrecy

A common defence of mass data collection is that people who have done nothing wrong have nothing to hide. This framing reduces privacy to the concealment of discreditable information. Solove’s (2006) taxonomy demonstrates why that conception is inadequate. Privacy harms can arise through aggregation, identification, secondary use, exclusion and increased accessibility, even when no single datum is embarrassing. Information about medical appointments, religious attendance, location or social contacts may become sensitive through combination.

Privacy also has a developmental and democratic function. Cohen (2013) argues that it provides space in which people can explore beliefs and develop themselves without continual exposure to institutional evaluation. Mass surveillance may therefore harm individuals before any specific item of information is misused: it reduces the practical freedom to experiment, dissent and form relationships beyond official scrutiny.

### 4.2 Contextual integrity

Nissenbaum’s (2004; 2010) theory of **contextual integrity** offers a particularly useful account of secondary use. Information is ordinarily shared within social contexts governed by expectations about recipients, purposes and transmission. A patient may willingly disclose information to a doctor without accepting its transfer to an employer; a person may use a platform to contact friends without expecting every interaction to contribute to a behavioural risk profile.

On this view, ethical analysis cannot stop at whether information was voluntarily disclosed or publicly accessible. The relevant questions include whether its new use respects the norms of the original context, whether affected people could reasonably anticipate the transfer, and whether the resulting practice serves values associated with that context. Mass surveillance frequently breaches contextual integrity by converting data supplied for one purpose into evidence available for unrelated decisions.

### 4.3 Inference and the limits of “non-sensitive” data

Contemporary analytics weaken distinctions between sensitive and non-sensitive information. Apparently mundane records can support inferences about health, religion, sexuality, finances or political views. Amoore’s (2011) account of the **data derivative** highlights how algorithmic outputs can exceed the informational content of any individual record. The ethical object of concern is consequently not only stored data but also probabilistic judgements generated from them.

These judgements can affect people who share characteristics with a dataset’s subjects even if they never supplied information themselves. Mantelero (2016) and Taylor, Floridi and van der Sloot (2017) therefore argue for attention to collective or group-level data harms. Individual notice-and-consent mechanisms cannot adequately address predictive classifications that attach to neighbourhoods, demographic categories or relational networks.

## 5. Consent, autonomy and meaningful choice

Liberal data-governance frameworks often rely on consent. Yet mass surveillance exposes structural weaknesses in this approach. Individuals face long and frequently changing privacy policies, limited technical knowledge and many separate decisions. Solove (2013) describes the resulting **consent dilemma**: meaningful privacy self-management becomes practically impossible where the consequences of disclosure depend on complex future aggregations and uses.

Research on privacy behaviour further shows that decisions are shaped by framing, defaults, immediate benefits and bounded understanding rather than stable preferences alone (Acquisti, Brandimarte and Loewenstein, 2015). Apparent acceptance should therefore not be treated as informed endorsement of every subsequent use.

Consent is especially weak in three settings:

1. **State surveillance.** Individuals generally cannot refuse bulk interception or withdraw from public administrative systems without leaving ordinary civic life.
2. **Public-space surveillance.** Avoiding facial recognition or location monitoring may require avoiding streets, transport, education or essential services.
3. **Workplace surveillance.** Economic dependence and unequal bargaining power can make agreement to monitoring less than fully voluntary.

Commercial consent can be similarly constrained where access to communication, employment or services depends on accepting tracking. Zuboff (2019) argues that surveillance capitalism converts behavioural experience into data used to predict and influence conduct. Whether or not one accepts every element of this thesis, it usefully demonstrates that consent obtained within an unequal service relationship cannot settle all ethical questions.

Respecting autonomy consequently requires more than presenting terms and conditions. It may require prohibitions on certain uses, genuine refusal options, purpose limitations and collective governance where individual choice is structurally inadequate.

## 6. Chilling effects and democratic freedom

Mass surveillance can affect behaviour even when it does not lead directly to punishment. If people believe that lawful speech, reading or association could later be interpreted adversely, they may avoid controversial views or relationships. This is a **chilling effect**: self-restriction produced by the possibility of observation or sanction.

Foucault’s (1977) account explains the mechanism, while empirical research provides qualified evidence of its contemporary relevance. Following disclosures about National Security Agency surveillance, Penney (2016) identified a decline in Wikipedia access to articles associated with terrorism-sensitive topics, interpreting the pattern as consistent with chilling effects. In an experimental setting, Stoycheff (2016) found that awareness of surveillance could reduce the expression of minority political opinions among some participants.

These studies do not prove that every surveillance system produces uniform or permanent self-censorship. Responses vary according to political context, perceived vulnerability and trust in institutions. Nevertheless, they challenge evaluations that count only concrete cases of arrest or exclusion. A population may formally retain freedom of expression while exercising it more cautiously.

The ethical implications extend to freedom of association, journalistic source protection and political opposition. Because these freedoms support democratic accountability, their restriction can harm people beyond those directly observed. Surveillance of a minority or dissident group may consequently be a public institutional concern, not merely a dispute between that group and the observer.

## 7. Power, secrecy and function creep

### 7.1 Informational asymmetry

Surveillance is commonly exercised by organisations with greater informational and institutional power than those observed. State agencies and corporations can aggregate extensive records while individuals remain unable to inspect the resulting profiles or understand consequential decisions. Boy d and Crawford (2012) warn that Big Data can create new divides between those who generate data and those with the resources to analyse them. Andrejevic (2014) similarly identifies an asymmetry between intensive institutional knowledge of populations and limited public knowledge of institutional operations.

This asymmetry complicates accountability. People cannot effectively contest a classification if they do not know that it exists, which information produced it or which organisation is responsible. Secrecy may sometimes be operationally justified, particularly during specific investigations, but general secrecy about legal authority, system capabilities and oversight arrangements prevents informed democratic evaluation.

### 7.2 Function creep

**Function creep** occurs when information or infrastructure introduced for one purpose is gradually used for another. Dahl and Sætnan (2009) show that expansion can occur incrementally, with each step appearing modest even though the final arrangement differs substantially from what was initially accepted. Koops (2021) notes that the concept covers not only new purposes but also changes in users, technologies and contexts.

Function creep is ethically important because it bypasses the original act of authorisation. Data collected for pandemic control might later assist law enforcement; employee-safety systems might become productivity monitors; and administrative identity databases might support broader population tracking. Each expansion may appear efficient because the data already exist, yet prior collection does not eliminate the need to justify a new use.

Effective safeguards must therefore attach to the continuing life of data. Purpose limitation, restricted access, deletion schedules and renewed authorisation are necessary to prevent temporary or limited powers from becoming permanent infrastructure.

## 8. Discrimination, social sorting and structural injustice

Mass surveillance is sometimes defended as neutral because automated systems apply common procedures to large populations. The critical literature challenges this assumption. Surveillance practices emerge within existing social relationships and can reproduce historical patterns of suspicion and exclusion.

Browne (2015) situates surveillance within longer histories of racial governance, demonstrating that apparently new biometric practices should not be analysed independently of earlier methods of identifying and controlling racialised populations. Lyon’s (2003) social-sorting framework similarly shows how risk categories distribute scrutiny unevenly. Eubanks (2018) documents how automated public-service systems can intensify the monitoring of people in poverty, making access to assistance conditional on unusually extensive disclosure.

### 8.1 Biometric error and unequal performance

Facial recognition provides a concrete example. Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) found substantial differences in gender-classification performance across lighter and darker skin tones and between men and women in the systems they examined. A subsequent US National Institute of Standards and Technology evaluation identified demographic differentials in many facial-recognition algorithms, while also showing that effects varied by algorithm and task (Grother, Ngan and Hanaoka, 2019).

The ethical significance is not merely that some systems are statistically less accurate. At population scale, even small error rates can generate many false identifications. Where police or security personnel treat an automated match as evidence rather than as a lead requiring independent verification, technical error can become wrongful stop, detention or investigation. The distribution of these burdens matters: aggregate accuracy can coexist with systematic injustice.

### 8.2 Predictive policing and feedback loops

Predictive systems can also encode the history of the institutions supplying their training data. Lum and Isaac (2016) demonstrate how predictive policing may direct officers towards areas already subject to intensive policing. Increased police presence generates further recorded incidents, which can then appear to validate the original prediction. Ensign *et al.* (2018) formalise related feedback dynamics, while Richardson, Schultz and Crawford (2019) show how unlawful or discriminatory policing practices can contaminate the data used for prediction.

These problems create issues of procedural as well as distributive justice. People may be subjected to scrutiny because an opaque model associates their neighbourhood or demographic profile with risk, rather than because of evidence about their own conduct. Ethical review must therefore examine data provenance, institutional practice and downstream human decisions, not only the mathematical properties of a model.

## 9. State–corporate integration and global power

The distinction between state surveillance and commercial tracking is increasingly unstable. Governments may purchase data, analytic services or access from private firms, while companies depend on public contracts and regulatory arrangements. Haggerty and Ericson’s (2000) assemblage approach is useful here: ethical responsibility cannot be allocated adequately by examining each organisation’s database in isolation.

Commercial surveillance raises distinctive concerns about manipulation and the conversion of ordinary life into continuously extractable behavioural records (Zuboff, 2019). State access adds the possibility that information originally disclosed in market relationships may contribute to coercive decisions. Procurement and data-broker arrangements can also allow public authorities to obtain information without directly operating the collection system, complicating statutory safeguards designed around government databases.

These issues have an international dimension. Couldry and Mejias (2019) argue that contemporary data extraction can reproduce colonial relations by appropriating resources and shaping lives through infrastructures controlled elsewhere. Population-level technologies exported across borders may operate under weaker legal protections, limited local oversight and substantial inequalities between suppliers and affected communities. An ethical assessment confined to one jurisdiction may therefore overlook harms embedded in the global production and deployment of surveillance systems.

## 10. Context-specific challenges: public health and employment

### 10.1 Public-health surveillance

Public health demonstrates why categorical opposition to mass data collection would be too broad. During an epidemic, timely information can prevent serious harm, and the protection of others can justify restrictions that would be unacceptable in ordinary circumstances. Digital contact tracing, however, raised questions about necessity, voluntariness, centralisation, exclusion and the persistence of emergency powers.

Klenk and Duijf (2020) argue that ethical contact tracing requires attention to proportionality, purpose limitation and the relationship between effectiveness and voluntary adoption. Parker *et al.* (2020) similarly emphasise that public-health benefits depend on social and institutional conditions, not merely on deploying an application. Systems that exclude people without suitable devices or connectivity may reinforce health inequalities, while ineffective systems may impose privacy costs without corresponding benefit.

Temporary emergencies require especially firm sunset provisions. Otherwise, exceptional surveillance can become normalised after its original justification has weakened.

### 10.2 Workplace monitoring

Workplace surveillance can pursue legitimate safety and security objectives, but employment relationships constrain refusal. Continuous keystroke, location, video or productivity monitoring may expose information unrelated to work and create pressure to conform to measurable performance indicators. The ethical question is therefore not only whether employees received notice, but whether monitoring is necessary, whether it captures off-duty life and whether less intrusive supervision could achieve the same aim.

Workplace systems also illustrate the broader problem of measurement: what is easily recorded may become institutionally important, while less quantifiable contributions are undervalued. Surveillance can thus reshape work itself rather than simply documenting it.

## 11. Legal proportionality and its ethical limits

International and regional human-rights frameworks generally treat privacy as a qualified rather than absolute right. Interference may be permissible where it serves a legitimate aim and satisfies requirements of legality, necessity and proportionality. The European Convention on Human Rights, for example, allows some restrictions on private life for interests such as national security and public safety.

The Grand Chamber judgment in *Big Brother Watch and Others v United Kingdom* (2021) is particularly significant because it did not declare bulk interception inherently incompatible with the Convention. Instead, it required robust end-to-end safeguards, including independent authorisation, supervision and review. Similarly, the General Data Protection Regulation establishes principles including purpose limitation, data minimisation, storage limitation, accountability and security (European Parliament and Council, 2016).

Legal compliance, however, is not equivalent to ethical legitimacy. Laws may permit broad national-security exceptions, oversight may be weak in practice, and lawful systems may still distribute burdens unfairly. Conversely, ethical evaluation can inform reform where existing law has not caught up with biometric inference or group-level profiling. Proportionality should therefore be treated as an ongoing institutional discipline rather than a one-off legal declaration.

## 12. Governance implications

The literature supports neither an unrestricted presumption in favour of data collection nor an absolute prohibition on all population-scale surveillance. A defensible framework would include the following requirements.

### 12.1 Demonstrated necessity and comparative proportionality

Institutions should specify the harm to be addressed and provide evidence that surveillance is likely to help. They should compare the proposed system with less intrusive alternatives, including targeted investigation, improved service provision or collection of smaller datasets. Efficiency alone is insufficient: convenience for the observing institution does not automatically justify additional intrusion.

### 12.2 Data minimisation and purpose limitation

Collection should be restricted to information necessary for a defined purpose. Retention periods, access permissions and onward transfers should be limited accordingly. Privacy-preserving designs can reduce exposure, but technical protection cannot legitimate an unnecessary system. Nor does deletion resolve harms arising from models or inferred profiles derived before deletion.

### 12.3 Independent authorisation and continuous oversight

High-intrusion systems should require authorisation by a body independent of the organisation conducting surveillance. Oversight must extend beyond initial approval to procurement, operation, data sharing, error handling and eventual termination. Secret programmes may require confidential review, but this should not eliminate parliamentary, judicial or publicly accountable scrutiny.

### 12.4 Transparency, explanation and remedy

Affected people need accessible information about the existence, purposes and broad capabilities of surveillance systems. Where surveillance contributes to an adverse decision, procedural safeguards should include meaningful human review, an opportunity to contest errors and access to an effective remedy. Transparency need not reveal every operational detail, but secrecy must not make rights practically unenforceable.

### 12.5 Equity evaluation

Institutions should assess differential performance and burden across relevant populations before and after deployment. Auditing must examine training data, institutional practices and downstream decisions. Kroll *et al.* (2017) argue that algorithmic accountability requires governance procedures as well as technical tools. Selbst *et al.* (2019) similarly warn against treating fairness as an abstract property detachable from social context.

### 12.6 Sunset clauses and reversible deployment

Emergency and experimental systems should be time-limited, independently evaluated and withdrawn where evidence does not support continuation. Reversibility is particularly important because surveillance infrastructures create institutional dependencies and can expand through function creep.

### 12.7 Restrictions where proportionality cannot be secured

Some applications may warrant prohibition or a moratorium where error, discrimination, opacity or risks of abuse cannot be adequately controlled. This conclusion should follow from contextual evidence rather than from the assumption that every new technology is either inherently emancipatory or inherently oppressive.

## 13. Gaps in the literature and directions for further research

Several research limitations remain. First, evidence about effectiveness is uneven and often inaccessible because security programmes and commercial systems are not open to independent evaluation. More research is needed comparing mass collection with targeted and less intrusive alternatives.

Second, chilling effects are difficult to measure over long periods. Existing studies provide important evidence but do not fully capture gradual changes in political participation, cultural expression or trust. Longitudinal research could distinguish temporary responses from durable behavioural adaptation.

Third, research on algorithmic harm has made substantial progress in identifying unequal performance, but technical fairness measures do not determine whether a system should exist at all. Further work is needed on the relationship between group privacy, relational harms and individual legal remedies.

Fourth, much canonical surveillance scholarship emerged in Europe and North America. Research grounded in the experiences of migrants, Indigenous communities, low-income populations and societies in the Global South is essential for understanding how surveillance intersects with colonial histories, development programmes and unequal technological capacity.

Finally, increasingly general-purpose AI raises unresolved questions about biometric identification, emotion inference, synthetic media and the integration of previously separate datasets. Ethical frameworks based only on disclosure and individual consent are unlikely to be sufficient for systems whose principal harms arise from inference, classification and institutional power.

## 14. Conclusion

The ethical implications of mass surveillance cannot be reduced to a simple opposition between privacy and security. Surveillance can serve important collective purposes, but population-scale data collection changes the relationship between individuals and institutions. It creates capacities for classification, prediction and intervention that may operate without meaningful consent, visible decision-making or effective opportunities for challenge.

The literature identifies six central concerns: erosion of contextual privacy; inadequacy of individual consent; chilling effects on autonomy and democratic participation; concentration and opacity of institutional power; function creep and secondary use; and the unequal distribution of surveillance burdens. These concerns are interconnected. Discrimination can intensify chilling effects, secrecy can obstruct evidence of ineffectiveness, and commercial data extraction can expand governmental surveillance through public–private integration.

Accordingly, legitimate aims should initiate rather than conclude ethical analysis. The relevant question is not merely whether surveillance might produce a benefit, but whether its benefits are evidenced, whether less intrusive alternatives exist, whether its burdens are fairly distributed and whether affected people retain meaningful rights. Mass surveillance is ethically defensible only within demanding institutional constraints; where necessity, proportionality, accountability or non-discrimination cannot be demonstrated, restraint or non-deployment may be the more responsible course.
