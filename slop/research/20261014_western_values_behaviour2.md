# Western values: institutions people built, joined, defended and used

2026-10-14. Second pass. Read `AGENTS.md` and the first pass; existing files left alone. New downloads and extracts: `.local/papers/western_behaviour2_20261014/`. I used the supplied oracle brainstorm to select five additional leads; no new oracle was called.

## Result

**Inference.** The first pass was too dismissive of institutions and too shallow on Henrich. His strongest contribution is not another opinion survey: it is centuries of people moving into relatively free towns, creating self-governing associations, leaving institutions to found alternatives, and paying to acquire skills from unrelated masters. This supports a historically deep Western tradition of chosen association, exit, personal development and limited self-government. It does **not** make medieval guilds modern liberal democracies.

**Inference.** Constitutional enforcement counts as behaviour. Litigants challenged restrictions, judges ruled against public authorities, and institutions continued to provide that remedy across generations. That is evidence for a durable practice of self-binding government, not merely an aspiration printed in a constitution. The US Bill of Rights is approximately **235 years** old, not 300; its ancestry is older. Robust judicial protection of speech is much younger than 1791. Nor does a court's decision measure what most citizens wanted in that dispute.

**Inference.** I would give these freedoms a substantial but uncertain prior in Western settings. Evidence is strongest for their institutional durability and recurrent demand, weaker for population-wide prevalence, and weakest for universal willingness to protect opponents. Neither their occurrence nor their moral validity is Western-exclusive.

## 2. What Henrich adds—and what is his inference rather than behaviour

### Marriage and family programme: both emancipation and coercion

**Quote — H:1524, chapter 5:**

> By roughly 1000 CE, manorial censuses confirm that peasant farming families lived in small, monogamous nuclear households and had two to four children.

**Quote — H:1434:**

> We don’t, for example, have any statistics on the declining rates of cousin marriage in different regions from 500 to 1200.

**Inference; source type: scholarly historical synthesis; strength moderate.** Actual household reconstruction is evidence of changed behaviour. The Church's repeated prohibitions are evidence of attempted enforcement, not an automatic count of compliance. Henrich also describes people lobbying against restrictions and buying dispensations. That is especially important: the programme restricted some spouse choices while undermining parental/clan control over others. Its later consequences cannot all be called voluntarily chosen freedom. The same chapter reports very young female marriage around 1000, exclusion of non-Christian spouses, threats of excommunication and secular coercion. A simple story of a liberty-loving Church would be false.

**Inference.** The European Marriage Pattern is relevant to independence and timing, but “unmarried at 30” is not “never married.” Nor are delayed marriage and low fertility proof of hostility to marriage law. The evidence supports capacity to establish separate households and some room for individual bargaining, with important constraints.

### Towns, guilds, universities and monasteries: demand and institutional competition

**Quote — H:2297, chapter 9:**

> In the four centuries after 1200 CE, western Europe’s urbanization rate doubled, exceeding 13 percent in 1600.

**Quote — H:2305:**

> In the next several centuries, over 80 cities would directly and explicitly copy Magdeburg’s charter, laws, and civil institutions.

**Inference; source type: demographic and institutional histories summarized by Henrich; strength moderate.** These are repeated settlement and copying, not merely a decree that towns may exist. Offers of citizenship, elected office and more secure property helped cities compete for people. Yet migration selects a bundle—work, safety, commerce and rights. We cannot estimate the portion attributable to autonomy. Rulers could support privileges for revenue and military advantage without personally believing in liberty. Successful institutions can embody rights even when their designers' motives differ.

**Quote — H:2550, chapter 10:**

> the University of Paris developed as teachers essentially unionized—forming a guild—in response to the efforts by the bishop and other local religious authorities to interfere in their instruction

**Inference; strength moderate.** This is a much better behavioural lead for intellectual autonomy than the mere existence of a university. Bologna students' organization, Paris teachers' organization and institutional secessions connect action to a particular interference. Monastic secession supplies a parallel for religious community choice. However, these organizations imposed their own discipline. Henrich reports guild fines and expulsion (H:2548), not unbounded individual sovereignty.

**Source chain check.** Henrich's notes identify guild counts with Gary Richardson's Database of English Guilds and university counts with Verger (H:4539); guild enforcement with Richardson and McBride (H:4541); university histories with Berman, Cantoni–Yuchtman, Huff and Verger (H:4543–4545). These are identifiable research lineages, **not independent replications I performed**. His note also acknowledges medieval universities' borrowing from Islamic/Central Asian precedents (H:4545). An exclusively European invention story would overstate even his account.

### Markets and impersonal prosociality

**Quote — H:2225, chapter 9, about contemporary Oromo communities in Ethiopia:**

> the more likely these communities were to form cooperative associations that created explicit rules to regulate timber extraction and pasturing.

**Inference; source type: behavioural experiment plus observational organizational outcomes, mediated through Henrich; strength moderate.** The surrounding study links paid cooperation-game behaviour with actual association formation. This is a useful human-wide comparison: non-Europeans can develop impersonal cooperation and formal collective rules. It does not independently prove the medieval European causal sequence.

**Inference.** Blood donation, honest reporting in paid tasks and low diplomatic parking violations are relevant behavioural measures in chapters 6–7. They are not measurements of speech freedom or spouse choice. Contemporary comparisons cannot themselves supply a 50-year behavioural time series. In particular, a correlation between modern cooperation and centuries of Church exposure does **not** observe the same psychology over those centuries. The historical membership, settlement, apprenticeship and enforcement records carry the duration argument.

### Law, rights and representative government: feedback, not a sudden philosophical discovery

**Quote — H:2869, chapter 12:**

> In some cities, the governing councils were simply merchant oligarchies.

**Quote — H:2893:**

> Psychology, norms, and formal institutions interact in a kind of feedback loop.

**Inference; source type: historical synthesis with modern comparative studies; strength moderate for institutional development, weaker for causal psychology.** Elections, written charters, corporate consent and more individual liability were assembled and used before modern rights declarations. Modern individualism and medieval institutional evidence are mutually consistent, but consistency does not establish every arrow in Henrich's account. He expressly says the bishopric/city analysis cannot directly identify the programme's psychological effects (H:2327). His canon-law discussion is partly history of arguments: lawyers theorizing natural rights is not itself popular costly behaviour. More probative are citizens founding councils, associations enforcing obligations, actual legal remedies and institutional expansion.

**Inference.** Apprentice payments and work (H:3092–3098) support investment in skill development without reusing the first pass's literacy anecdotes. Some apprentices were children whose contracts were arranged by adults; this is not proof that each freely chose the trade. Henrich also reports longer working hours in London from 1748 to 1803, but discusses witness-selection problems and consumer demand (H:2633–2645). Longer work is **not** clean evidence for valuing personal improvement, much less radical self-replacement. I would not use that as the main self-improvement evidence.

### Haidt and Boehm: the desire can be human-wide and politically ambivalent

**Quote — R:2473:**

> The result is a fragile state of political egalitarianism achieved by cooperation among creatures who are innately predisposed to hierarchical arrangements.

**Quote — R:2481:**

> The Liberty foundation obviously operates in tension with the Authority foundation.

**Quote — R:5693, chapter 8 note 25:**

> the empirical facts may prove otherwise.

**Inference; source type: psychological theory and anthropological synthesis, with Boehm mediated through Haidt; strength moderate as an explanatory framework, weak as a dated quantitative record.** I read the liberty/oppression discussion and its methodological note, not just the already-reported !Kung anecdote. Haidt treats liberty as provisional in this edition and expressly balances anti-domination with hierarchy. Boehm's reverse-dominance account concerns collective sanctions against dominating individuals; it is not evidence that ancestral people endorsed modern free speech or individual legal equality. Its prehistoric chronology is reconstruction, not a half-million-year observation series. The same anti-tyranny emotions can motivate suppression, retaliatory violence or defence of one's own privileged group. Thus Haidt helps explain why liberty demands recur beyond the West, and why their existence does not guarantee liberal outcomes.

### B3. Britain: centuries of constraining executive power, not US-style judicial supremacy

**Quote — [*Boyd v. United States*, 116 US 616 (1886)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/116/616), judicial historical account of *Entick v. Carrington*:**

> Lord CAMDEN pronounced the judgment of the court in Michaelmas term, 1765

**Inference; source type: later primary judgment recounting an earlier case; strength moderate for the earlier history.** The opinion describes trespass litigation over officials' entry and seizure of papers, and separately records damages won by Wilkes against officials. This is a legal lineage of remedies against Crown agents, not just a right announced without a remedy. I did not inspect the original 1765 report.

**Quote — [UK Supreme Court, *Miller/Cherry* press summary, 24 September 2019](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2019/41/press-summary):**

> The courts have exercised a supervisory jurisdiction over the lawfulness of acts of the Government for centuries.

> the Order in Council to which it led was also unlawful, void and of no effect and should be quashed.

**Inference; source type: official court summary, checked against the [full judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2019/41); strength strong for 2019 and moderate for the historical overview.** Eleven judges unanimously rejected the government's prorogation. The summary traces prerogative limits to the 1611 *Case of Proclamations*; the full judgment discusses parliamentary sovereignty and accountability. This is directly responsive to the objection that courts are “on behalf of the state”: the executive tried to use a power, and another state institution stopped it. The Bill of Rights also arose in a struggle over Crown authority and continues to enter actual litigation. But ordinary UK courts do not generally invalidate Acts of the sovereign Parliament as US courts do.

### B4. France, Europe and the later bills

The 1971 ruling relied on freedom of association as a fundamental republican principle, not simply an express association clause in the 1789 Declaration.

Findings of breach also expose failures of government. ECHR judgments do not directly repeal domestic statutes; implementation is supervised through a separate international process.

**Canada — source type: official retrospective case-law overview; strength strong/moderate.** Justice Canada's [chronological overview](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/rfc-dlc/ccrf-ccdl/cases.html) includes *Hunter v. Southam* (1984: search powers), *Big M Drug Mart* (1985: compulsory Sunday observance), *Oakes* (1986: presumption of innocence), and *Sauvé* (2002: prisoners' voting). For *Oakes*: **Quote:** “the Court struck down the offence because it was irrational to assume an individual was trafficking narcotics simply because they possessed small amounts of them.” For *Sauvé*: **Quote:** “As a result of this case, Canadian prisoners over 18 have the right to vote in Canada, regardless of the length of their sentence.” **Inference:** actual constraints and an implementation consequence, not just constitutional rhetoric; the Charter's own duration still falls short of 50 years. The same overview reports upheld speech restrictions, including *Keegstra*: Western constitutional practice is not uniform American absolutism.

**New Zealand — source type: primary law; strength strong for remedy limits, not a case series.** Section 4 prevents invalidation of other enactments solely for inconsistency with the Bill of Rights; section 6 favours rights-consistent interpretations. **Quote, s. 4:** “hold any provision of the enactment to be impliedly repealed or revoked, or to be in any way invalid or ineffective”. That is what the section says a court may **not** do on that basis. Sections 7A–7B now require parliamentary notification and a government response after final declarations of inconsistency. **Inference:** these are weaker-form self-binding mechanisms, not proof of US-style strike-down powers. An attempted *Taylor* case link failed; I do not represent a NZ case-law overview as freshly verified.

**Australia — source type: official legal guidance; strength strong for the described mechanism.** Attorney-General's guidance says **Quote:** “The freedom has been inferred by the court in declaring invalid Commonwealth laws that prohibited the broadcasting of political material in the lead-up to elections”. It cites *Australian Capital Television Pty Limited v Commonwealth* (1992) 177 CLR 106. **Inference:** behaviour by courts against a federal restriction despite the absence of a federal bill of rights. This is a limit protecting representative government, not an unlimited individual freedom to speak, and is only 34 years old.

### What enforcement does and does not reveal

**Inference.** A durable, funded, repeatedly used remedy against government is a meaningful institutional commitment. Keeping it through changes of administration, sometimes accepting results inconvenient to those in office, is consistent with **stable self-binding institutions sustained by enough voters and officials**. It would be wrong to discard this simply because judges are state officials. The state contains competing offices; constitutional restraints are designed to make that matter.

**Inference.** The stronger claim—“voters positively prefer every protected liberty to competing aims”—does not follow. Entrenchment, inertia, unequal access, strategic political support and judicial independence can sustain a rule against current majority wishes. Litigation measures selected disputes. A judgment is not proof of universal implementation. These sources demonstrate acts of restraint and, in some cases, resulting legal changes; I did not estimate how often governments complied or evaded them. The rights retained are also qualified, historically unequal, and sometimes narrowed or reversed.

## 4. Five oracle leads checked

### C1. Marriage delay / “marriage strike”: pattern confirmed, proposed interpretation narrowed

**Source:** Nico Voigtländer and Hans-Joachim Voth, [*How the West ‘Invented’ Fertility Restriction*](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17314), NBER working paper 17314, revised 2012; subsequently *American Economic Review* 103(6), 2013. [Full working paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17314/w17314.pdf), §2, especially pp. 6–8.

**Source type:** historical economic study with demographic and employment evidence. **Strength:** moderate/strong for marriage/employment patterns; moderate for causal model; weak for liberty motives.

**Quote — abstract:**

> the "European Marriage Pattern" (EMP) reduced childbirths by up to one third between the 14th and 18th century.

**Quote — p. 7:**

> Marriage typically implied an immediate termination of service.

**Inference.** Long-run observed delay is real, and the authors argue it was not simply a legal ban on early marriage. Yet their mechanism includes female employment opportunities and service contracts incompatible with marriage. That is a constrained economic trade-off, not demonstrated refusal of ecclesiastical domination. The oracle's more specific Scandinavian “marriage strike” was **not** established. Henrich's summary and this paper also differ in emphasis: kinship/Church change versus labour markets/pastoral production. Treating them as independent confirmation of the entire same causal story would be misleading.

### C2. Recusancy fines: the better long-run belief-persistence lead

**Source:** [UK National Archives, *Catholics* research guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/catholics/), §§3.2–3.4.

**Source type:** archive guide to original administrative/court records. **Strength:** strong provenance and duration; moderate inference pending individual record inspection.

**Quote:**

> These punishments were not always strictly enforced and there was considerable variation according to time and place

**Quote:**

> the records of their issue, collection and enforcement provide a rich source of information on catholics during this period.

**Inference.** This is not just a statute threatening penalties. The guide identifies collected revenues, fines and land-seizure records: Dorset recusants c.1580–c.1680 (PRO 30/24), seizures 1552–1744 (FEC 1–2), and recusant revenues 1627–1655 (AO). It also identifies repeated parliamentary returns in 1680, 1706, 1767 and 1781. These spans clear 50 years. They do not establish an execution total or an average willingness to pay. Martyr counts, including Foxe's, were not independently checked; the archive-backed fines lead is safer. Refusing established worship supports chosen belief, not tolerance of other religions.

### C3. Strikes under coercive law: recurring voice, not a clean vote for liberal government

**Sources:** [Tolpuddle Martyrs Museum/commemorative history](https://www.tolpuddlemartyrs.org.uk/story); [NPS, Pullman strike of 1894](https://www.nps.gov/pull/learn/historyculture/the-strike-of-1894.htm); [*In re Debs*, 158 US 564 (1895)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/158/564).

**Source type:** movement-affiliated historical account; official historical account; primary judgment. **Strength:** moderate for reconstructed participation/cost, strong for judicial backing of the injunction.

**Quote — Tolpuddle account:**

> Members swore of an oath of secrecy – and it was this act that led to the men’s arrest and subsequent sentence of seven years’ transportation.

**Quote — NPS:**

> Fighting between the military and workers at rail yards in the Chicago area left dozens dead and more wounded.

**Inference.** The sources establish organization, strike/boycott and punishment. Tolpuddle's offence was the oath, not a simple prosecution under an unrepealed blanket anti-union ban; its petition total is a commemorative account, not a count I checked. Pullman supplies recurrence 60 years later, but the ARU was open to white workers, not all workers. The state and courts opposed collective voice in this case. *Debs* is **counterevidence to a uniformly liberty-protecting judiciary**, while workers' actions support demand for collective bargaining. Economic motives are central; consent to a national constitution cannot be read off a wage strike.

### C4. Scientist persecution and rescue: support for others adds more than famous flight stories

**Source:** [Cara, *Our History*](https://www.cara.ngo/who-we-are/our-history), with citations/links to *Science* (1936) and *Nature* (1938).

**Source type:** organization-authored historical synthesis, reporting fundraising and rescue work. **Strength:** moderate; original ledgers and cited articles not separately audited.

**Quote:**

> Between May and August 1933, the AAC raised nearly £10,000 to get its work off the ground

**Quote:**

> most of it from UK academics.

**Inference.** Actual donations and placements are costly acts benefiting colleagues, better evidence for others' freedom than Einstein's speech alone. The account describes continuing help for people escaping Stalinist regimes, apartheid, Latin American juntas and later Middle Eastern persecution, spanning over 90 years. It also reports challenging British internment of refugee academics in 1940. Rescue from persecution bundles liberty, survival, professional solidarity and intellectual productivity. The oracle's exact differential-productivity claim was not verified. Many later beneficiaries were not Western; that supports a human-wide interpretation of whose freedom mattered to these donors.

### C5. Homesteading: actual costly uptake, poor isolation of autonomy

**Sources:** [National Archives, Homestead Act](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/homestead-act); [NPS, About the Homestead Act](https://www.nps.gov/home/learn/historyculture/abouthomesteadactlaw.htm).

**Source type:** official administrative/legal history. **Strength:** strong/moderate for duration and settlement; weak for political motives.

**Quote — NPS:**

> Nearly four million homesteaders settled land across 30 states over 123 years.

**Quote — National Archives:**

> most of those who purchased land under the act came from areas quite close to their new homesteads

**Inference.** This supports repeated costly pursuit of land and opportunity over more than a century, not the oracle's precise “~2% annual death risk,” which I did not substantiate. Four million settlers/claimants must not be silently called four million successful patents. Nearby relocation, subsidies, fraudulent claims, speculation and Indigenous dispossession all complicate a heroic liberty interpretation. I would not put homesteading among the strongest constitutional priors without more motive-specific evidence.

## 5. Revised candidate “Western priors” paragraph — inference, 120 words

I think autonomy, freedom of expression and belief, exit, spouse choice, self-development, consent and rule-bound government deserve substantial but uncertain priors in Western societies. Henrich documents centuries of people joining self-governing associations, moving between towns and paying to learn from unrelated masters; these were practices, not just declarations. The US Supreme Court’s decisions from *Near* to *Matal* show a durable institution restricting government even for disliked speech. British constitutional cases show a longer tradition of limiting executive power. These histories include coercion, exclusion and reversals, and cannot reveal every citizen’s preferences. Haidt’s discussion of Boehm makes resistance to domination plausible beyond the West. I therefore see historically cultivated commitments, not uniquely Western instincts or an automatic warrant to impose them.

## 6. Manual checks and residual risks

**Manual notes.** I checked the quoted book passages with their surrounding historical arguments, inspected actual court holdings rather than treating famous constitutional phrases as outcomes, and retained negative checks: coercive Church marriage restrictions, guild discipline, merchant oligarchies, service-contract constraints, racial exclusion in the ARU, the *Debs* ruling and early US speech repression. No training data, model answers or judge decisions were generated; a model-demo panel is not applicable. All model variants: not run.

**Residual risks — inference:**

- Henrich's causal account remains a synthesis; I did not reanalyze medieval datasets or independently audit each cited historical claim. Regional diversity, unequal membership and selection into cities/associations remain important.
- No representative 50-year revealed-preference series covers all eight values. Centuries of institutions and repeated acts establish durability and recurrence more securely than population prevalence.
- Choosing a religion, spouse, city or trade does not prove wanting everyone else to have the same choice. Institutions protecting opponents narrow that gap, but do not turn judges into survey respondents.
- Comprehensive US First Amendment invalidation totals were not obtained. The selected six-case panel is transparent and auditable but has no denominator. ECHR counts are all-rights, not US-style law-strike-down counts.
- Current acceptance of entrenched institutions can be acquiescence or inability to change them, not active preference. Actual compliance and remedial effectiveness require separate measurement.
- Canada, NZ and Australia's cited modern mechanisms individually fail the requested 50-year duration. French and British ancestry is not an uninterrupted record of today's rights or remedies.
- The archival recusancy guide points to original records; I did not read a sampled set of individual fines or confiscations. Cara's monetary totals and NPS's settler totals were not re-counted from ledgers.
- Several official sites blocked requests; a successful HTTP response sometimes led to a 404 page. Failed/challenge pages were excluded. Logs retain status and final URLs; raw pages and PDF extracts are local.
- Three propositions remain separate: that people hold a value, that its institutional development was relatively Western, and that a model should encode it. This evidence does not settle the third by itself. No constitution edits are proposed as already approved.
