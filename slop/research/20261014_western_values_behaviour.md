# Western values from costly acts, not declarations

2026-10-14. Research only; no existing file edited. Downloads and readable extracts: `.local/papers/western_behaviour_20261014/`. Read `AGENTS.md` first, all four requested prior reports, and the current “Rights in practice” section.

## Result

**Inference — recommendation.** Replace the GRIP percentage list **as evidence about people's values** with a short account of costly acts. Do not simply replace CIRIGHTS with another rights index: those indices mostly answer whether people enjoy a right, not whether they sacrifice for it. Keep CIRIGHTS as a qualified, secondary check on government practice, preferably in research notes rather than the constitution. Use V-Dem for longer, right-specific country comparisons, and separate-source Political Terror Scale scores for repression cross-checks.

**Inference — what the behavioural evidence supports.** People in Western societies have repeatedly paid for freedom from domination, movement, speech, religious practice, spouse choice, and learning. Several of the clearest acts occurred well before 1976. These are defensible historical inputs, not proof that every Western person favours every liberty, or that these preferences belong only to the West. The evidence is strongest for **people wanting freedom for themselves and their associates**. It is weaker for their willingness to give an opponent the same freedom. It does not establish a longstanding popular wish for radical bodily or personality replacement.

**Inference — two separate duration tests.** An act before 1976 passes “at least 50 years old”; it does not establish 50 years of continuous population-wide preference. Gretna elopements, religious flight, literacy investment, and Asian state evasion supply longer histories. None is a representative, uninterrupted survey of revealed preferences. Modern experiments can clarify mechanisms but cannot retrospectively supply the missing half-century.

**Strength words in this report:** **strong** = a well-documented costly act and a fairly direct connection to the limited preference claimed; **moderate** = historical synthesis, mixed motives, selected actors, or indirect measurement; **weak** = speculation or a claim substantially broader than the behaviour. Strength is my judgment, not a probability. Every conclusion below is an **inference**; blockquotes are **quotes**. PDF line-wrap hyphenation and HTML soft hyphens are removed for readability; words are otherwise preserved. Short fragments are deliberate excerpts, not complete sentences.

### Evidence behind the table

#### E1. Escape rather than continued domination

**Source type:** history; first-person autobiographical account of behaviour. **Strength:** strong for the author's own choice; selected, retrospective and written for abolitionist advocacy, not representative population evidence.

**Quote — Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass* (1845), chapter XI:**

> The thought of leaving my friends was decidedly the most painful thought with which I had to contend.

> I could not hope to get off with any thing less than the severest punishment, and being placed beyond the means of escape.

> on the third day of September, 1838, I left my chains, and succeeded in reaching New York without the slightest interruption of any kind.

[Full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/23.txt.utf-8), downloaded as `douglass.raw`. **Inference:** the last sentence reports the act; the preceding sentences report perceived costs. This is more than an abstract statement favouring freedom. It still does not isolate liberty from survival or later earnings.

#### E2. Ordinary people sustained costly resistance

**Source type:** history; US National Park Service account of the Montgomery bus boycott. **Strength:** strong for sustained collective action, moderate for detailed totals without reopening its underlying records.

**Quote:**

> For nearly a year, buses were virtually empty in Montgomery. Boycott supporters walked to work--as many as eight miles a day--or they used a sophisticated system of carpools with volunteer drivers and dispatchers.

> Car pool drivers were arrested and taken to court for petty traffic violations.

[NPS](https://www.nps.gov/articles/montgomery-bus-boycott.htm). **Inference:** do not present this as a campaign for unrestricted geographical movement. It was resistance to racial hierarchy in public transport. It is especially useful because the cost was repeatedly borne by ordinary participants, not only famous martyrs.

#### E3. Political expression at risk of arrest

**Source type:** history; institutional retrospective, UC Berkeley Bancroft Library. **Strength:** strong for mass arrest and advocacy, moderate for the precise count and the page's editorial explanation of motivations.

**Quote:**

> Some students defy it and then, on September 30th, organize a 10-hour sit-in in Sproul Hall.

> The police arrest 814 of the students.

[Archived original institutional page, *Berkeley in the 60s*](https://web.archive.org/web/20130114053209id_/http://bancroft.berkeley.edu/CalHistory/60s.html). **Inference:** quote the recorded acts, not Savio's famous speech, as evidence. This source gives 814 arrests; other histories use different totals. No argument here depends on the exact number. The page's subsequent dismissive account of later protests is interpretation, not evidence I adopt.

#### E4. A contemporaneous archival check on repression and resistance

**Source type:** history; Library of Congress exhibit based on Soviet archives, describing a 1971 telegram and government memorandum. **Strength:** strong for the existence of documented protest under coercion; moderate for motives. I read the exhibit description, not the Russian originals or translation attachments.

**Quote:**

> In a telegram from 1971, noted Soviet physicist Andrei Sakharov supports the protests of two dissidents, V. Fainberg and V. Borisov, who have been hospitalized in a Leningrad psychiatric institution for “asocial behavior.”

[LOC, “Attacks on Intelligentsia: Suppressing Dissidents,” item 16](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/archives/intn.html#obj16). **Inference:** the protests and confinement, rather than the authorities' label, are the behavioural evidence. This does not by itself establish why each person protested or a population preference for offensive speech.

**Samizdat lead — verification limit.** The [encyclopaedia account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samizdat) reports:

> Over 15 years, from April 1968 to December 1982, 65 issues were published, all but two appearing in English translation.

**Source type:** history, tertiary summary. **Strength:** moderate lead, not constitution-ready primary verification. It also reports persecution of producers and distributors. Several attempted primary/archival links failed; I did not verify individual sentences or prison terms. Its dating of the Sinyavsky–Daniel trial is problematic: it calls it a “1965 show trial,” whereas arrests and trial must be distinguished. Do **not** import that date. The continued copying and distribution, not the banned works' messages, would be the desired evidence; the catalogue and court records remain a follow-up.

#### E5. Exit from East Germany

**Source type:** history and behavioural population totals; Berlin Wall Foundation. **Strength:** strong for substantial actual departure, moderate for decomposing motives.

**Quote:**

> People had different reasons – political, economic and personal – for wanting to leave. By August 1961, the GDR had lost a sixth of its population – at least four million people.

> The SED had already closed off East Germany’s border with West Germany in 1952, making it increasingly dangerous to cross there.

[Berlin Wall Foundation, “The Berlin Wall”](https://www.stiftung-berliner-mauer.de/en/topics/berlin-wall). **Inference:** use this source's definition and total; do not silently substitute other sources' lower figures for particular periods or registered refugees. The same source describes continued westward migration after 1961 and armed guards instructed to stop escapes. This supports a durable wish to leave a restrictive system, **not a clean income-controlled valuation of political liberty**.

**Income/institution-control check — contrary or narrowing evidence.** Maryam Naghsh Nejad and Andrew T. Young, IZA DP 9309 (2015), subsequently published in *European Journal of Political Economy* (2016), study actual bilateral migration, not wishes to migrate.

**Source type:** behavioural data, observational econometric study; abstract checked through RePEc, full PDF unavailable. **Strength:** moderate for the abstract's reported finding, weak for causal identification or controls not inspected.

**Quote:**

> We investigate emigrant self-selection according to institutional quality using up to 3,566 observations on bilateral migration flows from 77 countries over the 1990-2000 period.

> Once economic freedoms are controlled for, measures of political institutions do not enter significantly into our estimations.

[Abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/iza/izadps/dp9309.html). **Inference:** ordinary migration is not sufficient proof that migrants selected free speech or democracy. The period is too recent and too short for the 50-year test. I did not establish a 50-year, income-controlled flow series isolating the six values. This is an explicit research gap, not a positive finding.

#### E7–E9. Marriage choice: acts, not Church rules

**E11 source type:** history and ethnographic synthesis; James C. **Quote — Scott, p. 23:**

## 2. CIRIGHTS / GRIP: credible for what?

### Who made it, history, and scholarly use

**Source type:** behavioural-data coding methodology and project history; the project describes itself. **Strength:** strong for provenance and published rules; moderate for its self-reported quality and adoption.

**Quote — project investigators:**

> Dr. Skip Mark (University of Rhode Island), Dr. David L. Cingranelli (Binghamton University), Dr. Mikhail Filippov (Binghamton University), Dr. David Richards (University of Connecticut).

[About CIRIGHTS](https://cirights.com/learn-about-the-cirights-project/).

**Quote — predecessor's observation years:**

> covering 1981 through 2011.

[CIRI successor/history page](https://cirights.com/ciri/). **Inference:** distinguish observation coverage from project production dates. The same page describes CIRI releases from 2004–2014; the underlying method was already published earlier. CIRI's history is not the same as saying today's CIRIGHTS or GRIP existed since 1981.

**Verified bibliographic lineage:**

- Cingranelli and Richards (1999), “Measuring the Level, Pattern, and Sequence of Government Respect for Physical Integrity Rights,” *International Studies Quarterly* 43, 407 onward: verified in Wood and Gibney's full-text references.
- Cingranelli and Richards (2010), “The Cingranelli and Richards (CIRI) human rights data project,” *Human Rights Quarterly* 32, 401–424: cited in CIRIGHTS's manual.
- Mark, Filippov and Cingranelli (2024), “CIRIGHTS: Quantifying Respect for All Human Rights,” *Human Rights Quarterly* 46(2), 264–286, DOI [10.1353/hrq.2024.a926222](https://doi.org/10.1353/hrq.2024.a926222): publisher-supplied metadata and abstract verified through Crossref; full publisher article blocked.
- Wood and Gibney (2010), “The Political Terror Scale (PTS): A Re-introduction and a Comparison to CIRI,” *Human Rights Quarterly* 32(2), 367–400: **independent comparative assessment**, full PDF read in relevant sections.

**Inference:** this is an established academic measurement lineage, not an anonymous advocacy table. OpenAlex and Crossref returned 12 citations for the recent CIRIGHTS article at access; such counts are incomplete, changing indicators of uptake, **not evidence that its estimates are correct**. I did not count citations to the entire CIRI lineage. GRIP is a URI reporting/ranking layer over the dataset, not another independent source validating it.

### Why it deserves qualified trust

**Quote — scoring manual, p. 2:**

> The primary data source is the US State Department (USSD) Country Reports on Human Rights Practices. This source is used for all variables.

> To score the “Physical Integrity Rights”, CIRIGHTS also uses the Amnesty International's Annual Report

[Manual, August 2023 version](https://cirights.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/cirights-codebook-v2.8.27.23.pdf). **Inference:** do not describe Amnesty as an independent cross-check for **every** right. It supplements the physical-integrity rights; many empowerment measures rely primarily on USSD.

**Quote — GRIP 2024, p. 5:**

> It takes us a year to score, check, double check, triple check the scores, and then clean the data before releasing it to the public free of cost.

[GRIP report](https://web.uri.edu/artsci/wp-content/uploads/sites/1132/GRIP-report-2024.docx_.pdf).

**Inference:** public rules, named academic teams, report-level traceability, repeated scoring and scholarly comparison are good reasons to treat it as informative. But “triple check” is the authors' process claim, not independently measured validity. Coders can reliably agree on a biased or incomplete source. I did not conduct a fresh blinded intercoder audit of CIRIGHTS v3.

### Known measurement risks, including State Department dependence

1. **Reporting is not a census.** **Quote — GRIP, p. 6:**

   > more precisely, it indicates we have no evidence that the right was violated given the information contained in human rights reports

   **Inference:** that qualification is indispensable. Concealment, access, press freedom and monitoring resources affect what is reported. A high score does not establish no victims. The selected coding manual does not resolve every country's exceptional source coverage; for example, comparable treatment of the US requires an explicit source audit rather than assuming USSD reports on itself in the same fashion.

2. **State Department bias can enter both the original narratives and resulting scores.** **Source type:** comparative behavioural-data methodology, peer-reviewed. **Quote — Wood and Gibney (2010), p. 372:**

   > Thus, any biases found in the annual reports of the two organizations should be evident in the PTS indices.

   [Full article](https://www.politicalterrorscale.org/Data/Files/Wood%20and%20Gibney%202010.pdf).

   **Inference:** PTS's instruction is about PTS, but the same dependence applies to CIRIGHTS's report-derived scores. USSD is a US foreign-policy institution, not a disinterested sampling agency; perceived allies/adversaries and diplomatic access are plausible channels. Poe, Carey and Vazquez (2001), “How Are These Pictures Different? A Quantitative Comparison of the US State Department and Amnesty International Human Rights Reports, 1976–1995,” *HRQ* 23, 650 onward, DOI [10.1353/hrq.2001.0041](https://doi.org/10.1353/hrq.2001.0041), is the specific historical bias comparison cited by Wood and Gibney. **Its bibliography was verified, but full text was blocked. I therefore do not assert the magnitude, direction, persistence, or current ally-bias effect from that paper.** Calling the whole dataset worthless because USSD contributed would be equally unsupported.

3. **Standards change over time.** **Source type:** behavioural-data measurement model, peer-reviewed; abstract checked, not a replication. **Strength:** strong warning, moderate evidence for the particular correction. **Quote — Christopher Fariss (2014), *APSR*:**

   > The standard of accountability used to assess state behaviors becomes more stringent as monitors look harder for abuse, look in more places for abuse, and classify more acts as abuse.

   [Publisher abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0003055414000070/type/journal_article), DOI 10.1017/S0003055414000070. **Inference:** more recorded violations can reflect improved detection or broader classification. Fariss's paper chiefly addresses repression; it does not validate every CIRIGHTS right or provide a ready correction for belief and spouse choice.

4. **Absolute abuse thresholds and country weights matter.** **Source type:** coding methodology. The CIRIGHTS manual's killing/disappearance/torture rules include thresholds such as 50 or more and explain that relative population is not the standard. **Inference:** this can be defensible for an absolute rights obligation, yet unsuitable for estimating an individual's probability of abuse. An unweighted country mean gives a microstate and a populous country equal weight. Neither is automatically a population-preference estimate. Different rights also have differently constructed ordinal categories; dividing by the maximum does not make their substantive distances equal.

5. **The target is government conduct, with some variables separately coding law.** **Quote — manual, p. 3:**

   > CIRIGHTS only scores the practices of the government, not the overall human rights situation in a country.

   **Inference:** private coercion, family pressure and non-state violence may not be captured by the relevant government-practice indicator. These are central to spouse choice and self-direction. Choose practice variables rather than inadvertently including “in law” variables.

### The current constitution misinterprets the numbers

**Quote — `constitution/v4.md.j2:177` at reading:**

> Kept about half the time: free and fair elections (61%), freedom of religion (59%), freedom to form associations and assemble (56%).

**Quote — GRIP, p. 18:**

> We take the global mean for the most recent year with available data (covering our 2024 rankings).

> we have standardized all scores to range from 0-1 and then multiplied the score by 100.

**Quote — GRIP, p. 5:**

> Our 2024 rankings and scores in this report cover the year 2022.

**Inference — material correction needed.** These are **normalized ordinal-score country means**, not percentages of occasions, years, people, or countries fully enjoying a right. On a 0–2 scale, a mean of 1 can occur if every country scores 1, or if half score 0 and half score 2. Those scenarios mean different things. “Kept about half the time” is therefore unsupported. The table's title and introductory “between 2000 and today” wording are confusing, but its actual calculation explicitly says most-recent-year means. “2024 report” must not be called 2024 observed behaviour. A global latest-year average also cannot establish a specifically Western value lasting 50 years.

## 4. What should go into the constitution?

**Inference — proposed approach, not approved text.**

1. **Keep** the distinction between government power and citizens' wishes. It is exactly right that a denied right can still be valued intensely.
2. **Replace** the GRIP percentage ranking as a values input. Use a few well-documented older behaviours: sustained resistance to domination, dangerous exit, belief preserved despite loss, marrying despite prohibition, and learning despite suppression. Keep episode details in the research citation, not a general model instruction.
3. **State the limited inference:** such acts justify giving these freedoms a substantial prior, while learning the particular user's values. They do not justify certainty, Western exclusivity, unlimited freedom, or irreversible imposed change.
4. **Retain practice indices separately**, if desired: CIRIGHTS for specific contemporary government-rights violations; V-Dem for historical country/right comparisons; PTS separate sources for repression. Avoid index percentages in a short constitution unless their meaning is explained accurately.
5. **Do not infer a ranking of human ends from protest frequencies.** The earlier “what people fight for” note's opening hierarchy overreaches. Counted events are not people, issue categories overlap, and datasets select visible campaigns. Recent World Protests data are useful but do not clear 50 years. NAVCO's long series concerns campaign goals such as regime change and self-determination, not all six personal liberties; changing rulers does not prove favouring universal liberty afterward.

**Inference — response to “Did Haidt or Henrich mention it?”** Yes, they help with mechanisms and the wider distribution. Haidt discusses Boehm's actual collective sanctions; Henrich discusses historical literacy investment, marriage institutions, and paid games. **Neither passage inspected here validates GRIP's table, CIRIGHTS's current scores, or the “half the time” interpretation.** Anthropology is not automatically behavioural validation: a scholar's hypothesis about liberty must be separated from the acts they document.

**Residual risks — inference.**

- The cases establish existence and recurrence better than prevalence or opponent-tolerance. A costly act is stronger evidence of preference under the available options, not automatic evidence of moral correctness.
- Exit, protest and elopement are constrained by means, health, family obligations and anticipated success. Absence of action under repression is not lack of demand. We have not modelled that selection.
- No verified 50-year, income-controlled migration study separating all six values was obtained. No long-run marriage-register analysis was performed. Gretna's historical count remains a mediated claim.
- Boehm's original comparative article and Poe–Carey–Vazquez's original bias study were not accessible. Their titles and lineage were checked; specific quantitative findings are not represented as freshly verified. The samizdat follow-up remains weaker than the other evidence.
- CIRIGHTS's current US source treatment, contemporary independent coder reliability, and all historical variable coverage need further audit if its exact scores remain important.
- The downloaded codebook is the August 2023 version; the homepage now recommends a November 2023 scoring-manual citation and provides a December 2024 v3 dataset. Do not assume those are identical releases.
- Web pages, citation counts and constitution line numbers can move. Fetch logs preserve URLs, final URLs, failures and local extracts. HTTP success sometimes returned bot challenges or irrelevant pages; those were rejected as evidence.
