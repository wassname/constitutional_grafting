# Research: Rights union filtered by de facto enforcement and amendment survival

Convention: **[OBS]** = observation of what a source says; **[INF]** = my inference.

## Summary

**No one has run the exact pipeline you describe.** There is no single study that takes the *union* of the Bill of Rights + UDHR + ICCPR/ICESCR + Magna Carta + French Declaration + African Charter + Cairo Declaration + ASEAN DHR + national constitutions, then filters and weights that union by de facto enforcement and by survival across amendment. **[OBS]** What exists is four well-built components that have never been bolted together: (a) a union-like census of what constitutions actually contain (Comparative Constitutions Project / Elkins-Ginsburg-Simmons); (b) a de jure vs. de facto compliance score *per right* (Law & Versteeg, "Sham Constitutions"); (c) a theory-plus-test of *which* rights self-enforce (Chilton & Versteeg); and (d) a per-right global de facto protection ranking (CIRIGHTS/GRIP). **[INF]** If you join them, the "enforced core" is *not* the intersection of the documents — it is a short, counter-intuitive list dominated by prohibitions on state violence and by organizationally-backed freedoms, while the rights most often written down (fair trial, expression, socioeconomic rights) are the ones most often broken.

The single most important finding for your purpose: **writing a right down is close to uncorrelated with respecting it, and for several rights the correlation is negative.** Law & Versteeg: "not only do countries often fail to live up to their constitutional promises, but they often perform worse than countries that refrain from promising in the first place." Torture is the flagship case: "whereas 12.3% of countries with constitutional prohibitions against torture comply fully with this prohibition, a much greater percentage of the countries that lack such a prohibition (23.3%) manage to refrain from torture." — David S. Law & Mila Versteeg, *Sham Constitutions*, 101 Cal. L. Rev. 863 (2013), Table 16, <https://comparativeconstitutionsproject.org/wp-content/uploads/2013_Sham-Constitutions_with-DL.pdf>. Source type: peer-reviewed law review, empirical.

So a "union of documents" and an "enforced core" are two different sets, and the second is much smaller. **[INF]**

## 1. Does the union analysis exist? Closest actual work, with quotes

**(a) The union census exists, but as a census, not a filter.** Elkins, Ginsburg & Simmons coded 74 distinct rights across 680 constitutional systems (1789–2006) and checked how many constitutions carry each, by era. "Nearly every single right has increased in prevalence since its introduction, but very few are close to universal." — Zachary Elkins, Tom Ginsburg & Beth Simmons, "Getting to Rights: Treaty Ratification, Constitutional Convergence, and Human Rights Practice," 54 Harv. Int'l L.J. 61 (2013), <https://journals.law.harvard.edu/ilj/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/2013/03/HLI102.pdf>. Source type: peer-reviewed law journal, quantitative. Their key ceiling finding: "The only rights that truly appear universal (found, say, in more than ninety percent of constitutions) at the end of the twentieth century are a few of the classic first generation rights: freedom of religion, freedom of expression, and freedom of assembly and association. Prohibitions on slavery and the right to privacy are also quite widespread."

**(b) Amendment/removal survival exists, at the level of rights — as a "one-way ratchet."** Same source: "We show that the prevalence of every right known to us, with possibly one exception, has increased monotonically since the right first entered the universe of national constitutions. We might think of the prevalence of a right as a one-way ratchet — once introduced, the right will not leave the scene and increases in popularity." Only four rights in their survey show a flat or negative trajectory: "the right to bear arms, the right to citizenship of those born in the state's jurisdiction (*jus soli* citizenship), intellectual property rights, and the right to a jury trial." **[OBS]** This is the nearest thing to a measured "what survived" list, but it tracks *adoption over time across countries*, not *removal from a given constitution*. Elkins, Ginsburg & Melton's *The Endurance of National Constitutions* (2009) measures constitution-level endurance, not right-level repeal. **[INF]** Nobody appears to have built a right-level repeal dataset.

**(c) The de facto filter exists, per right.** Law & Versteeg computed, for each of 15 rights, the share of countries that *fully honor* the guarantee they wrote. The spread is enormous: "considerable variation exists in the degree to which specific rights are upheld in practice, ranging from 12% compliance with torture prohibitions to 100% compliance with death penalty prohibitions." (Same source.)

**(d) The "which rights self-enforce" test exists.** Chilton & Versteeg find that only organizationally-practiced rights move the needle: "five rights that are practiced on an individual basis—the right to free speech, the prohibition of torture, the freedom of movement, the right to education, and the right to healthcare—are not associated with better rights practices, while three rights that are practiced by and within organizations ('organizational rights')—the freedom of religion, the right to unionize, and the right to form political parties—are associated with higher levels of respect for those rights in practice." — Adam Chilton & Mila Versteeg, *How Constitutional Rights Matter* (OUP 2020), Introduction, <https://academic.oup.com/book/32168/chapter/268147490>. Source type: peer-reviewed academic monograph. Their marginal effects are large: adopting freedom of religion "decreases [severe restriction] by 11.1 percentage points in each year, while the probability of fully respecting this right increases by 8.0 percentage points in each year" (Chilton & Versteeg, "Do Constitutional Rights Make a Difference?", 60 Am. J. Pol. Sci. 575 (2016), <https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2380&context=law_and_economics>).

**(e) A global de facto ranking per right exists.** CIRIGHTS/GRIP scores 24 rights for every country on the same methodology, 2000–present. "the least respected human right is the right to a fair trial... five rights emerge with 66% or greater of global protection... there are twelve rights with less than 50% protection, most of which are worker rights." — Rundlett, Izadi, Frost & Mark, *Global RIghts Project 2024 Report*, Univ. of Rhode Island, <https://web.uri.edu/artsci/wp-content/uploads/sites/1132/GRIP-report-2024.docx_.pdf>. Source type: university research report built on the CIRIGHTS expert-coded dataset (successor to CIRI).

**(f) Legal "cores" exist but are definitional, not empirical.** The ICCPR Art. 4(2) non-derogable list (life; torture/CIDT; slavery and servitude; no imprisonment for contractual debt; legality in criminal law; recognition as a person before the law; thought/conscience/religion) and the ILC's eight *jus cogens* norms (aggression, genocide, crimes against humanity, basic IHL, racial discrimination/apartheid, slavery, torture, self-determination) are both explicitly *stipulated* lists — UN docs, not measured ones. <https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/commentaries/1_14_2022.pdf>. John Rawls's "human rights proper" is likewise normative, deliberately narrowed to "Articles 3 to 18 of the UDHR" (*The Law of Peoples*, 1999). **[INF]** These are the *priors*; the empirical work above is the *test*, and the two disagree (e.g. torture is on every legal core list and is the second-least-enforced right on GRIP's list).

**(g) The "is there a cross-cultural core at all?" debate has no empirical resolution.** The most quotable statement of the problem is from Singapore's then-Foreign Minister, quoted approvingly in a survey of the Asian-values debate: "Diversity cannot justify gross violations of human rights. Murder is murder whether perpetrated in America, Asia or Africa. No one claims torture as part of their cultural heritage." But he immediately added that "the hard core of rights that is truly universal is perhaps smaller than we sometimes like to pretend." — Wong Kan Seng, quoted in Randall Peerenboom, "Beyond Universalism and Relativism," <https://journals.iupui.edu/index.php/iiclr/article/download/17786/17969/0>. Source type: law review article. The same article's own verdict: "if by murder one means wrongful killing, then it is by definition bad... agreement at this level of abstraction is not helpful in resolving most pressing social issues." **[OBS]** I found no representative global survey that yields a ranked universal-minimum list. The best attempt (6,791 students, 35 countries, all 30 UDHR articles) found "a shared cross-national meaning system" but no compact core (Doise, Spini & Clémence, *Eur. J. Soc. Psych.* 1999).

**[INF] Reading of the table:** the "enforced, surviving core" is roughly: (1) no arbitrary killing, disappearance or bodily atrocity; (2) no arbitrary arrest/detention; (3) freedom of physical movement; (4) freedom of religion/conscience; (5) prohibition of slavery and of the death penalty where written. That is *six-ish items*, all negative, all state-restraint. The near-universal-text rights — fair trial, expression, assembly, equality, education, health — fail the enforcement filter, and the socioeconomic ones are the weakest of all. **[INF]** This is a sharper and stranger result than the UDHR's own list.

**Two caveats you should carry into the constitution.** First, the de facto data are not independent of the de jure data: Law & Versteeg note the negative correlations may be selection ("abusive regimes... prohibit torture as a sop to their critics") rather than harm, and warn "these correlations... neither prove nor disprove the notion that formal constitutional rights make a practical difference." Second, the UDHR was not a neutral union — it was a *cause* of the union: "inclusion in the UDHR increases the likelihood of the right's inclusion in a constitution by more than fifty percent" (Elkins, Ginsburg & Simmons, incidence rate ratio 1.53, Table 1). So the "documents" are not independent witnesses; they are a citation cascade. **[OBS]**

## 3. Gaps — what nobody has measured

1. **The pipeline itself.** No paper computes (union of documents) ∩ (de facto respected) ∩ (survived amendment). The closest is Bassiouni's inductive comparison of criminal-process rights in international instruments vs. national constitutions to establish "general principles" of law — but it is confined to criminal procedure, is qualitative, and I could only retrieve it second-hand. **[OBS]** I could not verify its full citation or fetch the PDF.
2. **Right-level amendment survival.** Elkins/Ginsburg/Melton measure constitution endurance and rights *adoption* trends; nobody appears to have coded rights *repeal* or *suspension* within a constitution over time.
3. **The regional/cultural documents are not in the comparative datasets.** CCP codes national constitutions, not declarations. I found no dataset that codes Cairo Declaration (Arts 24–25 subordinate *every* right to Sharia: "all the rights and freedoms stipulated in this Declaration are subject to the Islamic Shari'ah"), ASEAN DHR (Art. 7: rights "must be considered in the regional and national context"), or African Charter Arts 19–24/27–29 (duties and peoples' rights) on the same axes as the UDHR. **So the union is currently unassemblable from any existing dataset** — it would have to be hand-built. **[INF]**
4. **ESC rights are barely measured.** GRIP: "Significant challenges remain in measuring and defining the rights to food, water, housing, healthcare, and education." So any enforcement weight on ICESCR rights is near-noise.
5. **De facto data start ~1981 (or 2000 for GRIP).** There is no enforcement evidence for the pre-1948 era, so "what survived over time" cannot be joined to "what was enforced" for Magna Carta or the French Declaration. **[OBS]**
6. **Deep history has no dataset.** Work on homicide/theft prohibitions across legal traditions exists ("Is the Prohibition of Homicide Universal? Evidence from Comparative Criminal Law"; Cambridge comparative histories of ancient law) and finds near-universal prohibitions on murder and theft with divergent procedure — but as legal history, not as a coded comparative series. **[OBS]**
7. **No compliance series per UDHR article.** A 2023 master's thesis scores all 193 UN members against 28 of 30 UDHR articles and concludes "none of the member states of the United Nations fully comply," with compliance tracking "democracy, secularism and a high level of development" — but it is a single unreviewed thesis, and the host page returned HTTP 403 so I could only read its abstract. **[OBS]** Treat as a lead, not a result.
8. **Religion-adjacent claim worth checking further:** Law & Versteeg find religious freedom both 70.9% honored *and* positively correlated with practice, and Chilton & Versteeg find it organizational — two independent methods converging on religion as the best-enforced positive right. **[INF]** That convergence is the strongest single signal in the whole literature.
