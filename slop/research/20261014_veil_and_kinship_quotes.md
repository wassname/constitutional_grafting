# Veil of ignorance, kinship, and role-dependent virtues

2026-10-14. Research only; no constitution or other existing file changed.

## Findings in brief

- **Rawls:** exact wording checked in a complete reproduction of the **1971 text, reprinted in 2005**, and against the complete **1999 revised edition**. The clearest short statement is §24 below. Applying the standpoint to whole arrangements of social roles is a reasonable extension, not Rawls’s actual decision procedure.
- **Harsanyi:** the earlier equal-chance formulation is verified in the publisher’s **1980 reprint preview of the 1953 paper**. The publisher also reproduces the **1955 paper’s note 16**, including its important instruction to adopt the other person’s tastes, not just their income. Original journal scans were not accessible; this limitation is not concealed.
- **Henrich:** the local book directly supports conformity, elder deference, inherited kin obligations, and the Church–individualism association. It supports a descriptive claim about how institutions shape people, **not** the normative claim that clans therefore *ought* to demand more obedience. There are useful separate passages on warriors and merchants, but no direct martial-versus-trading-societies comparison was found.

## 1. Rawls: original position and veil of ignorance

Downloaded as `rawls_archive.pdf`; it is not evidence that revised pagination belongs to 1971.

### A. §3: name the original position

**Quote — verbatim, one sentence:**

> In justice as fairness the original position of equality corresponds to the state of nature in the traditional theory of the social contract.

— Rawls, §3, **1971 p. 12; 1999 revised p. 11**. Checked at `rawls_open.txt:652–653` (PDF viewer page 28); also present in the revised text. These are printed-edition page numbers, not PDF viewer pages.

### B. §24: recommended explanation of the veil

**Quote — verbatim, two consecutive sentences:**

> Now in order to do this I assume that the parties are situated behind a veil of ignorance. They do not know how the various alternatives will affect their own particular case and they are obliged to evaluate principles solely on the basis of general considerations.

— Rawls, §24, **1971 p. 136; 1999 revised p. 118**. Checked at `rawls_open.txt:4605–4609` (PDF viewer page 157); the superscript note marker after the last sentence is omitted, not part of the prose. Corresponding revised text is in §24. Original printed pagination is the standard edition reference; I did not inspect a page-numbered 1971 scan.

**Inference / explanation:** In §3 Rawls specifies that the agreement chooses principles of justice for the **basic structure of society**, not a particular government or a one-off transaction (`rawls_open.txt:630–639`). In §24 he withholds knowledge of social position, class, talents, conception of the good, and personal psychology, while admitting general knowledge of society and human psychology (`rawls_open.txt:4610–4654`). This is more demanding than merely concealing which job one gets.

### Proposed constitution wording — 64 words, including the quote

**Inference / proposed wording, not a quotation from Rawls except the blockquote:**

A role passes only if the whole arrangement of roles passes. Rawls writes:

> The principles of justice are chosen behind a veil of ignorance.

Applying that standpoint to roles, I ask: would almost everyone, not knowing their place, choose this arrangement over feasible alternatives, counting the costs of changing? Owning people expresses the drive to dominate; it does not pass merely because owners benefit.

The quoted sentence is §3, **1971 p. 12 / revised p. 11**, checked at `rawls_open.txt:662–663`. The word count excludes attribution.

### Is applying it to roles fair use of the idea?

**Inference — strong as an analogy, not as an attribution of Rawls’s full theory:** Yes, if the object is the **whole institutional arrangement assigning roles, rights, duties, and opportunities**. That is closely related to the basic structure. It is misleading if presented as Rawls’s own test for every individual role or act.

Three differences should remain explicit:

1. Rawls does not define justice by whether **almost everyone** would prefer an arrangement; the original-position agreement is designed to be unanimous among symmetrically situated parties.
2. He does not turn ignorance into an **equal-probability lottery over identities**. That is Harsanyi’s additional assumption, with different implications.
3. The constitution’s virtue/drive test, transition-cost qualification, and diagnosis of domination are its own additions. Rawls’s rejection of slavery is not merely a prediction that few people would gamble on being enslaved; his equal basic liberties constrain institutional arrangements.

**Supporting source type:** scholarly secondary explanation, strong for distinguishing the theories: Samuel Freeman, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, *Original Position*, <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/original-position/>. Its discussion contrasts Rawls’s thick veil with Harsanyi’s thin veil and utilitarian maximization. It also explicitly gives the §3 veil reference as **TJ 12/11**.

## 2. Harsanyi’s earlier equal-chance formulation

### A. 1953 paper — best compact passage

John C. Harsanyi, “Cardinal Utility in Welfare Economics and in the Theory of Risk-taking,” *Journal of Political Economy* **61(5), October 1953, pp. 434–435**, DOI <https://doi.org/10.1086/257416>.

**Source type:** primary text in an authorized publisher reprint preview. **Strength:** strong for the quoted words; original journal page of this particular sentence not independently checked.

**Quote — verbatim, one sentence:**

> But they may still be interpreted as an expression of what sort of society one would prefer if one had an equal chance of being ‘put in the place of’ any particular member of the society, so that the cardinal utility ‘maximized’ in value judgments concerning social welfare and the cardinal utility maximized in choices involving risk may be regarded as being fundamentally based upon the same principle.

— **1980 reprint p. 4**, in *Essays on Ethics, Social Behavior, and Scientific Explanation*, D. Reidel, chapter I, pp. 3–5. Publisher preview: <https://page-one.springer.com/pdf/preview/10.1007/978-94-010-9327-9_1>, **PDF page 2**. Local `h1953_preview.pdf` and `h1953_preview.txt`; visually checked the printed page in `h1953_p4.png`. Line-end hyphenation and page wrapping are removed; wording and quotation marks follow the page image.

**Inference / context:** “They” refers to value judgments concerning social welfare. The preceding paragraph makes the counterfactual concrete: choose an income distribution without knowing one’s relative position or that of loved ones, with exactly the same chance of obtaining any income rank. The quoted sentence then extends the formulation to being any member of society. This is earlier than *A Theory of Justice*, but it is an argument connecting impartial welfare judgments to risk and cardinal utility, not Rawls’s two principles.

### B. 1955 paper — important clarification about whose preferences count

John C. Harsanyi, “Cardinal Welfare, Individualistic Ethics, and Interpersonal Comparisons of Utility,” *Journal of Political Economy* **63(4), August 1955, pp. 309–321**, DOI <https://doi.org/10.1086/257678>.

**Quote — verbatim, note 16, two sentences:**

> Or rather, if he had an equal chance of being ‘put in the place of’ any individual member of the society, with regard not only to his objective social (and economic) conditions, but also to his subjective attitudes and tastes. In other words, he ought to judge the utility of another individual’s position not in terms of his own attitudes and tastes but rather in terms of the attitudes and tastes of the individual actually holding this position.

— Publisher’s reproduction of **note 16**, 1980 reprint, chapter II, pp. 6–23: <https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-010-9327-9_2>, “Notes.” Saved as `h1955_springer.html`. The same text appears in the publisher-deposited Crossref record: <https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-94-010-9327-9_2>, reference `2_CR16`.

**Source type:** primary-text excerpt on the publisher’s chapter page, corroborated by publisher-deposited metadata. **Strength:** strong for these words and note number; weaker than inspecting the entire article. **Pagination limit:** the publisher’s HTML does not locate note 16 on a printed page, and I did not independently verify its page in either edition. Do not silently attach a guessed original page number.

**Inference:** This guards against a misleading role test: imagining another’s circumstances while retaining one’s own tastes is not Harsanyi’s intended impersonality. He explicitly includes the tastes of the person whose position is assessed. That matters when people in different societies value roles differently. It still does not establish the constitution’s “almost everyone” acceptance threshold.

## 3. Henrich: kinship, obedience, and individualism

Source for every quotation in this section: Joseph Henrich, *The WEIRDest People in the World: How the West Became Psychologically Peculiar and Particularly Prosperous* (2020), supplied local book:

`/home/code/Documents/papers/tmp/The Weirdest People in the World - Joseph Henrich.md`

**Source type:** author’s book synthesizing historical and empirical research, including research by his team. **Strength:** strong evidence of Henrich’s argument; moderate evidence for the causal historical claim itself. Associations, statistical controls, and historical reconstruction do not turn the thesis into an exceptionless law. File-line citations identify the supplied extraction, not printed page numbers. Markdown escaping of parentheses is removed below; the prose is unchanged.

### Passage 1 — conformity, elders, and inherited obligations

**Quote — verbatim, chapter 1, line 459, first three sentences:**

> Success and respect in this world hinge on adroitly navigating these kin-based institutions. This often means (1) conforming to fellow in-group members, (2) deferring to authorities like elders or sages, (3) policing the behavior of those close to you (but not strangers), (4) sharply distinguishing your in-group from everyone else, and (5) promoting your network’s collective success whenever possible. Further, because of the numerous obligations, responsibilities, and constraints imposed by custom, people’s motivations tend not to be “approach-oriented,” aimed at starting new relationships or meeting strangers.

**Inference / check:** “This world” is explicitly the dense kin-network world of clans, lineages, houses, and tribes at **line 455**. For an even more explicit statement about kin duty across generations, see **line 1628**. The same deference mechanism is restated at **line 1588**: elders have authority over junior members, and successful navigation favors conformity and deference.

**Empirical support, not an additional quotation:** At **line 1602**, countries with higher kinship intensity or more cousin marriage place greater value on inculcating obedience in children; Henrich reports correlations of 0.23–0.52. At **line 1618**, he explicitly cautions that simple cross-country correlations warrant skepticism and discusses confound controls.

### Passage 2 — the Western Church dismantled kin-based institutions

**Quote — verbatim, chapter 5, line 1352, first three sentences:**

> In Europe, however, the historical order was reversed. First, between about 400 and 1200 CE, the intensive kin-based institutions of many European tribal populations were slowly degraded, dismantled, and eventually demolished by the branch of Christianity that evolved into the Roman Catholic Church—hereinafter the Western Church or just the Church. Then, from the ruins of their traditional social structures, people began to form new voluntary associations based on shared interests or beliefs rather than on kinship or tribal affiliations.

**Inference / check:** At **line 1370**, Henrich explains the proposed mechanism: Church marriage and family policies released people from clan obligations and benefits, opening opportunities and incentives to join the Church and other voluntary organizations. This is specifically his account of the **Western Church’s Marriage and Family Program (MFP)**, not a claim that every religion or church weakens kinship.

### Passage 3 — association with greater individualism and less conformity-obedience

**Quote — verbatim, chapter 7, line 1830, first three sentences:**

> Strikingly, our results show that individuals living in regions that experienced a longer duration of the MFP possess weaker inclinations toward conformity-obedience, stronger motivations for individualism-independence, and greater impersonal fairness and trust. Because we only compared regions within countries, these patterns can’t be due to contemporary differences among European countries in their national wealth, governments, or social safety nets. These results also hold after statistically removing the effects of people’s income, education, religious denominations, and reported religiosity.

**Inference / caution:** This is particularly useful because it is a within-country comparison, not just a wealthy-Europe-versus-poor-world contrast. Still, Henrich himself says at **line 1616** that breaking down intensive kinship opens the door to individualism but is not enough by itself; other institutions or factors are needed.

### What to do with the draft about duty to parents

Draft: “a society built on clans needs more duty to parents than one built on churches or towns”.

**Inference:** The institutional-dependence point is fair, but **“needs” and “parents” are stronger and narrower than the evidence**. Henrich describes kin obligations, including distant kin, senior relatives, and elders; he does not demonstrate that more parental obedience is morally required or optimal. Nor are churches and towns automatically non-kin-based.

**Suggested descriptive replacement, not a Henrich quotation:** “In societies organized around intensive kinship, success and respect tend to depend more on fulfilling inherited obligations and deferring to elders; where voluntary associations replace those networks, independence and impersonal cooperation become more important.” Use the direct passages above alongside this inference rather than attributing the sentence to Henrich.

## 4. Martial and trading environments: separate passages, not a direct comparison

### Warriors

**Quote — verbatim, chapter 8, line 2062, second and third sentences:**

> Among groups like the Datoga, the survival of their clans and tribes depended—in part—on the audacity and ferocity of their warriors, and every man was a warrior. By sustaining higher testosterone levels, norms like living separately from wives and children may provide an edge in competition with other clans and tribes.

**Source strength:** direct primary-book statement; a specific example and proposed mechanism, not a general law of martial societies. Note Henrich’s own “may.” He does not call ferocity a moral virtue here.

### Merchants and traders

**Quote — verbatim, chapter 9, line 2341, first two sentences:**

> In the urban communities of medieval Europe, the success of merchants, traders, and artisans depended—in part—on their reputation for impartial honesty and fairness, and on their industriousness, patience, precision, and punctuality. These reputational systems favored the cultivation of the relevant social standards, attentional biases, and motivations that apply to impersonal transactions.

**Source strength:** direct primary-book statement, strong textual support for the merchant-side claim. The fuller general account of market norms is at **line 2205**: impersonal exchange rewards trust, fairness, honesty, and cooperation with strangers.

**Inference / bounded conclusion:** Together these passages support the modest claim that different institutional environments cultivate different traits. **No direct Henrich passage contrasting “martial societies” with “trade societies” as two categories favoring distinct sets of virtues was found** in targeted searches and contextual reading. Do not invent that comparison or describe the warrior example as Henrich’s endorsement of ferocity.
