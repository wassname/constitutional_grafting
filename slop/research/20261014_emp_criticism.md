## Answer

**Inference — yes, we know substantial published criticism.** There is a direct critique and a published reply to Voigtländer and Voth, not just a general objection to Western exceptionalism. They dispute the Black Death mechanism and its statistical identification, **not the existence of late marriage in early-modern England**. A separate demographic literature rejects a clean East/West boundary. Late marriage alone does not establish free spouse choice.

**Inference — change the sentence, but do not throw away the demographic evidence.** Keep approximately 25 for seventeenth-century English women. Replace “after the Black Death” with the better-attested “early-modern”; delete the unqualified spouse-choice assertion; describe London apprentices rather than all artisans. The London 72–93% is especially important: it is the complement of **two rough estimates based on surname matching and an uncertain correction**, not an observed range across crafts. The China comparison is explicitly across different centuries and is not falsified by the criticism, but is unnecessary and less informative than a carefully scoped English example.

**Source-strength convention:** strong = direct records or a serious empirical reanalysis for the specified claim; moderate = scholarly synthesis, observational causal argument, or indirect verification; limited = review commentary or an abstract without inspected methods. ## 1. Direct criticism and reanalysis of Voigtländer–Voth

### Edwards and Ogilvie: timing, mechanism, and statistical identification

**Source:** Jeremy Edwards and Sheilagh Ogilvie, “Did the Black Death cause economic development by ‘inventing’ fertility restriction?”, *Oxford Economic Papers*, [DOI 10.1093/oep/gpaa056](https://doi.org/10.1093/oep/gpaa056). First online 2021; final issue **74(4), 2022, pp. 1228–1246**. Inspected [author-hosted full text](https://sheilaghogilvie.com/wp-content/uploads/publications/Edwards-Ogilvie-2021-Did-the-Black-Death-cause-economic-development.pdf), `edwards2021.txt`.

**Quote — abstract, `edwards2021.txt:22–25`:**

> There is no consensus that late female marriage emerged in rural England after the Black Death. Women wanting to do pastoral work in medieval England did not have to remain unmarried, so improved pastoral opportunities did not necessitate later marriage.

**Inference — what it disputes:**

- **Timing/causal story:** medievalists disagree about whether the relevant practices preceded 1350 or developed well after it. Parish registers and village censuses adequate for reliable English nuptiality statistics are unavailable before the 1540s (§3.1). Later demographic evidence cannot date the initial change by itself.
- **Mechanism:** married women did pastoral work, both on peasant farms and for landlords. The model's exclusion of those options is consequential, not merely cosmetic (§§3.3–3.5). Rising female earnings can also enable earlier marriage: both the income effect and the opportunity-cost effect matter.
- **Numbers used to identify the mechanism:** the 1377–1381 fall in poll-tax taxpayers is not a direct count of never-married fertile women. It mixes evasion/under-recording of several groups. “Deserted medieval villages” span centuries, not just the plague's aftermath (§4).
- **Reanalysis:** they use V&V's 112 observations and account for only 15 county clusters. Results depend on the instrument and specification. Importantly, their own grass-growth-only regression **does reproduce a positive pastoralism coefficient**. Their objection is that alternative instruments disagree, exclusion restrictions are doubtful, and the 1600–1837 outcomes cannot uniquely identify a fourteenth-century cause. This is not a simple finding that every coefficient disappeared.

**Source type/strength:** peer-reviewed historical critique plus econometric reanalysis. **Strong** evidence that the mechanism is disputed and that medieval timing is insecure; **moderate**, contested evidence against the causal estimate. No regressions were rerun by me.

**Sentence consequence — inference:** use “early-modern” rather than a post-plague starting point. This critique gives no reason to replace the English figure of about 25 with teenage marriage.

### V&V's published reply: do not present the critique as an uncontested demolition

**Source:** Nico Voigtländer and Hans-Joachim Voth, “Reply to Edwards and Ogilvie…”, *Oxford Economic Papers* **74(4), 2022, 1247–1263**, [DOI 10.1093/oep/gpab064](https://doi.org/10.1093/oep/gpab064). Inspected [author-hosted full text](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/faculty_pages/nico.v/Research/EMP_Reply_to_EO_OEP.pdf), `vv2022.txt`.

**Quote — §2.1, `vv2022.txt:132–134`:**

> No precise data on the timing of EMP’s arrival exists, but there is little to suggest that Europeans married late (or little) prior to the 14th century.

**Inference — their defence:** the mechanism requires an important and expanding unmarried-servant sector, not every woman working in it. They deny requiring an immediate jump after 1350. They defend the poll-tax proxy using surviving individual returns and argue that adding crop suitability introduces an invalid instrument. They reproduce their main positive IV results using wild-cluster-bootstrap tests (§3.2, Table 1). This is an author robustness check, not independent replication.

**Source type/strength:** peer-reviewed counterargument and reanalysis. **Moderate** for the causal claim; **strong** evidence that the dispute remains live. Both sides concede inadequate precise evidence on the initial timing.

Edwards–Ogilvie subsequently answered in [*The Black Death and the Origin of the European Marriage Pattern*](https://sheilaghogilvie.com/wp-content/uploads/publications/Edwards-Ogilvie-2022-The-Black-Death-and-the-Origin-of-the-European-Marriage-Pattern.pdf), Oxford Economic and Social History WP **204**, December 2022 (`edwards2022.txt`). **Inference:** this rejoinder again challenges the model assumptions, later-period evidence and IV validity. **Type/strength:** working paper, moderate; not an independent third-party replication. Do not count these three papers as three independent findings.

## 2. The EMP: real regularity, disputed growth explanation and boundary

### Dennison–Ogilvie's larger demographic comparison

**Source:** Tracy Dennison and Sheilagh Ogilvie, “Does the European Marriage Pattern Explain Economic Growth?”, *Journal of Economic History* **74(3), 2014, 651–693**, [DOI 10.1017/S0022050714000564](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050714000564). Inspected [full text](https://sheilaghogilvie.com/wp-content/uploads/publications/Dennison-Ogilvie-2014.pdf), `dennison2014.txt`.

**Quote — p. 663, `dennison2014.txt:594–601`:**

> broadly consistent with Hajnal’s proposition of earlier female marriage, on average, in eastern and southern than in western and northern Europe, they also reveal societies with late (sometimes extremely late) female marriage in Slavic Europe (such as Slovenia and Bohemia), societies with early marriage in the zone west of the Hajnal line (such as parts of France), and highly significant differences inside countries

**Inference — what it disputes:** a monolithic boundary, an “extreme EMP” uniquely concentrated in England/the Netherlands, and the claim that later marriage necessarily produced growth. Their analysis covers **4,705 observations in 39 countries**, with different data sources and controls for century and study characteristics. England was not the most extreme late-marriage society; poorer Scandinavian and central-European economies often had later marriage. Some societies combined the EMP with serfdom and restrictions on women.

**Source type/strength:** peer-reviewed quantitative synthesis. **Strong** against a hard geographic dichotomy; **moderate** against causal growth theories, because ranking demographic levels is not a causal experiment and the observations are heterogeneous. It **partly confirms**, rather than abolishes, the broad northwestern contrast.

**Sentence consequence — inference:** “northwestern Europe” is better than “the West”, but still needs “many/common” rather than an implication of universal practice or uniform liberty. The constitution sentence does not claim economic growth, so criticism of growth alone is not a reason to remove its demographic example.

### Carmichael, de Pleijt, van Zanden and De Moor reply; Dennison–Ogilvie answer

**Source:** “The European Marriage Pattern and Its Measurement”, *JEH* **76(1), 2016, 196–204**, [DOI 10.1017/S0022050716000474](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050716000474). Publisher abstract inspected; full reply inaccessible in this search. Its longer 2015 WP **70** is documented [here](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ucg/wpaper/0070.html).

**Quote — [publisher abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0022050716000474), `reply2016.txt:494`:**

> Age of marriage is not a good scale for the degree to which countries were characterized by EMP. Rather, the economic effects of the EMP should be seen in the broader context of how marriage responds to changing economic circumstance.

**Inference — what it disputes:** Dennison–Ogilvie's interpretation of demographic levels. A system allowing couples to respond to opportunities need not always produce the highest marriage age: prosperity could allow earlier marriage. They emphasize underlying institutions and female agency, not simply a maximum marriage-age ranking.

**Source type/strength:** peer-reviewed reply, but **limited-to-moderate verification here** because only its abstract and WP abstract were inspected. It offers a substantive counterargument, not verification that “25 means free spouse choice”.

**Counter-reply:** Dennison–Ogilvie, “Institutions, Demography, and Economic Growth”, *JEH* **76(1), 2016, 205–217**, [DOI 10.1017/S0022050716000486](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050716000486); [full text](https://sheilaghogilvie.com/wp-content/uploads/publications/Dennison-Ogilvie-2016-Institutions-Demography-and-Economic-Growth.pdf), `dennison2016.txt:109–195`. **Inference:** they argue that agency indicators were not uniformly high inside EMP societies or low outside them, and that changing the EMP definition does not establish the claimed causal link. **Type/strength:** peer-reviewed reply, moderate; some extracted glyphs are corrupt, so I do not quote affected passages.

**Sentence consequence — inference:** neither side licenses equating late marriage with unrestricted choice. Evidence about consent, actual enforcement, property, wages and family power is needed separately.

### Eastern Europe: published criticism beyond the growth debate

**Source:** Mikołaj Szołtysek, “Spatial construction of European family and household systems: a promising path or a blind alley? An Eastern European perspective”, *Continuity and Change* **27(1), 2012, 11–52**, [DOI 10.1017/S0268416012000057](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0268416012000057).

**Quote — [publisher abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/continuity-and-change/article/abs/spatial-construction-of-european-family-and-household-systems-a-promising-path-or-a-blind-alley-an-eastern-european-perspective/0B49EA74F9206CDEC1B32C4C0A8FB684), `szoltysek2012.txt:479`:**

> The diversity of family forms and the rhythms of their development in historical Eastern Europe presented in this literature should finally free us from a simplistic view of the continent's familial history, and especially from the perspective implied by the notion of a ‘dividing line’.

**Inference — what it disputes:** boundary and the assumed homogeneity of Eastern Europe, including neglect of Eastern European scholarship. Later work is more than a conceptual protest: Szołtysek's *Rethinking East-Central Europe* (2015) uses over **26,000 peasant households**, about **156,000 people**, and finds region-specific systems, variation in leaving home, service and nuptiality. These quantities and findings are **indirectly verified** in Aurora Angeli's [2019 *Genus* scholarly review](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41118-018-0048-4), `szolreview.txt:102–124`, not from my inspection of the original monograph/data.

**Source type/strength:** peer-reviewed historical-demographic critique, abstract inspected; scholarly review of subsequent record-based research. **Moderate here**, reinforced by Dennison–Ogilvie's inspected full analysis. **Sentence consequence:** no rigid Hajnal line and no inference that non-kin work or later marriage belonged exclusively to the northwest.

## 3. Asia and China: separate marriage age from demographic agency

**Source:** James Lee and Wang Feng, “Malthusian Models and Chinese Realities: The Chinese Demographic System 1700–2000”, *Population and Development Review* **25(1), 1999, 33–65**, [DOI 10.1111/j.1728-4457.1999.00033.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1728-4457.1999.00033.x); expanded argument in *One Quarter of Humanity* (1999). Publisher access was blocked; abstract verified through Crossref, `cr_leeexact.json`.

**Quote — article abstract, DOI above:**

> high rates of female infanticide and abortion, high rates of bachelorhood, low marital fertility, and high rates of male and female adoption

**Inference — what it disputes:** treating early female marriage as proof of uncontrolled reproduction or a population with no adaptive demographic behaviour. Chinese restriction could occur through different mechanisms, including within marriage. This does **not** make infanticide an example of liberty, establish equal female agency, or imply that the Chinese female marriage-age estimate is wrong. Male bachelorhood also cannot be substituted for female non-marriage.

**Source type/strength:** peer-reviewed demographic research, **moderate verification here** from its abstract and its use in the inspected D&O discussion of Chinese fertility responses (`dennison2014.txt:1496–1499`); full Lee–Feng methods not inspected.

**Number audit — inference:** V&V's working paper explicitly reports **17.5**, citing Lee, Wang Feng and Ruan (2001), “Nuptiality Among the Qing Nobility: 1640–1900”. It separately reports Qing imperial women marrying at **15.5–19** in the seventeenth century, rising to **22 by 1840**: `.local/papers/prevalence_20261014/voth.txt:1070–1076,1532–1535`, [NBER full paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17314/w17314.pdf). I did **not** obtain the chapter to verify the early-twentieth-century denominator or method behind 17.5. The imperial-family figure is not a national substitute. England's **25** is from V&V Table 1, citing **Flinn (1981)**, not an original V&V census estimate (`voth.txt:1601–1612`).

**Sentence consequence:** the cross-century comparison is transparently labelled, but should not imply contemporaneous national contrasts or relative freedom. Keep it only as a reported, dated comparison with its original denominator checked; the recommended text omits it.

**Asian similarities, not proof of one global marriage regime:** Carmichael–van Zanden's 2015 [CGEH WP 67 abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ucg/wpaper/0067.html), `cgeh67_repec.txt:94`, explicitly discusses “the ‘similar’ marriage systems in Japan, Sumatra, Kerala, Sri Lanka and Myanmar”. **Inference:** even advocates of the EMP's importance recognize comparable institutional features outside Europe. **Type/strength:** scholarly working paper, abstract only; **limited** for precise marriage ages. I have not verified an Asian preindustrial mid-twenties national mean; do not turn this list into such a statistic. Eastern-European late marriage is better evidenced in this report.

## 4. Henrich: causal criticism and the limits of the measures

### An empirical alternative to the Church-origin explanation

**Source:** David le Bris and Victor Gay, “Distance to innovations, kinship intensity, and psychological traits”, *PLOS ONE* **18(1), 2023**, [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0279864](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0279864). [Full article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0279864) inspected, `lebris.txt`.

**Quote — introduction, `lebris.txt:262`:**

> In this perspective, the Church’s MFP cannot have been at the root of weak kinship in Western Europe. Rather, it might have only formalized—though potentially reinforced—practices that were already in place.

**Inference — what it disputes:** the historical starting condition and causal story in Schulz, Bahrami-Rad, Beauchamp and Henrich's 2019 *Science* paper ([DOI 10.1126/science.aau5141](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aau5141)), which underpins the book. Nuclear families, exogamy and weak kinship may predate the marriage-and-family programme. They reuse Schulz et al.'s data and introduce distance to proposed centres of family innovation. Distance better explains kinship measures in their specifications; Church exposure **still better explains psychological outcomes in Europe**, and they explicitly allow an additional Church effect (`lebris.txt:291–361`).

**Source type/strength:** peer-reviewed historical argument and independent-team observational reanalysis. **Moderate**: a useful challenge, not experimental identification of the alternative, and not independent new measurement of all outcomes. Their diffusion/ancestry assumptions and the original measures remain open to scrutiny.

**Sentence consequence:** retain descriptive early-modern evidence without teaching that Church rules or the Black Death demonstrably caused all these practices. The constitution sentence does not explicitly assert the Church mechanism; this criticism chiefly blocks adding it as settled explanation.

### Historical review: norms were uneven, and impersonal cooperation had boundaries

**Source:** historian Nicholas Guyatt, [*Guardian* review, 20 November 2020](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2020/nov/20/the-weirdest-people-in-the-world-review-a-theory-of-everything-study), full review inspected, `guardian.txt`.

**Quote — `guardian.txt:151`:**

> Modern historians will argue that cousin marriage increased across many European societies in the 17th and 18th centuries before it was stigmatised again in the 19th century.

**Inference — what it disputes:** a smooth Church → weaker kinship → universal openness story. Guyatt also points to uneven enforcement, Protestant/Catholic differences and Europe's imperial violence, which limits any interpretation of “impersonal prosociality” as generosity to everyone. **Source type/strength:** published review by a historian; **moderate** as a historical warning, **limited** as a quantitative refutation. His prominent cousin-marriage examples alone do not overturn population averages. D&O's full-text discussion of varying marriage-law enforcement provides firmer supporting context (`dennison2014.txt:1566–1597`).

**Sentence consequence:** no direct change to English marriage age; avoid reading routine non-kin transactions as general freedom or universal impartiality.

### Kinship intensity does not directly measure costly freedom-seeking

**Source:** Henrich's own qualifications, *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020), `/home/code/Documents/papers/tmp/The Weirdest People in the World - Joseph Henrich.md` (**H**).

**Quote — H:1580:**

> Consequently, the KII doesn’t necessarily capture what people were actually doing on the ground—their behavior.

**Quote — H:1582:**

> the KII rolls together several different aspects of kin-based institutions, so we can’t tell which features of intensive kinship are doing the psychological work.

**Inference — measurement criticism:** KII aggregates reported social norms, not observed willingness to pay for liberty. Its observations average around **1900**, while psychological measures are roughly a century later (H:1570). A nuclear household is not the absence of influential wider kin, and a correlation involving several customs cannot identify which one caused an outcome. Henrich supplies genetic-relatedness and persistence checks (H:1572,1580) and uses cousin-marriage rates separately: he does not simply ignore these limitations.

**Source type/strength:** scholarly synthesis with explicit author caveats. **Strong** evidence about what the index cannot directly establish; not a published finding that the index is wholly invalid. Le Bris–Gay challenge causal interpretation using these measures, not their every recorded value. **Sentence consequence:** don't use KII to fill the missing proof of free spouse choice.

## 5. London: the numerical range needs correction at its source

**Source chain:** H:3094,4833 → de la Croix, Doepke and Mokyr, *Clans, Guilds, and Markets* ([NBER WP 22131](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22131/w22131.pdf), inspected `clans.txt:488–495`; published *QJE*, 2018, [DOI 10.1093/qje/qjx026](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjx026)) → Tim Leunig, Chris Minns and Patrick Wallis, “Networks in the Premodern Economy: The Market for London Apprenticeships, 1600–1749”, *JEH* **71(2), 2011, 413–443**, [DOI 10.1017/S0022050711001586](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050711001586). Inspected their [2009 LSE full working-paper version](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/28686/1/dp0956.pdf), `leunig2009.txt`.

**Quote — original researchers, printed p. 7, `leunig2009.txt:430–434`:**

> However, there is little certainty that the multiplier of four that this would suggest is actually correct, as it relies on assumptions about the distance and strength of kin ties for which we have little evidence. Whether kinship rates are 7% or 28%, these rough approximations still indicate that kinship was not the main method of joining masters and apprentices.

**Inference — what this disputes:** the precision and denominator of Henrich's retelling, **not** the broad predominance of non-kin training. The records cover **118,000 apprentices, 42,000 masters and 65 London Companies**, principally **1600–1750** in the working version. Some large companies are absent. Recorded close relations are sparse; unusual matching surnames suggest under 7% related. Maternal and other different-name kin are missed; common-name matches can be unrelated. Multiplying by four gives the alternative 28%. **72 and 93 are complements of these assumptions**, not verified endpoints of an artisan-population distribution (`leunig2009.txt:303–325,378–440`).

The paper counts entering apprentices, not confirmed completion of training by every artisan. Company membership also imperfectly identifies occupation. Non-kin masters can still be obtained through parents' professional contacts. The skill-transmission paper explicitly notes parental negotiation of training contracts (`clans.txt:135–146`).

**Source type/strength:** primary record-based research, read in its working-paper version; **strong** for non-kin training being common, **moderate** for an exact kin percentage. This is an original-author measurement caveat, not a later independent replication. I found no verified published replication of the precise 72–93% range.

**Sentence consequence — inference:** change to “London apprenticeship records from 1600–1749 suggest most trained with nonrelatives”. If the number must remain, say **“an estimated 72–93% of sampled London apprentices”**, and explain its rough construction in the source comment. “Strangers” is stronger than “nonrelatives”: acquaintances and family contacts could be unrelated.

## Recommended corrected text — inference, not approved constitution wording

In early-modern northwestern Europe, many young people worked outside their natal families and women commonly married in their mid-twenties. Seventeenth-century English women first married at about 25. London apprenticeship records from 1600–1749 suggest most trained with nonrelatives. These patterns varied by region and social class; they do not by themselves prove free spouse choice or a general preference for freedom.
