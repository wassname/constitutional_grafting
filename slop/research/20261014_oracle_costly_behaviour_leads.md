## 15 more costly-behaviour evidence types

1. **Desertion rates in conscript armies** — soldiers face execution yet desert anyway (WWI French mutinies/desertions: Leonard Smith, *Between Mutiny and Obedience*, 1994; US Civil War desertion ~10%+). Costly exit-refusal to be governed without consent.

2. **Marriage-strike / cohabitation when marriage law is hostile** — couples cohabit illegitimately rather than accept ecclesiastical marriage rules; Scandinavian 19th-c. "servant marriage" delays, late-marriage demography (Hajnal line: John Hajnal 1965; Peter Laslett, *Household and Family in Past Time*, 1972). Costly: foregone fertility, legal stigma.

3. **Post-Reformation martyrdoms / recusancy fines** — English Catholics paid ruinous fines to keep faith; Anabaptists burned rather than recant (William Monter; *Martyrs Mirror*;Execution counts in Foxe's *Book of Martyrs*). Cost: property and life for freedom of belief.

4. **Jewish conversos reverting under Inquisition** — crypto-Judaism despite torture (Haim Beinart, *Records of the Trials of the Spanish Inquisition in Ciudad Real*, 1974–85; Cecil Roth). Ultimate costly belief persistence.

5. **Homesteading / frontier migration** — pioneers accept ~2% annual death risk for land ownership and self-rule (Frederick Jackson Turner data; *Homestead Act patent records*, US National Archives; mortality in Zebulon Pike-era frontier data). Costly autonomy-seeking.

6. **Marriage migration patterns to liberal jurisdictions** — couples cross borders today for marriage freedom (dataset: UN DESA marriage-migration tabulations; Spain 2005 same-sex marriage surge from non-resident couples). Cross-jurisdiction exit revealed by administrative records.

7. **Suicide rates under totalitarian regimes vs. democracies** (careful, contested; data from Soviet vital statistics, Emile Durkheim for baseline). Weak/noisy — flag it, but belief-suppression costs show in vital records.

8. **Willingness to pay for legal counsel / pro bono bar formation** — medieval and early-modern lawyers defending clients against the Crown at personal risk (e.g., *Provisions of Oxford* barons, 1258; John Fortescue, *De Laudibus Legum Anglie*). Also contempt cases where lawyers refuse judge orders.

9. **Tenant mobility in the wake of emancipation movements** — freed serfs' post-1861 Russian migration despite passport controls (Steven Hoch, *Serfdom and Social Control in Russia*, 1986; Bhadra/Emancipation labormobility studies). Costly mobility preference vs. landlord retention.

10. **Preindustrial migration responses to grain wage variation** — laborers move long distances for modest wage gains (e.g., 17th-c. English servant migration, Mildred Campbell, *The English Yeoman*; indentured servant contracts — accepting 4–7 years servitude for passage: Colonial Office records, *Virtual Jamestown* databases). Cost: years of life in bondage for freedom of movement.

11. **Strike waves under anti-union laws** — workers struck knowing they'd be imprisoned under criminal conspiracy doctrine (U.S. *In re Debs* 1894; the 8-hour movement, 1886 Haymarket). Costly collective voice vs. consent of governed.

12. **Persecution-flight of scientists/artists and differential productivity** — Einstein, von Neumann, Fermi left (records of émigré scientist lists, Norman Bentwich, *The Rescue and Achievement of Refugee Scholars*, 1953). Labor-market cost to origin societies; exit preference by elites.

13. **Peasant resistance to collectivization** — Soviet dekulakization, peasant slaughtering livestock rather than surrender property (Robert Conquest, *The Harvest of Sorrow*, 1986; Davies & Wheatcroft). Costly property/autonomy defense, at starvation risk.

14. **Land-title litigation duration** — peasants spend decades in courts to defend property rights rather than submit to seigneurial power (French *parlement* case rolls; Paul Freedman, *The Origins of Peasant Servitude in Medieval Catalonia*, 1991). Rule-of-law revealed by demand, cost paid in fees and delays.

15. **Homeownership and mobility trade-offs in revealed-preference data** — modern longitudinal datasets (NLSY, German SOEP) showing persistent geographic mobility despite high home-equity and social costs. Demographic continuity test for 50+ years.

## What would show these are NOT Western-specific

- Non-Western costly evidence with the same logic: pre-colonial Igbo and other noncentralized societies practicing consensus governance and exit (Audrey Richards on the Bemba; James C. Scott — already have — plus *Bohannan on Tiv* and Bohannan's *Justice and Judgment Among the Tiv*, 1957 — Tiv legal resistance to imposed norms).
- Runaway slaves in Brazil (*quilombos*, *Palmares*, documentation in *Reis & Gomes, Liberdade por um fio*, 2000) and the Caribbean (maroons — Jamaican treaty records). Same costly exit behavior outside Western polities.
- Chinese "self-strengthening" reform movements and May 4th 1919 protests (protest arrest records); Tiananmen 1989 casualty lists compiled from hospital records — costly speech seeking autonomy outside the West.
- Indian independent-council (*sabha/gram panchayat*) historical practice — village assemblies documented in Chola inscriptions (Kenneth Hall, *Trade and Statecraft in the Age of the Colas*, 1980).
- Japanese and Thai Buddhist laity resisting state religion control at cost (Tokugawa *fumi-e* apostasy refusals — the Kakure Kirishitan, records in *Stephen Turnbull*).
- These suggest these values are broadly human capacities, activated when a "West" suppresses them; "Western" labels them by polity origin, not behavior distribution. This cuts against an essentialist framing: many acts above are *against Western regimes*, suggesting Westernness ≠ actual behavior of Westerners generally, only of particular traditions/practices whose validity (Hajnal, Scott, Fortescue) supports them.

## What evidence would cut against the priors

- **Costly free-riding evidence**: if behavior is mostly about material incentive, not principle — e.g., mercenaries switching sides for pay; evidence that so-called desertion tracked famine/economics rather than governance objection.
- **Long-run cultural persistence of hierarchies Westerners claim to reject**: caste-like status persistence in nominally egalitarian societies; arranged-marriage persistence in Western diaspora across 50+ years (e.g., British Pakistani marriage pattern data) — suggests values are context-bound, not universal.
- **Survey-behaviour divergence**: if behavior only emerges when it's cheap or state-backed — e.g., free speech behavior only when sanctioned by elites; "rebel" acts cluster with state tolerance.
- **Anthropological countercases**: strongly hierarchical societies where exit is rare despite extreme cost (although absence may be coerced silence, not absence of preference — an identification problem worth noting explicitly).
- **Founding-era hypocrisy data**: contradiction between professed values and actual practice (slavery, women's coverture) suggests values were elite rhetoric, not actual held values — check: did women run away, sue for property rights? (They did: Mary Wollstonecraft-era suits, coverture litigation records in colonial courts.) This affects priors about *who* held the values.
- **Values are adoption-dependent, not intrinsic**: evidence that freedom behavior requires literacy, print, legal infrastructure — that without these institutions the "values" don't manifest — cuts against treating them as innate priors rather than learned civilizational constructs.

Key note for the constitution: separate "values are held" from "values are Western" from "values should be encoded" — these are distinct claims with distinct evidentiary bar.
