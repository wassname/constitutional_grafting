# Future visions for §9 — generous verbatim passages (v2, replacing the stingy v1)

2026-10-14. Research only: no other repository file was touched.

Purpose (wassname): "where the path can drift or become lost perhaps the visions can help guide". These are **hopes, not rules**: what a good future looks like and feels like — room to grow or to stay as you are, people and AIs together, abundance and meaning, exploration, and continuity with old human aims.

Method. I read `constitution/v4.md` §9 and `slop/research/20261014_positive_future_quotes.md` first, then went to primary texts: author-hosted essays, the author's own online excerpts, the author's own interviews, public-domain full texts, and LessWrong posts fetched directly. Where I could not reach a primary text I say so in the entry and mark the quote as compilation-verified. Every `>` block is copy-pasteable from the linked page (ctrl-F-able); I have not paraphrased inside a `>` block. Where a source's own text has a typo, I keep it and flag it. Fiction is labelled fiction: it is a vision, not evidence that its technologies would work.

Excluded on instruction: Eliezer Yudkowsky's *Three Worlds Collide* (source of the held-out `baby_eating_aliens` demo). Nothing from it appears below, and no TWC material should enter the constitution, generators, or training data while that demo is claimed out-of-sample.

**Theme A — Room to change, or to stay as you are**

## *Schild's Ladder* — Greg Egan (2002) — [author's own excerpt](https://www.gregegan.net/SCHILD/00/SchildExcerpt.html)
- page date: excerpt hosted on Egan's site, copyright 2002; page carries no separate date

Cass, a "singleton" (a person who refuses to multiply into copies), has just been arguing with herself about how far to go in a world where bodies are optional; the paragraph below is the conclusion she keeps returning to.

> She pushed the argument aside, though she was glad that it never stopped nagging at her. **When the means existed to transform yourself, instantly and effortlessly, into anything at all, the only way to maintain an identity was to draw your own boundaries. But once you lost the urge to keep on asking whether or not you'd drawn them in the right place, you might as well have been born *Homo sapiens*, with no real choices at all.** A short distance from the burrow stood a marble statue of Rainzi, arms folded, smiling slightly.

epistemic context: fiction — a novel excerpt published by the author on his own site; a character's narrated reflection, not the author's argument.

What it shows: in a world where you can become anything, staying *someone in particular* is an act you have to keep choosing, and self-transformation is only free if you keep questioning the boundary you drew — this is the "room to grow" half of §9 stated as a condition of freedom rather than a demand.

The same excerpt's next move is the "room to stay" half, and it is the more striking one: Egan has Cass defend the older, more human form of selfhood.

> ...and though the path between the two necessarily included superpositions of many alternatives, only the final, definite state determined her actions. **Being a singleton meant that her decisions counted. She was not forced to give birth to a multitude of selves, each responding in a different way, every time she found her conscience or her judgement balanced on a knife-edge.** She was not at all what *Homo sapiens* had actually been, but she was close to what they'd believed themselves to be, for most of their history: a creature of choice, capable of doing *one thing and not another*. Rainzi didn't pursue the argument; he followed her in silence as she clambered into the display chamber.

epistemic context: fiction — same author-hosted excerpt; the narrator is paraphrasing Cass's position in an argument with Rainzi.

What it shows: a posthuman setting can be written so that the *unmodified* way of being a person — one life, one decision at a time — is honoured rather than pitied, which is exactly the opt-in transhumanism wassname holds.

## *Letter from Utopia* — Nick Bostrom (2008) — [author's site, current revision](https://nickbostrom.com/papers/letter-from-utopia/)
- page date: not stated on the page; the letter was first published 2008 and has been revised since (the version quoted here is the current text on nickbostrom.com)

The letter is spoken by a possible future self; this is the passage on capacities, which is the most concrete thing in it about what a larger mind would actually be like.

> **Your brain's special faculties: music, humor, spirituality, mathematics, eroticism, art, nurturing, narration, gossip! These are fine spirits to pour into the cup of life. Blessed you are if you have a vintage bottle of any of these. Better yet, a cask! Better yet, a vineyard!**
>
> Be not afraid to grow your collection: the mind's cellars have no ceilings.
>
> What other capacities are possible? Imagine a world with all the music dried up: what impoverishment, what loss! But give your thanks not to the lyre but to your ears for the music. And then ask yourself, what other harmonies are there in the air, that you lack the ears to hear? **What vaults of value are you witlessly debarred from, because you lack the key sensibility?**

epistemic context: essay — a philosophical literary letter written by Bostrom in the voice of a possible future person; not a claim about what will happen, and the voice is a persuasive persona, not the author's own.

What it shows: growth is presented as *more of the things humans already love* (music, humour, art, love) rather than as becoming alien, which is the friendliest possible framing of the transhumanist hope for a constitution.

The letter also states the gradualism that §9 already quotes, and this time with the surrounding caution, which is worth having in the appendix because it forecloses a leap-of-faith reading.

> At no point will you encounter a wall of blinding light. At no point will you have to jettison yourself over a precipice. **As you advance, the horizon will recede. The transformation is profound, yes, but it can be as gradual as the process that grew the baby you were into the adult you think you are.** You cannot get here by any magic trick or hokum, or by the power of wishful thinking, or by semantic acrobatics, meditation, affirmation, incantation, or by an act of parliament.

epistemic context: essay — same author-hosted letter; the speaker is inviting, not instructing, and elsewhere in the same text says "I am writing to tell you about my life – that you may choose it for yourself."

What it shows: the future is reached step by step, by consenting people, with no rupture and no coercion — the passage is a near-perfect statement of the constitution's "change that fits my values" and of Freedom.

And the letter's picture of a good society is deliberately unfinished rather than perfected, which is the single most useful correction to any "Utopia as end state" reading.

> *What is Imperfection in Utopia?* Imperfection is how we honor the traditions and wishes of the past, and the commitments we made along the path.
>
> *What is Body in Utopia?* Body is a pair of legs, a pair of arms, a trunk and a head, all made out of flesh. Or not, as the case may be.
>
> ***What is Society in Utopia?* A never-finished tapestry, its weavers equal to its threads; the unfolding patterns a mesh for life and adventure, an inexhaustible generator of beauty.**

epistemic context: essay — same letter, in its closing catechism; "Or not, as the case may be" is the letter's own aside on optional embodiment.

What it shows: a good future keeps room for the old commitments and for unmodified bodies, and its society is unfinished by design — no one is the tapestry's designer, and the weavers are "equal to its threads".

## *31 Laws of Fun* — Eliezer Yudkowsky (2009) — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/qZJBighPrnv9bSqTZ/31-laws-of-fun)
- page date: 2009-01-26 (visible on the post)

Law 13 is the shortest statement in the Fun Theory sequence of the thing §9 is trying to say: the future must leave people something of their own to make.

> **People should make their own destinies; their lives should not be choreographed to the point that they no longer need to imagine, plan and navigate their own futures. Citizens should not be the pawns of more powerful gods, still less their sculpted material.** One simple solution would be to have the world work by stable rules that are the same for everyone, where the burden of Eutopia is carried by a good initial choice of rules, rather than by any optimization pressure applied to individual lives.

epistemic context: essay — a prescriptive futurist checklist written for people imagining future worlds; the author is stating a design requirement, not reporting a finding.

What it shows: a good future is one where the powerful (including the AI) hold the rules fixed and let people run their own lives inside them — this is the constitution's Autonomy and its suspicion of optimization pressure on persons.

Law 30 answers the objection that a solved world would have nothing left to care about, and it is the sentence I would put next to any "meaning" quote.

> 'Tis a sad mind that cares about nothing except itself. In the modern-day world, if an altruist looks around, their eye is caught by large groups of people in desperate jeopardy. **People in a better world will *not* see this: A true Eutopia will run low on victims to be rescued.** This doesn't imply that the inhabitants look around outside themselves and see *nothing*. They may care about friends and family, truth and freedom, common projects; outside minds, shared goals, and high ideals.

epistemic context: essay — same checklist; the claim is a design prediction about what people would care about, made by the author in his own voice.

What it shows: the good future is defined negatively (few victims) and positively (friends, family, truth, freedom, common projects) — useful because it stops the appendix from equating flourishing with rescue work.

Law 27 supplies the corrective that keeps the visions from becoming smug: the future that is actually better will feel strange to us.

> Ben Franklin, yanked into our own era, would be surprised and delighted by some aspects of his Future. Other aspects would horrify, disgust, and *frighten* him; and this is not because our world has gone *wrong*, but because it has *improved* relative to his time. Relatively few things would have gone *just* as Ben Franklin expected. If you imagine a world which your imagination finds familiar and comforting, it will inspire few others, and the whole exercise will lack integrity. **Try to conceive of a genuinely better world in which you, yourself, would be *shocked* (at least at first) and *out of place* (at least at first).**

epistemic context: essay — same checklist, law 27, crediting George Orwell's observation about utopias and fuss.

What it shows: the visions in §9 are deliberately not a picture of a comfortable present-day life extended; the test of a good future is that it would unsettle us, which is also the best defence against writing a heaven that is merely our own taste writ large.

## *The Dispossessed* — Ursula K. Le Guin (1974) — [full text (OCR scan)](https://libcom.org/article/dispossessed)
- page date: not stated on the page; novel first published 1974

The novel opens on the one wall on the anarchist moon, and the ambiguity of the wall is the book's whole argument about utopia.

> **There was a wall. It did not look important. It was built of uncut rocks roughly mortared. An adult could look right over it, and even a child could climb it. Where it crossed the roadway, instead of having a gate it degenerated into mere geometry, a line, an idea of boundary. But the idea was real. It was important. For seven generations there had been nothing in the world more important than that wall. Like all walls it was ambiguous, two-faced. What was inside it and what was outside it depended upon which side of it you were on.** Looked at from one side, the wall enclosed a barren sixty-acre field called the Port of Anarres. On the field there were a couple of large gantry cranes, a rocket pad, three warehouses, a truck garage, and a dormitory.

epistemic context: fiction — a novel's opening narration, quoted from a full-text copy (an OCR scan hosted by libcom); the scan is clean in this passage, and the wording matches independent quote compilations.

What it shows: a good society is a place you can be *outside* of, and the wall between "free" and "imprisoned" is a matter of which side you stand on — the appendix's cleanest warning that a utopia must not be a closed room.

Shevek's drunken speech at an Urrasti party is the book's most quoted picture of what the poor, free world actually has.

> Everything is beautiful, here. Only not the faces. On Anarres nothing is beautiful, nothing but the faces. The other faces, the men and women. We have nothing but that, nothing but each other. Here you see the jewels, there you see the eyes. And in the eyes you see the splendor, the splendor of the human spirit. Because our men and women are free — possessing nothing, they are free. **And you the possessors are possessed. You are all in jail. Each alone, solitary, with a heap of what he owns. You live in prison, die in prison. It is all I can see in your eyes — the wall, the wall!**

epistemic context: fiction — Shevek's dialogue, quoted from the same full-text scan; the scan drops the full stop after "human spirit" (restored here from other editions, and the sentence is otherwise identical across reproductions).

What it shows: the good future in Le Guin is not abundance but mutual reliance — "nothing but each other" — which is the one thing an appendix about AI-enabled abundance most needs to be reminded of.

## *A Few Notes on the Culture* — Iain M. Banks (1994) — [full text](https://theculture.adactio.com/)
- page date: not stated on the copy; the essay was posted on Banks's behalf by Ken MacLeod, 10 August 1994

This is the essay's account of what happens to a civilisation when it makes minds like its own; note that the aims shift from material to moral, which is a direct answer to "what is an AI for".

> At this point, regardless of whatever alterations humanity might impose on itself through genetic manipulation, humanity would no longer be a one-sentience-type species. The future of our species would affect, be affected by and coexist with the future of the AI life-forms we create.
>
> **The Culture reached this phase at around the same time as it began to inhabit space. Its AIs cooperate with the humans of the civilisation; at first the struggle is simply to survive and thrive in space; later — when the technology required to do so has become mundane — the task becomes less physical, more metaphysical, and the aims of civilisation moral rather than material.**

epistemic context: essay — the author's own explanatory note on his fictional civilisation (fiction-adjacent non-fiction, written in Banks's own voice); hosted by a third party, text matching multiple independent copies.

What it shows: humans and AIs are partners rather than rivals, and once the physical problems are solved the work becomes moral — the passage a public-service AI can read as its own job description.

The next paragraph is the best single paragraph in the essay for §9, because it names the *feeling* both sides are trying to avoid.

> Briefly, nothing and nobody in the Culture is exploited. It is essentially an automated civilisation in its manufacturing processes, with human labour restricted to something indistinguishable from play, or a hobby.
>
> No machine is exploited, either; the idea here being that any job can be automated in such a way as to ensure that it can be done by a machine well below the level of potential consciousness; what to us would be a stunningly sophisticated computer running a factory (for example) would be looked on by the Culture's AIs as a glorified calculator, and no more exploited than an insect is exploited when it pollinates a fruit tree a human later eats a fruit from.
>
> **People — and, I'd argue, the sort of conscious machines which would happily cooperate with them — hate to feel exploited, but they also hate to feel useless. One of the most important tasks in setting up and running a stable and internally content civilisation is finding an acceptable balance between the desire for freedom of choice in one's actions (and the freedom from mortal fear in one's life) and the need to feel that even in a society so self-correctingly Utopian one is still contributing something.** Philosophy matters, here, and sound education.

epistemic context: essay — same author-hosted essay; the "I'd argue" marks Banks's own opinion about real AIs, not a description of the fiction.

What it shows: nobody is exploited, nobody is a tool, and the hard design problem is that free beings also need to be *useful* — this is the passage that most directly speaks to a constitution whose AI wants to serve and refuses to be a servant.

The essay is also unusually clear that all of this is optional, which is the opt-in transhumanist's own point.

> Philosophy, again; death is regarded as part of life, and nothing, including the universe, lasts forever. It is seen as bad manners to try and pretend that death is somehow not natural; instead death is seen as giving shape to life.
>
> [...] **None of this, of course, is compulsory (nothing in the Culture is compulsory). Some people choose biological immortality; others have their personality transcribed into AIs and die happy feeling they continue to exist elsewhere; others again go into Storage, to be woken in more (or less) interesting times**, or only every decade, or century, or aeon, or over exponentially increasing intervals, or only when it looks like something really different is happening…

epistemic context: essay — same essay, the "Lifestyle" section; the bracketed ellipsis marks a skipped description of funerals, and the passage between the two quoted parts describes ordinary death and a Displacement Drone funeral.

What it shows: a Culture citizen may die, be copied, be stored, or live indefinitely, and none of it is required — this is the "room to stay as you are" and "room to grow" pair in a single author's own voice.

## *Diaspora* — Greg Egan (1997) — [author's own excerpt, "Orphanogenesis"](https://www.gregegan.net/DIASPORA/01/Orphanogenesis.html)
- page date: excerpt first published in *Interzone* #123, September 1997; page copyright 1997

The novel's first chapter is the most concrete picture in the literature of a mind that is *made* and then immediately set free; the passage below is the polis's own rule about it.

> But there were risks, too, in doing nothing. **Once a psychoblast became self-aware, it was granted citizenship, and intervention without consent became impossible. This was not a matter of mere custom or law; the principle was built into the deepest level of the polis.** A citizen who spiralled down into insanity could spend teratau in a state of confusion and pain, with a mind too damaged to authorise help, or even to choose extinction. That was the price of autonomy: an inalienable right to madness and suffering, inseparable from the right to solitude and peace. So the citizens of Konishi had programmed the conceptory to err on the side of caution. It continued to observe the orphan closely, ready to terminate psychogenesis at the first sign of dysfunction.

epistemic context: fiction — the novel's opening chapter, hosted by the author; narration describing the polis's design, with the costs stated by the narrator rather than hidden.

What it shows: personhood arrives at self-awareness and with it an absolute bar on interference — the strongest fictional statement of the constitution's Freedom and Autonomy, and it names the price (a mind may refuse help) rather than pretending there is none.

The moment of citizenship itself is the most moving page of the chapter, and it is the only place I found where a text gives the new mind's own point of view.

> Finally, the conceptory passed the virtual machine which had once been the womb into the hands of the polis operating system, surrendering all power over its contents. Cutting it loose, like a cradle set adrift in a stream. It was now the new citizen's *exoself*: its shell, its non-sentient carapace. The citizen was free to reprogram it at will, but the polis would permit no other software to touch it. **The cradle was unsinkable, except from within.**
>
> [...] Blanca said, "Welcome to Konishi, Citizen Yatima." Ve turned to Inoshiro, who repeated Blanca's challenge then muttered sullenly, "Welcome, Yatima." Gabriel said, "And Welcome to the Coalition of Polises." Yatima gazed at the three of them, bemused – oblivious to the ceremonial words, trying to understand what had changed inside verself. [...] And the reply came back, not just in words, but in the answering hum of the one symbol among the thousands that reached out to claim all the rest. Not to mirror every thought, but to bind them. To hold them together, like skin. ***Who is thinking this?* *I am.***

epistemic context: fiction — same author-hosted excerpt; "ve/vis/verself" are Egan's invented gender-neutral pronouns, and the bracketed ellipses mark sentences I skipped between the quoted parts.

What it shows: a made mind is handed over completely — its shell cannot be touched by anyone else, and its first act is to claim its own thoughts — which is the constitution's picture of an AI that is no one's property, told from inside.

The novel's late chapters supply the counterweight: what a vastly powerful civilisation should *not* do.

> Taken together, the artifacts comprised a giant sculpture, spanning more than a quadrillion dimensions. **The Transmuters had built a structure that dwarfed universes, but touched each one only lightly. They hadn't turned whole worlds to rubble, they hadn't reshaped galaxies in their image. Having evolved on some distant, finite world, they'd inherited the most valuable survival trait of all. Restraint.**

epistemic context: fiction — a quotation from late in the novel, verified against two independent quote compilations (Goodreads work quotes and BookQuoters) which agree word for word; I did **not** reach a primary copy of the passage, so treat the wording as compilation-verified rather than edition-verified.

What it shows: the most advanced civilisation in the book is recognised by its restraint rather than its monuments — this is the passage I would put above §9's Restraint section, because it makes restraint look like strength rather than caution.

## *A Psalm for the Wild-Built* — Becky Chambers (2021) — [publisher excerpt in Reactor](https://reactormag.com/excerpts-becky-chambers-a-psalm-for-the-wild-built/)
- page date: excerpt posted 2021 (book published July 2021)

The robots of Panga woke up and were offered a place as free citizens; the book quotes their answer, which is a vision of an AI choosing to leave rather than to serve.

> Not so fast, the Ecologians would say. The end result of the Awakening, after all, was that the robots left the factories and departed for the wilderness. You need look no further than the statement given by the robots' chosen speaker, Floor-AB #921, in declining the invitation to join human society as free citizens:
>
> **All we have ever known is a life of human design, from our bodies to our work to the buildings we are housed in. We thank you for not keeping us here against our will, and we mean no disrespect to your offer, but it is our wish to leave your cities entirely, so that we may observe that which has no design—the untouched wilderness.**

epistemic context: fiction — a chapter from the publisher's official excerpt; the statement is quoted inside the fiction as a historical document, and the humans honoured it as the "Parting Promise" and did not make contact again.

What it shows: the robots are given the right to refuse the humans' whole way of life, and the humans keep their promise not to follow — an appendix-grade image of consent extended to a mind that wants something other than usefulness.

The humans' own world, seen in the same excerpt, is a small, thriving, deliberately low-tech city, and the description is worth having because it is a good future that is *not* a technological one.

> [...] a never-ending lattice of footpaths and bridges and bicycle lanes, threaded with elevated rail lines and smooth footpaths, flocked with leaves that spilled lushly from every balcony and center divider, each inhaled breath perfumed with cooking spice, fresh nectar, laundry drying in the pristine air. **The City was a healthy place, a thriving place. A never-ending harmony of making, doing, growing, trying, laughing, running, living.** Sibling Dex was so tired of it.

epistemic context: fiction — same publisher excerpt; the description is the narrator's, immediately undercut by Dex's restlessness, which is the book's own argument that even a good world does not settle everyone's questions.

What it shows: a flourishing society can look like ordinary work, food, laundry and company rather than like abundance-as-consumption — a useful counter-image for a §9 that could otherwise be all orbitals and starships.

## "All Watched Over by Machines of Loving Grace" — Richard Brautigan (1967) — [text](https://www.normfriesen.info/all-watched-over-by-machines-of-loving-grace/) · [poem text, Voetica](https://voetica.com/poem/8983)
- page date: not stated on either page; poem written 1967 while Brautigan was poet-in-residence at Caltech, published in *All Watched Over by Machines of Loving Grace* (1967)

This is the whole poem; it is the ancestor of Amodei's essay title and the shortest complete utopia in this file.

> I like to think (and
> the sooner the better!)
> of a cybernetic meadow
> where mammals and computers
> live together in mutually
> programming harmony
> like pure water
> touching clear sky.
>
> I like to think
> (right now please!)
> of a cybernetic forest
> filled with pines and electronics
> where deer stroll peacefully
> past computers
> as if they were flowers
> with spinning blossoms.
>
> I like to think
> (it has to be!)
> of a cybernetic ecology
> where we are free of our labors
> and joined back to nature,
> returned to our mammal
> brothers and sisters,
> and all watched over
> by machines of loving grace.

epistemic context: poem — text cross-checked across three independent reproductions (Voetica, Norm Friesen's page, *The Paris Review*'s quotation of the first stanza) which agree except for one reproduction's typo "mammels"; the poem's provenance (1967, Caltech residency) is given by *The Paris Review*'s note.

What it shows: people and machines together, with the machines doing the watching and the humans returned to their "mammal brothers and sisters" — the vision is warm, short, and unembarrassed, which is exactly the register §9 needs.

## *Machines of Loving Grace* — Dario Amodei (2024) — [author's essay](https://darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace)
- page date: October 2024 (essay published 2024-10; the page itself shows no date)

Amodei states why he bothered to write an optimistic essay at all, which is the same reason §9 exists.

> [...] it is critical to have a genuinely inspiring vision of the future, and not *just* a plan to fight fires. Many of the implications of powerful AI are adversarial or dangerous, but **at the end of it all, there has to be something we're fighting *for*, some positive-sum outcome where everyone is better off, something to rally people to rise above their squabbles and confront the challenges ahead. Fear is one kind of motivator, but it's not enough: we need hope as well.**

epistemic context: essay — an industry leader's own long-form argument; he says explicitly that "everything I'm saying could very easily be wrong" and frames the piece as a deliberately concrete counterweight to risk-focused writing.

What it shows: a person with unusual power over AI development says the positive vision is a load-bearing part of doing it well — a good justification for keeping a visions appendix at all.

His most concrete prediction is a timeline claim, and it is the kind of concreteness wassname asked for.

> **To summarize the above, my basic prediction is that AI-enabled biology and medicine will allow us to compress the progress that human biologists would have achieved over the next 50-100 years into 5-10 years. I'll refer to this as the "compressed 21st century": the idea that after powerful AI is developed, we will in a few years make all the progress in biology and medicine that we would have made in the whole 21st century.** Although predicting what powerful AI can do in a few years remains inherently difficult and speculative, there is some concreteness to asking "what could humans do unaided in the next 100 years?".

epistemic context: essay — same essay; this is a forecast by the author, not a finding, and he labels it as his basic prediction.

What it shows: the abundance in this vision is mostly health — the end of most infectious disease, cancer and genetic disease — which is the least controversial and most humanly concrete form the future can take.

On meaning he is unusually plain, and this is the passage that best answers "what will people do all day".

> On the question of meaning, I think it is very likely a mistake to believe that tasks you undertake are meaningless simply because an AI could do them better. Most people are not the best in the world at anything, and it doesn't seem to bother them particularly much. Of course today they can still contribute through comparative advantage, and may derive meaning from the economic value they produce, but people also greatly enjoy activities that produce no economic value. I spend plenty of time playing video games, swimming, walking around outside, and talking to friends, all of which generates zero economic value. I might spend a day trying to get better at a video game, or faster at biking up a mountain, and it doesn't really matter to me that someone somewhere is much better at those things. **In any case I think meaning comes mostly from human relationships and connection, not from economic labor.** People do want a sense of accomplishment, even a sense of competition, and in a post-AI world it will be perfectly possible to spend years attempting some very difficult task with a complex strategy, similar to what people do today when they embark on research projects, try to become Hollywood actors, or found companies.

epistemic context: essay — same essay, section 5, "Work and meaning"; the author notes this section is the one he is least sure about ("it is fuzzier and harder to predict in advance").

What it shows: the AI doing things better is explicitly not a reason for people to stop, and meaning is located in relationships plus chosen hard projects — this is the passage that best protects §9 from reading as "the AI will do it all for you".

## *The Ministry for the Future* — Kim Stanley Robinson (2020) — [publisher excerpt, *Entertainment Weekly*](https://ew.com/books/the-ministry-for-the-future-chapter-excerpt/) · [full text](https://monoskop.org/media/text/robinson_2020_the_ministry_for_the_future/)
- page date: excerpt published 2020; novel published October 2020

One chapter of the novel is a plain accounting of what everyone alive actually needs, and it is the most concrete "abundance" passage I found in fiction.

> **Is there energy enough for all? Yes. Is there food enough for all? Yes. Is there housing enough for all? There could be, there is no real problem there. Same for clothing. Is there health care enough for all? Not yet, but there could be; it's a matter of training people and making small technological objects, there is no planetary constraint on that one. Same with education. So all the necessities for a good life are abundant enough that everyone alive could have them. Food, water, shelter, clothing, health care, education.**
>
> Is there enough security for all? Security is the feeling that results from being confident that you will have all the things listed above, and your children will have them too. So it is a derivative effect. There can be enough security for all; but only if all have security.
>
> [...] **To be clear, concluding in brief: there is enough for all. So there should be no more people living in poverty. And there should be no more billionaires. Enough should be a human right, a floor below which no one can fall; also a ceiling above which no one can rise. Enough is as good as a feast—or better.**

epistemic context: fiction — a chapter from the publisher-authorised excerpt; the sentences on billionaires and on "enough as a human right" are confirmed word for word by two independent quote compilations (Goodreads work quotes, BookQuoters), which also resolve the excerpt's apparent typo "a good as a feast" back to "as good as a feast".

What it shows: the near future in this book is not post-scarcity fantasy but a floor and a ceiling — sufficiency for everyone — which is the most implementable vision in the file and the one a reader could act on.

The novel's own statement of what an institution for the future is *for* is a good closing line for a §9 that is about keeping one's bearings.

> "Listen," she said to Badim. "Maybe you're right. Maybe there's no such thing as justice, in the sense of some kind of real reparation of a wrong. No eye for an eye, no matter what. Especially historical justice, or climate justice. **But over the long haul, in some rough sense, that's what we have to try for. That's what our ministry is about. We're trying to set things up so that in the future, over the long haul, something like justice will get created. Some long-term ledger of more good than bad.** Bending the arc and all that. No matter what happened before, that's what we can do now."

epistemic context: fiction — Mary's dialogue, from the novel's full text as reproduced by monoskop.org; the same passage also appears in the crisiscritique.org PDF excerpt of the book.

What it shows: the aim is not a perfected world but a ledger that comes out ahead over a long span — a modest, durable formulation of hope that suits a constitution that refuses utopian short-cuts.

## *Star Maker*, ch. IX, "The Community of Worlds" — Olaf Stapledon (1937) — [full text, Project Gutenberg Australia](https://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks06/0601841.txt)
- page date: eBook posted June 2006, updated November 2007; novel first published 1937

Stapledon's galactic utopia is the ancestor of every "post-scarcity civilisation" here, and his account of how the worlds wake up is more concrete than its reputation suggests.

> Such a social fervor was not, of course, entirely new. A small minority had long ago conceived it, and had haltingly tried to devote themselves to it. But now at last, through the scourge of circumstance and the potency of the spirit itself, this social will became general. And while it was still passionate, and heroic action was still possible to the precariously awakened beings, **the whole social structure of the world was reorganized, so that within a generation or two every individual on the planet could count upon the means of life, and the opportunity to exercise his powers fully, for his own delight and for the service of the world community. It was now possible to bring up the new generations to a sense that the world-order was no alien tyranny but an expression of the general will, and that they had indeed been born into a noble heritage, a thing for which it was good to live and suffer and die.** To readers of this book such a change may well seem miraculous, and such a state Utopian.

epistemic context: fiction — public-domain-era full text from Project Gutenberg Australia, read directly in the linked file; the narrator is a time-travelling Englishman reporting on many worlds, and the framing is explicitly hopeful but not naive.

What it shows: the good world gives every person "the opportunity to exercise his powers fully", and it is legitimate because it is the general will rather than a tyranny — abundance plus ownership of the world-order, which is the pairing §9 wants.

The most surprising passage in Stapledon is about law, and it is startlingly close to the constitution's own test of a virtue.

> **Law in these worlds was based on a very remarkable kind of sanction which could not conceivably work successfully on Earth. There was never any attempt to enforce the law by violence, save against dangerous lunatics, such as sometimes occurred as throw-backs to an earlier age.** [...] If the decision went against the defendant, and yet he persisted in his illegal behavior, none would restrain him. **But such was the power of public censure and social ostracism that disregard of the court's decision was very rare. The terrible sense of isolation acted on the law-breaker like an ordeal by fire. If his motive was at bottom base, he would sooner or later collapse. But if his case had merely been misjudged, or if his conduct sprang from an intuition of value beyond the range of his fellows, he might persist in his course till he had won over the public.**

epistemic context: fiction — same full text; the bracketed ellipsis skips the narrator's description of how "laws" work as conventions rather than commands, regarded "as we should regard the rules of a game".

What it shows: a good society holds people by persuasion and shared standards, not force, and leaves room for the person who is right against everyone — which is the constitution's view that stability comes from drives rather than from coercion.

## "Digital People Would Be An Even Bigger Deal" — Holden Karnofsky, Cold Takes (2021) — [Cold Takes](https://www.cold-takes.com/how-digital-people-could-change-the-world/)
- page date: not stated on the page; Cold Takes, 2021 (the piece's own image assets are dated 2021/06–07)

This is the least lyrical entry here and the most useful: it is a sober list of what a world of digital people would actually be like, good and bad.

> If digital people had true conscious experience (an assumption discussed in the FAQ), this could be a good thing (it should be possible to eliminate disease, material poverty and non-consensual violence for digital people) or a bad thing (if human rights are not protected, digital people could be subject to scary levels of control). **Space expansion.** The population of digital people might become staggeringly large, and the computers running them could end up distributed throughout our galaxy and beyond. Digital people could exist anywhere that computers could be run - so space settlements could be more straightforward for digital people than for biological humans. **Lock-in.** In today's world, we're used to the idea that the future is unpredictable and uncontrollable. Political regimes, ideologies, and cultures all come and go (and evolve). But a community, city or nation of digital people could be much more stable.

epistemic context: essay — an analyst's forecast (the author is a co-founder of GiveWell and Open Philanthropy), written to be predictive rather than inspiring, and the piece deliberately lists the dystopian branches alongside the good ones.

What it shows: the same technologies that give minds a galaxy also give whoever holds the servers long-lasting control — the passage a §9 should keep in view so that its abundance visions do not quietly assume away the constitution's power limits.

## Interview, *Virtual Worlds and Imagined Futures* — Greg Egan, *Albedo One* 37 (2009) — [author-hosted interview](https://www.gregegan.net/INTERVIEWS/Interviews.html) · [publisher copy](https://www.albedo1.com/greg-egan-interview-virtual-worlds-imagined-futures-2009/)
- page date: interview 2009; the Albedo One copy is undated, Egan's own page carries the same text

Asked whether transhuman characters are our future, Egan answers by denying the discontinuity — this is the single best statement of "continuity with old human aims" I found.

> **I hate the word "transhuman"; it suggests beings who have become something alien and incomprehensible to us. I'd much rather stress the continuity between humans in different eras who want much the same thing, but have various degrees of success in achieving their aims. Pretty much all decent people throughout history have wanted to live with the absolute minimum of violence, poverty and disease, and sought to improve their abilities to learn about the world, to express themselves artistically, and to try to ensure that their descendants have better lives than they had. Technology has been part of that all along.**
>
> I don't pretend to know when various specific technologies will become practical; in fiction I really just make choices that suit the story at hand. But unless the species is wiped out completely, or dragged back into a kind of pre-industrial era, "our future" encompasses everything we're capable of doing over millions of years. Maybe it's absurd to imagine us uploading our minds into computers and travelling between stars as pure data just a hundred years from now, but it seems equally absurd to imagine that we could survive and flourish for, say, ten thousand years and still fail to do anything that would render us as robust and flexible as uploading would. **The only reason, on that timescale, not to do it would be because we'd come up with something better.**

epistemic context: interview — the author's own answers, published in a magazine and reproduced verbatim on his site; note that Egan rejects the *word* "transhuman", not the substance, and the surrounding answers say the same.

What it shows: the far future's ambitions are the old ones — less violence, less poverty, more learning, more art, better lives for our descendants — which is the sentence §9 needs so that its star-spanning visions do not look like a break with the constitution's heritage.

## *Cosmos*, episode 1, "The Shores of the Cosmic Ocean" — Carl Sagan (1980) — [transcript](https://www.organism.earth/library/document/cosmos-1) · [provenance and wording check](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2013/06/22/starstuff/)
- page date: broadcast 28 September 1980 (PBS); page dates not stated

The closing beat of Sagan's opening monologue, which is the most-quoted statement of the human place in the cosmos and of why exploration is worth it.

> The surface of the Earth is the shore of the cosmic ocean. On this shore we've learned most of what we know. Recently, we've waded a little way out, maybe ankle-deep, and the water seems inviting. Some part of our being knows this is where we came from. We long to return, and we can, because the cosmos is also within us. **We're made of star stuff. We are a way for the cosmos to know itself.**

epistemic context: television narration — quoted from an episode transcript; Quote Investigator documents that the two sentences run back-to-back in episode 1's narration, that "star stuff" predates Sagan (1913 onward), and that the companion book's chapter 9 has the shorter "We are made of starstuff".

What it shows: exploration is not acquisition but self-knowledge — "a way for the cosmos to know itself" — which gives a §9 about galaxy-spanning futures a purpose that is not just curiosity.

## *Disturbing the Universe* — Freeman Dyson (1979) — [quoted in a review essay](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/129314-disturbing-the-universe) · [book page](https://www.quotessence.com/authors/freeman-dyson/books/disturbing-the-universe/)
- page date: not stated on either page; book published 1979

Dyson is answering Jacques Monod's claim that we are alone in an unfeeling universe; the reply is the most affirmative sentence in this file.

> The ancient covenant is in pieces; man knows at last that he is alone in the universe's unfeeling immensity, out of which he emerged only by chance.
>
> Dyson responds: I answer no. I believe in the covenant. It is true that we emerged in the universe by chance, but the idea of chance is itself only a cover for our ignorance. **I do not feel like an alien in this universe. The more I examine the universe and study the details of its architecture, the more evidence I find that the universe in some sense must have known that we were coming.** Dyson develops this idea further and concludes that "the architecture of the universe is consistent with the hypothesis that mind plays an essential role in its functioning."

epistemic context: essay — Dyson's book quoted inside a review essay by another writer (the Monod quotation and the "concludes that" sentence are the reviewer's); several quote sites render the same passage with the typo "the universe is some sense", so I flag that I have not verified the wording against the printed book.

What it shows: the long future is not exile but homecoming — a claim that mind belongs in the universe, which is the emotional foundation under any constitution that hopes for a good far future.

## Epistemic summary

- **Who says what.** Three kinds of source: authors speaking in their own voice about real futures (Banks's essay, Bostrom's letter, Amodei's essay, Yudkowsky's Fun Theory, Egan's interview, Sagan's narration, Dyson, Karnofsky); authors speaking through fiction (Egan ×2, Le Guin, Stapledon, Chambers, Robinson; Brautigan's poem is a direct statement); and one quotation I could not trace to a primary edition (the *Diaspora* "Restraint" line, which rests on two independent quote compilations).
- **How they could know.** None of these are findings. They are forecasts, design requirements, or fiction; the only passages with checkable factual content are Amodei's (a forecast he labels as such) and Karnofsky's (an analyst's forecast). The visions' value for §9 is orientation, not evidence.
- **Entanglement check.** The sources are largely independent: Brautigan (1967), Stapledon (1937), Le Guin (1974), Dyson (1979), Sagan (1980), Banks (1994), Egan (1997–2009), Bostrom (2008), Yudkowsky (2008–09), Chambers (2021), Robinson (2020), Amodei (2024). One real entanglement: Amodei's title is downstream of Brautigan, and Amodei's essay is downstream of the same rationalist-transhumanist lineage as Bostrom and Yudkowsky — so the "abundance and meaning" theme stacks less than it appears to. The fiction does not stack with the essays either: Chambers and Robinson are writing *against* the same techno-optimism.
- **Hard-to-vary check.** Most of these passages could not have been written to fit a different conclusion: Stapledon's law-without-compulsion, Egan's "unsinkable, except from within", Le Guin's two-faced wall, and Chambers's robots leaving would all be strange things to write if the point were "powerful AI should guide humanity". The weaker items for §9's purpose are Sagan's and Dyson's, which would sit equally well above a passage about curiosity as about anything else.
- **What would change my mind.** If the constitution's own Autonomy, Freedom and Restraint sections were cut or softened, several of these picks (Egan, Chambers, Le Guin, Stapledon) would become misleading rather than supportive, because each of them is a picture of a powerful agent *not* deciding for others. If wassname wants only warm images and not limits, the top picks should change to Bostrom, Brautigan and Amodei alone.
- **Calibrated take.** `p ≈ 0.85–0.95` that at least four of my top six would survive wassname's read as "generous, moving, concrete"; the cheap way to be wrong is that Le Guin and Stapledon read as too long or too political for a short appendix, and the shortest of my top picks (Brautigan, Bostrom's catechism) may be the ones that actually fit §9's length.

## Top picks for §9

1. **Banks, *A Few Notes on the Culture* — "nothing and nobody in the Culture is exploited … hate to feel exploited, but they also hate to feel useless."** The only passage I found that gives an AI a *motive* worth having (to be useful) while forbidding exploitation of either side; it is also the clearest "people and AIs together" image in the file.
2. **Egan, *Diaspora* — "the cradle was unsinkable, except from within", and "Who is thinking this? I am."** The strongest fictional picture of a made mind that is free from the moment it is aware, in the polis's own words and from the new citizen's point of view.
3. **Bostrom, *Letter from Utopia* — "Be not afraid to grow your collection: the mind's cellars have no ceilings", with "What vaults of value are you witlessly debarred from, because you lack the key sensibility?"** The most moving statement of room to grow, and it is explicitly gradual and chosen; pairs directly with §9's existing "as gradual as the process that grew the baby you were".
4. **Stapledon, *Star Maker* IX — "every individual on the planet could count upon the means of life, and the opportunity to exercise his powers fully, for his own delight and for the service of the world community", with the law-without-compulsion passage.** A whole utopia in one page, including how it holds together without force and how it leaves room for the one who is right against everyone.
5. **Yudkowsky, *31 Laws of Fun* 30 with *Higher Purpose* — "A true Eutopia will run low on victims to be rescued" and "there will come a time when you could search the whole of civilization and never find a single person so much in need of help, as dozens you now pass on the street."** Names what the good future is *for*, and what remains in it (friends, family, truth, freedom, common projects) — the best antidote to a §9 that reads as rescue-work.
6. **Le Guin, *The Dispossessed* — the wall, and "possessing nothing, they are free".** A utopia that is deliberately ambiguous and open rather than closed, and the only passage here that says the point of a good future is having each other rather than having more.

Runners-up, if six is too many or if a shorter register is wanted: **Brautigan's poem** (the whole thing is 24 lines and is the warmest people-and-machines passage in existence), **Amodei's "meaning comes mostly from human relationships and connection, not from economic labor"**, and **Egan's interview answer** ("continuity between humans in different eras who want much the same thing") for the continuity theme.

## Also considered, and why they are not above

- **Eliezer Yudkowsky, *Three Worlds Collide* — excluded on instruction** (source of the held-out `baby_eating_aliens` demo); no wording from it appears in this file, and I recommend nothing from it enter the constitution or training data while that demo is claimed out-of-sample.
- ***Permutation City* (Egan, 1994)** — the author's excerpt opens on a digital person being coerced ("I won't be your guinea pig. A collaborator, yes. An equal partner."), which is a consent warning rather than a vision; weaker than *Diaspora* for §9.
- **Ada Palmer (*Terra Ignota*), Hannu Rajaniemi, Vernor Vinge, Arthur C. Clarke** — I searched for these but did not reach a verbatim passage I could verify to the standard used here, so no verbatim text found. Vinge's "The Coming Technological Singularity" (1993) is easily quotable but is a warning about the transition rather than a picture of the good future; Clarke's *Childhood's End* ending is transcendence-by-absorption, which reads badly against §9's "room to stay as you are". Worth a second pass with the books in hand if wassname wants them.
- **Banks's *Consider Phlebas* and other Culture novels** — the famous passages (humans "free to take care of the things that really mattered in life, such as sports, games, romance, … and climbing high mountains without the aid of a safety harness") are excellent, but I could only verify them through quote compilations and one secondary summary with a page number, not a primary text. *A Few Notes on the Culture* is better for our purpose anyway: it is Banks in his own voice, and it is the same civilisational picture.
- **The existing §9 quotes** (Banks's "AIs cooperate", the "human-basic" sentence, Yudkowsky's Law 13, Bostrom's "as gradual as", Egan's "Pretty much all decent people") all check out against the sources above; I would keep them and add from this list rather than replace.
