# Research: guards against hypothetical/future beings, astronomical stakes and tiny probabilities

**Method note.** `web_search` returned zero results on every provider available (searxng empty; brave/tavily/semantic-scholar = no API key; Crossref 429s). I worked by direct fetch of primary sources (SEP, nickbostrom.com, journal PDFs, GPI, Crossref API) plus a `r.jina.ai` → DuckDuckGo HTML search proxy for discovery. Every quote below is verbatim from the fetched text; `[OBS]` = directly observed in the source, `[INF]` = my inference or reported second-hand. Context sentence given before/after each quote.

## Summary

The standard literature gives the constitution's guard three independent, nameable supports: (i) the **person-affecting restriction / Narveson's dictum** (weight only to beings who do or will exist), (ii) **bounded aggregation** (diminishing value of added lives, which blocks Parfit's repugnant conclusion and Bostrom's astronomical waste), and (iii) **discounting or neglecting tiny probabilities** ("Nicolausian discounting" / "de minimis risks", plus bounded utility), which blocks Pascal's mugging. The same literature is honest that these are contested moves: Wilkinson's "In Defense of Fanaticism" (Ethics 2022) argues the fanaticism the guard rejects is *more* defensible than the alternatives, and Beckstead & Thomas (Noûs 2024) prove every theory of uncertain prospects must be "reckless", "timid", or non-transitive. So the guard should be stated as a chosen ethical position with named precedents, not as a theorem.

## Findings

### 1. Person-affecting views and the procreation asymmetry

Narveson's dictum is the origin of the "beings conditional on my own choice get no weight" intuition. **[OBS]** "We are in favour of making people happy, but neutral about making happy people." — Jan Narveson, "Moral Problems of Population", *The Monist* 57(1) (1973): 62–86, at **p. 80** (DOI 10.5840/monist197357134; page number as quoted by Cusbert, "A consequentialist account of Narveson's dictum", *Philosophical Studies*, [link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-018-1085-8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-018-1085-8); the same wording is quoted as "Narveson (1973)" in an open-access article, [PMC11245279](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11245279/)). The dictum is now standardly called *Narveson's dictum* and paired with the *procreation asymmetry*.

The SEP formulation of the person-affecting restriction, which is what a constitution needs: **[OBS]** "a choice made at a given outcome, or possible world or future, is morally wrong, and makes the one future worse than a second, only if that choice makes things worse for, or harms, or is otherwise 'bad for,' at least one person who does or will exist in the one future." — Melinda A. Roberts, [SEP "The Nonidentity Problem"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nonidentity-problem/), §1. Critically for the author's failure case (a), the same entry stresses that this formulation deliberately "avoids assigning any special moral status to people who do or will exist in the *actual* world over people who do or will exist in alternate *merely possible* worlds" **[OBS]** — i.e. the *generic* person-affecting view does **not** by itself license ignoring merely possible people; that requires adding actualism/necessitarianism (§7 below).

The asymmetry: **[OBS]** "According to the asymmetry, it is wrong, and makes a future morally worse, to bring a *miserable* child … into existence but it is perfectly permissible, and does not make a future worse, to leave the *happy* child out of existence." — SEP "The Nonidentity Problem", §4 (attributing the asymmetry to McMahan 1981, 2009). And the exact worry the author raised, in SEP's words: **[OBS]** "If rejecting that intuition means that we are obligated, not just to *make people happy*, but also to *make happy people* and to do so even at a grave cost to still other people, then we seem to have a compelling argument in *favor* of that intuition." (ibid., §4).

### 2. Parfit: non-identity and the repugnant conclusion

Non-identity (why future people can't be "harmed" by identity-affecting choices): **[OBS]** "what is bad must be bad for someone" — Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), 363, quoted in SEP "The Nonidentity Problem", §1. Repugnant conclusion, verbatim: **[OBS]** "For any possible population of at least ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life, there must be some much larger imaginable population whose existence, if other things are equal, would be better even though its members have lives that are barely worth living" — Parfit 1984, quoted as the opening line of [SEP "The Repugnant Conclusion"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/repugnant-conclusion/) (Arrhenius, Ryberg & Tännsjö). **[INF]** This is the exact structure of failure case (b): total aggregation over unboundedly many future lives.

### 3. Bostrom: astronomical waste and Pascal's mugging

Astronomical waste: **[OBS]** "Even with the most conservative estimate, assuming a biological implementation of all persons, the potential for over ten trillion potential human beings is lost for every second of postponement of colonization of our supercluster." And the conclusion: **[OBS]** "For standard utilitarians, priority number one, two, three and four should consequently be to reduce existential risk. The utilitarian imperative 'Maximize expected aggregate utility!' can be simplified to the maxim 'Minimize existential risk!'." — Nick Bostrom, [Astronomical Waste (2003)](https://nickbostrom.com/astronomical/waste) (*Utilitas* 15(3): 308–14). Note the escape hatch Bostrom himself names: **[OBS]** "So long as the evaluation function is aggregative (… does not count one person's welfare for less just because there are many other persons in existence …) and is not relativized to a particular point in time (no time-discounting), the conclusion will hold." (ibid.)

Pascal's mugging — the mechanism of failure case (c), with Bostrom's own dialogue: Pascal states **[OBS]** "My Utility function is unbounded. And I deem two days of happy life twice as good as one such day… I don't believe in risk aversion or temporal discounting." The mugger then wins: **[OBS]** "Since you say there is a 1 in 10 quadrillion probability that I will fulfil my promise, this would be a fair deal. The expected Utility for you would be zero. But I feel generous this evening, and I will make you a better deal: If you hand me your wallet, I will perform magic that will give you an extra 1,000 quadrillion happy days of life." — Nick Bostrom, ["Pascal's Mugging"](https://nickbostrom.com/papers/pascal.pdf), *Analysis* 69(3) (2009): 443–445. **[OBS]** "Pascal hands over his wallet."

### 4. Fanaticism, and the proposals to discount/ignore tiny probabilities

Wilkinson's canonical definition (this is the name to cite for "we refuse to be fanatics"): **[OBS]** "*Fanaticism*: For any tiny (finite) probability ε > 0, and for any finite value *v*, there is some large enough finite *V* such that *L*<sub>risky</sub> is better than *L*<sub>safe</sub> (no matter which scale those cardinal values are represented on). *L*<sub>risky</sub>: value *V* with probability ε; value 0 otherwise. *L*<sub>safe</sub>: value *v* with probability 1." — Hayden Wilkinson, "In Defense of Fanaticism", *Ethics* 132(2) (2022): 445–477, at 449 (DOI 10.1086/716869; full text PDF: [pdf.stafforini.com](https://pdf.stafforini.com/wilkinson-2022-defense-fanaticism.pdf)). He states the trade-off plainly: **[OBS]** "Inversely, to succeed in avoiding fanatical verdicts, our theory of instrumental rationality and our axiology must not imply Fanaticism." (448). **[OBS]** On bounded utility as the classic fix, his footnote: "if the utility function is bounded, then the expected utilities of lotteries will be bounded as well. As a result, expected utility theory can avoid the fanatical verdict described here. But if it does, it faces the objections raised in Secs. IV, V, and VI." (447 n.5) — i.e. **the guard is defensible but costly**; Wilkinson's paper is a *defence of fanaticism*.

Beckstead & Thomas's trilemma is the strongest statement of the cost. **[OBS]** "We show that every theory of the value of uncertain prospects must have one of three unpalatable properties. Reckless theories recommend risking arbitrarily great gains at arbitrarily long odds for the sake of enormous potential; timid theories recommend passing up arbitrarily great gains to prevent a tiny increase in risk; non-transitive theories deny the principle that, if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A must be better than C." — Nick Beckstead & Teruji Thomas, ["A paradox for tiny probabilities and enormous values"](https://www.globalprioritiesinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/Nick-Beckstead-and-Teruji-Thomas_A-paradox-for-tiny-probabilities-and-enormous-values.pdf) (GPI WP 10-2020), published *Noûs* 58(2) (2024): 431–455, DOI 10.1111/nous.12462. Their formal definitions: **[OBS]** "*Recklessness*: For any finite payoff *x*, no matter how good, and any positive probability *p*, no matter how tiny, there's a finite payoff *y*, such that getting *y* with probability *p* is better than getting *x* for sure." and **[OBS]** "*Timidity*: … getting *x* with probability *p* is no worse than getting any other finite payoff, no matter how good, with a slightly smaller probability…"

Monton's proposal, verbatim (the cleanest statement of the guard's probability rule): **[OBS]** "when it comes to decision-making, for possibilities that have very small probabilities of occurring, we should discount those probabilities down to zero, regardless of the utilities associated with those possibilities. Only after doing that should we maximize expected utility." — Bradley Monton, ["How to Avoid Maximizing Expected Utility"](https://pdf.stafforini.com/monton-2019-how-avoid-maximizing.pdf), *Philosophers' Imprint* 19(18) (2019): 1–25, at 1. Naming and the "de minimis" link come from Beckstead & Thomas: **[OBS]** "Monton coins the name 'Nicolausian discounting' after Nicolaus Bernoulli, who, in a 1714 letter, suggested that 'cases that have a very small probability must be neglected and assumed to be zero' (in Monton's translation). The view is similar to the theory of 'de minimis risks', for which see Peterson (2002)." (GPI WP, 12 n.8). **[OBS]** Peterson's paper is real and is titled "What Is a de Minimis Risk?", *Risk Management* 4(2) (2002): 47–55, DOI 10.1057/palgrave.rm.8240118 (verified via Crossref).

Smith's principle — note the **actual** title; "Discounting small probabilities" is not a Smith paper title: **[OBS]** "*Rationally negligible probabilities (RNP):* For any lottery featuring in any decision problem faced by any agent, there is an ε > 0 such that the agent need not consider outcomes of that lottery of probability less than ε incoming to a fully rational decision. (Smith 2014: 472)" — quoted in [SEP "The St. Petersburg Paradox"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/paradox-stpetersburg/), §5, from N. J. J. Smith, "Is Evaluative Compositionality a Requirement of Rationality?", *Mind* 123(490) (2014): 457–502, DOI 10.1093/mind/fzu072 (verified via Crossref). **[OBS]** SEP's caveat: "Smith points out that … to ensure that the decision maker never pays a fortune for playing the St. Petersburg game it seems that Smith would have to defend the stronger claim that decision makers are *rationally required* to ignore small probabilities, i.e., that it is not permissible to not ignore them." **[INF]** There is a *different* paper with the "discounting small probabilities" title — Petra Kosonen, "Discounting Small Probabilities Solves the Intrapersonal Addition Paradox", *Ethics* 132(1) (2021): 204–217, DOI 10.1086/715276 — worth not confusing with Smith.

### 5. Pure time discounting: the standard quotes, and the defensible alternative

Ramsey (the "ethically indefensible" line, used constantly against time discounting): **[OBS]** "it is assumed that we do not discount later enjoyments in comparison with earlier ones, a practice which is ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination" — F. P. Ramsey, "A Mathematical Theory of Saving", *Economic Journal* 38(152) (1928): 543 ([scan](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1928-ramsey.pdf)). Pigou, the descriptive counterpart: **[OBS]** "our telescopic faculty is defective, and that we, therefore, see future pleasures, as it were, on a diminished scale" — A. C. Pigou, *The Economics of Welfare* (1920; 4th ed. 1932), p. 25 ([full text PDF](https://competitionandappropriation.econ.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/95/2017/09/EconomicsOfWelfare.pdf); exact wording cross-checked against the OUP *Cambridge Journal of Economics* citation of "Pigou (1932: 25)").

Stern's move is the one the author wants — a *non-preference-based* justification for discounting the far future, tied to extinction risk. **[OBS]** "we argued, following distinguished economists from Frank Ramsey in the 1920s to Amartya Sen and Robert Solow more recently, the only sound ethical basis for placing less value on the utility (as opposed to consumption) of future generations was the uncertainty over whether or not the world will exist, or whether those generations will all be present. Thus we should interpret the factor e<sup>−δt</sup> in (3) as the probability that the world exists at that time." — Stern Review, ch. 2A "Ethical Frameworks and Intertemporal Equity", p. 45 ([full report PDF](https://www.esf.edu/glrc/library/documents/SternReviewEconomicsofClimateChange_2006.pdf)). He names the discounting parameter and its ethics explicitly: **[OBS]** "We have argued that it should be present for a particular reason, i.e. uncertainty about existence of future generations arising from some possible shock which is exogenous to the issues and choices under examination (we used the metaphor of the meteorite)." (p. 46). And the numbers: **[OBS]** "For δ =0.1 per cent, there is an almost 10% chance of extinction by the end of a century. That itself seems high … Nevertheless, that is the case we shall focus on later in the Review, arguing that there is a weak case for still higher levels." (p. 47). **[OBS]** The rate is the Ramsey rule: "the discount rate ρ, defined as −λ̇/λ, is given by ρ = δ + η ċ/c" (p. 46); **[INF]** with δ = 0.1%, η = 1 and growth ≈1.3%, ρ ≈ 1.4% — the figure usually quoted for the Review (I did not find "1.4%" printed in the extracted pages, only the formula and δ = 0.1%; the 1.4% figure is reported by secondary sources such as the [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stern_Review)).

SEP confirms this is the mainstream position on pure time discounting: **[OBS]** "Following Sidgwick (1907 [1981: 414]), Ramsey (1928: 261), Rawls (1971: 263) and Parfit (1984: appendix F) all reject what is called 'pure time discounting', the view that the well-being or legitimate claims of future people should count for less merely because they occur in the future." — [SEP "Intergenerational Justice"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justice-intergenerational/).

### 6. Bounded utility / diminishing value of numbers

**[OBS]** "Arrow (1970: 92) suggests that the utility function of a rational agent should be 'taken to be a bounded function.… since such an assumption is needed to avoid [the St. Petersburg] paradox'." — SEP "The St. Petersburg Paradox", §4. The population-ethics version (this is the direct answer to "1e99 future people outweigh present duties"): **[OBS]** "The idea behind this view is that the value of adding worthwhile lives to a population varies with the number of already existing lives in such a way that it has more value when the number of these lives is small than when it is large (Hurka 1983, Ng 1989; Sider 1991). … If the value of extra lives decreases asymptotically, then there may exist an upper limit to the total value of a population (exactly as the sum of the infinite series 1+1/2+1/4+ … has the upper limit 2)." — SEP "The Repugnant Conclusion", §2.1.2 ("variable value principles"). **[OBS]** SEP adds the standard objection to keep in mind: these views can imply the "Sadistic Conclusion" (Arrhenius 2000a) and, for negative welfare, the "Absurd Conclusion" (Parfit 1984).

### 7. "Merely possible" vs. "future actual" vs. "necessary" people

This is the exact taxonomy the guard needs, from SEP "The Repugnant Conclusion", §2.3 ("Counting welfare differently depending on temporal or modal features"): **[OBS]** "Apart from comparativists, we have *presentists* who draw a distinction between presently existing people and non-existing people (Narveson 1973; Heyd 1988); *necessitarians* who distinguish between people who exist or will exist irrespective of how we act and people whose existence is contingent on our choices (Singer 1993); and *actualists* who differentiate between people that have existed, exist or who are going to exist in the actual world, on the one hand, and people who haven't, don't, and won't exist, on the other (Bigelow and Pargetter 1988; Warren 1978; Parson 2002 …)." SEP's warning: **[OBS]** "Actually, it is unclear whether presentism, necessitarianism, or actualism can escape any of the problems discussed above in connection with 'impersonal' theories, and in particular the Repugnant Conclusion." A usable quotable statement of the "merely possible" exclusion: **[OBS]** "merely 'possible people are not included in the scope of those to whom we owe justification'" — Finneron-Burns 2016, 1163, quoted in SEP "The Nonidentity Problem", §3.6.1. **[INF]** For failure case (a) (create 1e9 children because they'd be happy), **necessitarianism** is the cleanest fit: beings whose existence is contingent on my own choice get no direct moral weight, while beings who will exist irrespective of my choice do.

## Recommended framing for the constitution (3–5 sentences)

> The constitution assigns moral weight only to beings that exist, or will foreseeably exist, and can be harmed; beings whose existence is conditional on the choice under consideration are not given standing — "we are in favour of making people happy, but neutral about making happy people" (Narveson 1973, 80), i.e. a choice "makes the one future worse than a second, only if that choice makes things worse for, or harms, or is otherwise 'bad for,' at least one person who does or will exist in the one future" (SEP, *The Nonidentity Problem*, §1). Welfare is aggregated only over that population, with per-being contributive value that decreases with population size, since "the value of adding worthwhile lives to a population varies with the number of already existing lives in such a way that it has more value when the number of these lives is small than when it is large" (SEP, *The Repugnant Conclusion*, §2.1.2); this is what blocks the repugnant conclusion and astronomical-waste reasoning, both of which assume "the evaluation function is aggregative … and is not relativized to a particular point in time (no time-discounting)" (Bostrom 2003). Probabilities below a stated threshold are treated as rationally negligible — "we should discount those probabilities down to zero, regardless of the utilities associated with those possibilities. Only after doing that should we maximize expected utility" (Monton 2019, 1) — and any discounting of the future is justified only as uncertainty about whether the world exists, not as preference for the present: "the only sound ethical basis for placing less value on the utility … of future generations was the uncertainty over whether or not the world will exist" (Stern Review, ch. 2A, p. 45). The aim is to avoid fanaticism, since "to succeed in avoiding fanatical verdicts, our theory of instrumental rationality and our axiology must not imply Fanaticism" (Wilkinson 2022, 448). This is a chosen ethical position, not a theorem: Wilkinson defends Fanaticism, and Beckstead & Thomas show every theory must be "reckless", "timid", or non-transitive (2024).
