# Brown's human universals: discussion, criticism, and constitutional use

2026-10-15. Source review, not a constitution edit. -- PI/Sol

## Bottom line

**Inference:** Keep Brown as a useful, revisable inventory of recurring human capacities, practices, sentiments, and constraints. Do **not** present his inventory as an established list of universally endorsed values, innate mechanisms, or final ends. Brown himself explicitly warns about weak sampling, imprecise descriptions, and unequal confidence across entries. Later criticism strengthens these warnings without showing that the entire enterprise is mistaken.

**Inference:** The most useful later distinction is Norenzayan and Heine's: a capacity can exist across cultures without having the same function or being equally readily used. This directly blocks a leap from universal empathy, fairness concepts, or moral sentiments to a single universal preference ordering.

**Inference — important scope mismatch:** The task describes a proposed ends / social tools / limits arrangement. The live `constitution/v4.md:130-152` instead sorts by presumed membership in human nature, useful values, and institutions. Both arrangements need qualifications, but for different reasons. The former is **our normative interpretation**; the latter additionally makes causal and confidence claims that Brown's list alone cannot establish. The prior `slop/research/20261015_brown_categories.md` already gives the detailed entry-by-entry analysis; this report adds reception and methodological criticism.

**Inference — research limitation:** I verified three contemporary journal reviews bibliographically, but could not obtain their review texts. I therefore do not invent their praise or criticism. The strongest verified criticism here comes from Brown's own methodological concessions, Antweiler's introduction, Norenzayan–Heine, and WEIRD. Antweiler's 2012 book is covered through an explicitly identified review, not a claimed full-book reading. Pinker's appendix is available through a reproduction; his discussion is checked against his own later summary, not a full reading of the 2002 book.

## 2. Brown's own later qualifications

### Brown 2004: the list is not equally secure, and universality is not one thing

Source type: **paper**, Donald E. Brown, “Human universals, human nature & human culture,” *Daedalus* **133**(4), 2004, pp. 47–54. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1162/0011526042365645). Readable [author-manuscript PDF](https://www.humiliationstudies.org/documents/BrownUniversalsDaedalus.pdf); local extraction `.local/papers/brown_crit_20261015/brown2004.txt`. The manuscript's pagination differs from the published article.

**Quote — Brown, manuscript p. 4:**
> In most cases this sampling has not been rigorous.

**Quote — Brown, same paragraph:**
> Thus the confidence one can have in particular claims of universality is quite variable.

Source: [PDF](https://www.humiliationstudies.org/documents/BrownUniversalsDaedalus.pdf), “Methodological and disciplinary issues,” manuscript p. 4.

**Inference — what it says:** No investigator knows every society. Records differ in observer, date, detail, and precision. Claims therefore rest on samples, often not rigorously constructed. This is Brown's own concession, not an external caricature.

**Inference — strength / change:** **Very strong** evidence against an unqualified statement that every listed item is established in every known society. Use different confidence levels by entry. A literary synthesis is not a uniform, audited society-by-trait dataset.

**Quote — Brown, manuscript p. 2:**
> Many universals have a collective rather than individual referent.

Source: [PDF](https://www.humiliationstudies.org/documents/BrownUniversalsDaedalus.pdf), “Kinds of universals.”

**Inference:** His example is music and dance: societies have them, but not everyone makes music or dances. The same caution applies to religious practices, leadership, or childcare. A society-level recurrence is not evidence that each individual endorses or wants the practice.

**Inference — Brown's formal classification, not our classification:** Brown distinguishes **absolute**, **near**, **conditional**, **statistical**, and **universal-pool** claims (manuscript pp. 2–3). Absolute means no known exception in the investigated record; near allows a few exceptions; conditional means an if–then regularity; statistical means recurrence above chance in unrelated societies; a pool is a restricted set of possible variants from which cultures select. A statistical universal does **not** mean occurrence in every society. He also distinguishes individual from collective traits, emic from etic descriptions, and simple traits from complexes. These axes should not be collapsed into an innate / cultural ranking.

**Inference — causation:** His causal discussion includes ancient diffusion, shared physical facts, mental structure and operation, and mental evolution (manuscript pp. 5–6). Fire and cooking can be ancient, useful cultural traditions. Universality alone does not distinguish a genetic adaptation from shared learning, historical spread, or convergence on a common problem. Brown is strongly sympathetic to evolutionary psychology, but does not say that every list entry is a separate adaptation.

**Inference — strength / change:** **Strong conceptual and methodological correction**, not a modern empirical audit of every entry. Retain the inventory; weaken causal lead-ins and keep frequency, explanation, and endorsement separate.

### Antweiler, *Our Common Denominator* (2016): universals without biological reduction

Source type: **book**, checked through its full ten-page introduction and publisher's table of contents, not the entire book. [Publisher](https://www.berghahnbooks.com/title/AntweilerOur); [introduction PDF](https://www.berghahnbooks.com/downloads/intros/AntweilerOur_intro.pdf). Local `antintro.txt`.

**Quote — Antweiler, introduction p. 1:**
> These commonalities, or universals, while deriving in part from human nature, may also have specific social, cultural, and systemic sources.

Source: [introduction](https://www.berghahnbooks.com/downloads/intros/AntweilerOur_intro.pdf).

**Inference — what it says:** Antweiler defends the search for shared patterns and regards cultural diversity as essential evidence, not an obstacle to be ignored. He explicitly aims to correct Brown's evolutionary-psychology emphasis by considering other causes. He distinguishes empirical universals from claims about what everyone morally ought to accept. His introduction warns that political hopes for common humanity can tempt researchers to assume similarities rather than demonstrate them.

**Quote — Antweiler, introduction p. 8:**
> universals, contrary to broadly held assumptions, are not the same as natural or inherent human attributes.

Source: [introduction](https://www.berghahnbooks.com/downloads/intros/AntweilerOur_intro.pdf).

**Inference — classification actually verified:** Chapter 6 is a taxonomy by **levels, spheres and time frame; substance and depth; degree of universality; and conditional or other specific forms**. Chapter 7 separately classifies explanations, including **cultural transmission/diffusion; function, convergence and structural implication; evolution/adaptation; and complex causes**. These are separate questions: *what kind of claim is this?* and *why does the pattern occur?* Neither is our moral three-way sorting.

**Inference — important secondary report:** Brown's [2017 retrospective](https://literary-universals.uconn.edu/2017/06/25/human-universals/) reports that Antweiler distinguishes **biotic universals** from patterns recurring above chance among unrelated peoples. Brown accepts the importance of causal distinctions but argues that comparative ethnography is ultimately needed to verify universality whether or not a trait is biotic. This classification is **verified as Brown's account of Antweiler**, not as a quotation from Antweiler's unread Chapter 6. It would be misleading to present the exact full taxonomy as independently checked.

**Inference — strength / change:** **Strong** conceptual support for a plural-cause, qualified prior. This book is not a reason to relabel every doubtful entry as false. It is a reason to stop treating universal, innate, valuable, and immutable as synonyms. The live constitution's human-nature ranking needs more evidence than the inventory supplies.

### Norenzayan and Heine (2005): a usable hierarchy, with explicit tests

Source type: **paper**, Ara Norenzayan and Steven J. Heine, “Psychological universals: What are they and how can we know?”, *Psychological Bulletin* **131**(5), pp. 763–784. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.131.5.763); [author-hosted PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~heine/docs/universals.pdf). Read relevant methodological, abstraction, taxonomy, and example sections; local `nh2005.txt`.

**Quote — authors, p. 770:**
> At too abstract a level, universals are too diffuse to be of significant empirical import

Source: [PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~heine/docs/universals.pdf), local `nh2005.txt:500-509`.

**Inference — levels, pp. 772–776:** Their hierarchy concerns **psychological processes**, not whether a society has an institution. In their deliberately heuristic framework:

| Level | Plain meaning | Evidence needed to distinguish it |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonuniversal** | A process or cognitive tool is absent from at least some cultures' repertoire. | Strong evidence of absence, not one failed task or an unfamiliar instruction. Their examples include culturally acquired abacus strategies. |
| **Existential universal** | The tool is available across cultures, but its ordinary uses/functions can differ. | Show the process can be elicited across cultures, including under suitable instructions or conditions; do not require the same spontaneous use. |
| **Functional universal** | The same process is available and serves the same function across cultures, but its accessibility differs. | Show comparable use/function while measuring differences in frequency, ease, or effect size. |
| **Accessibility universal** | The same process has the same function and is comparably accessible across cultures. | Strong evidence for the existence, use, and accessibility claims. A nonsignificant difference in a weak study is not enough. |

**Inference:** Table 1 orders evidence for existential, functional, and accessibility universality as weak, moderate, and strong; it also requires strong evidence for a nonuniversal. Accessibility is not a moral ranking, and functional does not mean morally beneficial.

**Quote — authors, p. 772, footnote 4:**
> These are not meant to be different kinds of universals in a metaphysical sense.

Source: [PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~heine/docs/universals.pdf), local `nh2005.txt:675-684`.

**Inference — how to test:** Their strategies are (1) compare highly divergent cultures; (2) triangulate with a third culture to separate competing explanations; and (3) use wider cross-cultural surveys. They require comparable measures, supported by back-translation, locally developed measures, multiple methods, and control conditions. Developmental, neural, or cross-species evidence can contribute, but does not replace cross-cultural tests. Common neural involvement alone does not prove a culturally independent mechanism: acquired skills can have neural specialization too. See pp. 766–770.

**Inference — a revealing example:** Positive self-regard can be expressed as Western self-enhancement or East Asian self-improvement and face maintenance. The possible shared end is more abstract than one culturally familiar strategy. Likewise, having a fairness concept does not demonstrate equal-split preferences, and mental-state understanding does not demonstrate equal compassionate concern for everyone.

**Inference — strength / change:** **Very strong conceptual and methodological relevance**, with empirical examples rather than an exhaustive test of Brown. Add an existence/function/accessibility distinction to the research annotations, not necessarily a long taxonomy inside the persona text. Treat confidence in a claim separately from the level of universality claimed.

### Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan (2010): WEIRD is a warning about inference, not a refutation of all universals

Source type: **paper**, “The weirdest people in the world?”, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* **33**, pp. 61–83. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X0999152X); [published article and commentaries PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~heine/docs/weirdpeople.pdf), local `weird2010.txt`. The earlier PDF also downloaded as `weird.raw` is a 2009 manuscript; use `weird2010` for the published text.

**Quote — authors, abstract:**
> there are no obvious a priori grounds for claiming that a particular behavioral phenomenon is universal based on sampling from a single subpopulation.

Source: [published PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~heine/docs/weirdpeople.pdf), p. 61.

**Inference — what it says:** The reviewed experimental literature draws heavily on Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic populations, often students, who can be outliers rather than representative humans. Their historical database audit reports 96% of psychology participants from Western industrialized countries containing 12% of the world's population; these are statistics about that reviewed literature, not a measurement of Brown's ethnographic sources. They document variation in fairness, cooperation, perception, reasoning and self-concepts, while also identifying promising shared patterns.

**Inference — strength / change:** **Strong** evidence against importing a familiar Western implementation or effect size as the universal itself. It does not show that Brown merely surveyed Western undergraduates, or that every society lacks fairness concepts. It does undermine a leap from the word fairness to a fixed distributive rule, from private inner life to Western individualism, or from attachment to one universal attachment style. Keep the constitution's WEIRD discussion, but apply its caution to the Brown paragraph too.

## 4. The objections: what survives scrutiny?

**Inference:** Relativist and interpretive anthropology are not one position. An objection to a thin list as a definition of humanity need not deny biology or every cross-cultural similarity. Brown himself describes Geertz as advocating interaction between biology, psychology, society, and particular cultures, and accepts important parts of that argument (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:3490-3541`). Geertz's discussion predates the 1991 book; it is **not** a review of Brown.

| Objection | Evidence and assessment | Change to constitutional use |
|---|---|---|
| **Too trivial or abstract** | Brown discusses classification versus content at `brown_human_universals.txt:2327-2346`; Norenzayan–Heine explicitly accept the abstraction problem. Saying every society has religion, property, or morality can conceal enormous variation. **Strong objection to overinterpretation**, not a demonstration that all broad categories are useless. | State the narrow commonality; do not silently give it a familiar Western content. |
| **Defined to fit / made unfalsifiable** | Broadening a category after every counterexample can preserve its name while losing testable content. Brown's distinctions are sometimes legitimate clarifications, but operational definitions must be stated before assessing exceptions. **Strong methodological risk**; this review has not demonstrated intentional manipulation by Brown. | Preserve original claims, definitions, exceptions, and source versions in annotations. Do not move casually from no exceptions to statistical recurrence while retaining the same certainty. |
| **Biased or incomplete ethnography** | Brown 2004 directly acknowledges sampling and reporting limitations. Norenzayan–Heine note that HRAF conclusions depend on the reliability and validity of the underlying ethnographies. Observers can overlook ordinary activities, rely on elites, or describe norms rather than conduct. **Strong general objection**; the specific direction and size of each bias need investigation. | Lack of a report is not observed absence; lack of a reported exception is not exhaustive confirmation. |
| **Sampling units are not independent** | Shared ancestry, diffusion, ecology, colonial contact, and borrowed descriptions can make many apparent observations less independent than their count suggests. This is a methodological inference from Brown's diffusion account and the later demand for divergent/triangulated samples, not a fresh measured audit of his corpus. | Do not count 100 related reports as 100 independent origins. Keep transmission and recurrence separate. |
| **Coding and aggregation** | The same word can denote different phenomena; different words can denote similar phenomena. Society-level presence, typical behaviour, individual capacity, professed ideal, and enacted rule are different variables. **Strong**, directly supported by the emic/etic and collective/individual distinctions. | Use a separate claim and definition for each, rather than treating every list item as one binary value. |
| **Etic categories imposed from outside** | Brown intentionally uses etic categories, not only insiders' explicit categories. That is not automatically illegitimate: grammar need not be explicitly conceptualized to exist. But an analyst's category does not prove an explicit shared value. **Strong objection to the value inference**, not a blanket objection to comparison. | Distinguish researcher-described pattern from participants' recognized category or endorsed ideal. |
| **Western bias** | WEIRD gives empirical examples of misleading generalization; Antweiler explicitly asks how to avoid Eurocentric categories. Brown 2017 acknowledges the charge. **Strong reason to check categories and measurement**, insufficient to dismiss every universal without testing it. | Seek non-Western interpretations and exceptions; do not define fairness, modesty, selfhood or law solely by modern Western examples. |
| **Universal therefore innate** | Brown and Antweiler both allow other causes. Near-universal learning opportunities can yield common outcomes; an innate conditional mechanism can yield variable outcomes. **Decisive logical objection** to that implication. | Remove unsupported innateness rankings. Do not call every unwanted trait an evolutionary byproduct. |
| **Universal therefore good** | Rape, deception, ethnocentrism, and conflict can recur along with their restraint. Occurrence and endorsement are distinct, and endorsement still does not settle moral justification. **Decisive logical objection**. | Brown informs value learning and constraints; the constitution's independently stated moral test evaluates what to cultivate. |

## 5. Specific contested or easily overstated entries

**Inference — scope:** The table distinguishes an actual documented qualification from a claim disproved by later research. I did not find and verify modern decisive counterexamples for every requested entry; do not read the table as declaring them all refuted.

| Entry | Verified issue | Strength and proposed treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Incest prevention / avoidance** | Brown explicitly separates taboo, avoidance, and developmental inhibition. His own conclusion says taboo is not absolute, while avoidance may be universal. He reviews Egyptian sibling marriage and competing readings of kibbutz evidence. | **Strong source-based qualification.** Keep a narrowly specified tendency or constraint; do not equate it with a universally agreed kinship boundary, invariably effective avoidance, or a universal moral rule. Do not infer that all family-like co-residence or all culturally defined incest shares one mechanism. |
| **Rape proscribed** | The chapter's legal claim concerns prohibitions **in certain situations**, not universal modern consent law or protection of every person in every relationship. | **Sexual modesty** | Brown explicitly allows customary nakedness and describes normal privacy in intercourse and elimination. | **Religion / supernatural belief** | Brown reports Hockett's objection that some peoples do not conceptualize religion as a separate sphere, and answers that his claim is etic. The UP claim concerns belief beyond the visible and palpable. | **Strong category problem.** Distinguish institutional religion, explicit supernatural concepts, recurring explanatory tendencies, and individual belief. | **Fairness / equity; empathy** | Existence of an equity concept does not fix its rule or beneficiaries. Brown's empathy means understanding others' inner states; it is not identical to benevolent care. | **Strong conceptual correction.** Keep the capacity/concern as a tentative input; infer final valuation and scope separately. |

## 6. Did anyone already make our values / tools / limits distinction?

**Inference — answer:** **I found no verified source in this search that sorts Brown's inventory into exactly these three normative groups.** This is a bounded negative result, not a claim of originality across all anthropology or ethics. Brown's formal and causal classes, Antweiler's taxonomy, and Norenzayan–Heine's hierarchy are different schemes. Neither universal-pool nor accessibility means valued for itself; neither cultural nor evolved means morally approved.

**Inference — useful partial precedents:**

- **Malinowski's needs and cultural responses**, as described by Brown, distinguish a need from the institution addressing it. The table pairs bodily comfort with shelter, safety with protection, and health with hygiene. This resembles ends versus means, but is not a sorting of Brown's list and has no corresponding unwanted-limits bin. Source type: **book's account of an earlier book**, `.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:3142-3188`.
- **Norenzayan–Heine's positive-self-regard example** explicitly treats self-enhancement and self-improvement as different means to a proposed shared end. This is a local analytical precedent, not the requested inventory-wide taxonomy. Source type: **paper**, [PDF](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~heine/docs/universals.pdf), p. 771, local `nh2005.txt:597-609`.
- **Brown's adaptation versus incidental effect distinction** is about evolutionary explanation, not moral status. A biological adaptation can be unwanted, and a byproduct can be enjoyed for itself. Source type: **book**, `.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4729-4745`.

**Quote — Brown's table headings for Malinowski:**
> Basic Needs

**Quote — paired table heading:**
> Cultural Responses

Source: `.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:3172-3180` (**book**, printed p. 67).

**Inference — consequence:** It is defensible to use our three groups **as an explicit interpretive aid**, provided they are overlapping, revisable, and not attributed to Brown. A person can enjoy music for itself and use it for ritual; generosity can be admired and sustain exchange; attachment can be cherished and produce grief. Causal origin, present usefulness, and final valuation are separate questions.

## 7. Concrete recommendations — eight

1. **Replace the certainty claim, subject to author approval.** Suggested wording, **our inference, not a source quotation**: *Brown's inventory describes traits reported across many or all studied societies. It is a revisable starting point for learning what people care about and what constrains them, not a list of universally endorsed or innate values.*
2. **Name our sorting as our interpretation.** Ends, social tools, and responses/limits are overlapping uses of the evidence, not Brown's taxonomy. Do not let the live draft's nature-confidence ranking stand without independent trait-specific causal evidence.
