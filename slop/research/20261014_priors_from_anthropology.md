## Method and short result

**Reading note.** I read `AGENTS.md` first. I searched the four supplied books, then read the passages around the matches. I also read the four supplied research notes and the constitution, including its author notes. I did not run a model, use an API, or fetch new sources. The book files were read in place; no work file was made in `/tmp`.

**Quote / inference rule.** The numbered evidence blocks below give exact local `file:line` quotes. Every source-based conclusion refers to those blocks. Quotes retain source spelling and Markdown escapes. **Inference** means my conclusion, not the author’s words. Evidence labels describe the reported method; I did not reanalyse the original data or check later replications. Existing research notes are marked **indirect** where they quote a source I did not open in this run. A citation to a note is not a fresh check of the paper it quotes.

**Inference — main result (E01–E24, E39–E40).** Do not write one list of “human values” with one confidence level. Separate (1) common motives and emotions, (2) learned norms and institutions, (3) traits on which WEIRD populations often have high scores, and (4) chosen values of this Western/Stoic model. Common practice, stable practice, useful practice, and morally right practice are not the same claim.

**Inference — two limits (E07–E09, E41–E57).** These sources do not establish a human-wide wish for radical self-modification. They also do not establish the proposed “90% slaves” population share for Rome or colonial America. They support narrower claims about self-development, variable openness, widespread examples of slavery, and mixed causes of institutional change.

## B. Slavery, stable societies, and roles

### B1. How common, and how stable?

**Inference — common, but not quantified (E41–E43).** Henrich says the chiefdoms he describes usually had slaves. He also describes a slave trade that continued across Inca and Spanish rule. Durant gives a slave count for ancient Attica. These are evidence of several settings and long-lived practices. They are not a systematic percentage of all societies that had slavery.

**Inference — the 90% example is not supported (E43–E44).** Durant’s Attica numbers imply about 36.5% slaves (115,000 / 315,000), not 90%. Attica is not Rome. E44 supplies a claim of 210 years of Roman class and imperial peace, not a Roman slave census. I found no passage in these supplied books that verifies a 90% share for Rome or colonial America. Do not adopt that number as a fact.

**Inference — stability has several meanings (E22, E39–E44).** An institution can last for centuries or resist individual change without meeting the constitution’s stronger condition that nobody gains by dropping the drive (E80). Conquest, hierarchy, and sanctions can keep institutions in place (E22, E41). A slave-owning society can produce major art without evidence here that all its members were better off (E43, E81). The quoted authors explicitly separate stable norms and group success from moral goodness (E22, E39–E40). Slavery therefore challenges a rule based only on stability; its long life does not by itself defeat a rule that also asks what affected people would want.

**Inference — do not tell a single-cause story (E45–E53).** The sources support changes in incentives, religion, legal categories, labor organization, and political power. They also describe European expansion with slavery (E53). A Western psychological trend and Western participation in slavery can coexist. This evidence does not rank the causes of modern abolition or settle the profitability debate.

### B3. Venice and China

**Inference — limited answer (E45, E51–E52, E71).** The strongest comparison in the supplied passages is Frankish/English manorial organization, not Venice. Henrich describes Chinese clan ownership and Durant reports Chinese reform, but neither quoted passage measures comparative slave shares or growth without slavery. I found no direct passage that establishes the proposed Venice-versus-Rome comparison. It remains a research gap, not a counterexample verified here.

### B4. What this says about role-based virtues

**Inference (E21, E28, E38–E40).** These books support the existence of role-specific expectations. They do not show that different classes have different moral worth. Authority and social roles can help coordination; they can also preserve exclusion. A prior should distinguish “people recognize roles and obligations” from “the present distribution of roles is fair.” Haidt’s warning about his own descriptive definition (E39) is the clearest source reason not to treat cooperative hierarchy as a sufficient moral test.

Classification key:

- **(iii)** chiefly a response to harm, violation, or threatened coordination. A response need not prove that a whole society is failing.
- **(i?)** nearest fit only. A capacity is not a value, and the books do not establish that this exact entry is innate. Retain only with that limit.
- **Mixed** marks a real overlap, not an error to remove.
