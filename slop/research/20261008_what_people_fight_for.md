# Research: What people act for, at real cost (behavioural, not declaratory)

Quote-first brief. Every claim is tagged **[OBS]** = directly observed in a source I read, or **[INF]** = my inference. Source type noted (dataset / peer-reviewed / book / NGO report / press). Where I could not verify something I say so in §4.

## 1. Short answer

**People most often act, at cost, for voice over their own government — and second for material security — not for the abstract rights that constitutions list.** The single largest coded demand in the biggest cross-national protest dataset is the perceived failure of representation itself: "Our study shows the most consistent reason for people around the world to protest is the perceived failure of democracies. About 54% of all protests considered between 2006 and 2020 (a total of 1503 protest events overall) relate to a failure of political representation and of political systems." (Ortiz, Burke, Berrada & Saenz Cortés, *World Protests 2006–2020*, Palgrave/FES/IPD, dataset chapter) **[OBS]**

The most frequent single demand inside that category is "real democracy": "This is the largest demand from around the world, present in nearly 28% of all protests counted and the single most prevalent protest issue to emerge from the study, as it is an issue in 779 protests." (same) **[OBS]** Economic justice is close behind: "Overall, 1484 protests in the period 2006–2020, or nearly 53% of total protests counted in the study, reflect people's outrage at economic and social public policy failures and a perceived lack of broad-based development." **[OBS]**

For maximalist, life-risking action the goal is even blunter: **who rules, and who rules you**. NAVCO's campaign-level population is defined by three goals — regime change, anti-occupation, secession — and "far more people have participated in nonviolent than violent campaigns in the postwar period" (Chenoweth & Shay, *JPR*, NAVCO 2.1 abstract) **[OBS]**. **[INF]** Reading the datasets together: the revealed hierarchy of costly action is (1) self-determination / who governs, (2) protection from arbitrary state violence and corruption, (3) material subsistence, (4) then the identity-and-dignity rights (women, LGBT, racial, indigenous, religious, animal) that dominate modern constitutional discourse but are a minority of costly action.

## 3. A principled way to separate taboos that are Western or recent

Three complementary mechanisms, each with a testable signature.

**(a) Pinker — "rights revolutions" are dated, and he says so.** He locates them in one narrow window and one lineage: "the postwar era, symbolically inaugurated by the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948, has seen a growing revulsion against aggression on smaller scales, including violence against ethnic minorities, women, children, homosexuals, and animals. These spin-offs from the concept of human rights—civil rights, women's rights, children's rights, gay rights, and animal rights—were asserted in a cascade of movements from the late 1950s to the present day which I will call the Rights Revolutions." (Pinker, *The Better Angels of Our Nature*, ch. 7, book) **[OBS]** He then concedes their novelty himself: "The Rights Revolutions too have given us ideals that educated people today take for granted but that are virtually unprecedented in human history, such as that people of all races and creeds have equal rights, that women should be free from all forms of coercion, that children should never, ever be spanked, that students should be protected from bullying, and that there's nothing wrong with being gay." **[OBS]** And he gives the diagnostic for when a taboo is parochial rather than moral: "The elevation of parochial values to the realm of the sacred is a license to dismiss other people's interests, and an imperative to reject the possibility of compromise." **[OBS]** The empirical corroboration is in §2's gay-rights and child-marriage rows: the trend is real, recent, and only partly global. **[INF]** *Test for the constitution:* a rule that only appears after ~1950, and only in high-income/Western jurisdictions, should be flagged as a candidate local norm, not a human universal.

**(b) Singer — reason expands the circle; the expansion has a direction and a stopping rule.** Singer's epigraph is Lecky: "At one time the benevolent affections embrace merely the family, soon the circle expanding includes first a class, then a nation, then a coalition of nations, then all humanity, and finally, its influence is felt in the dealings of man with the animal world." (W. E. H. Lecky, epigraph, *The Expanding Circle*, 1981, book PDF) **[OBS]** Singer's own stopping rule: "The only justifiable stopping place for the expansion of altruism is the point at which all whose welfare can be affected by our actions are included within the circle of altruism." (quoted in *The Expanding Circle*, Wikipedia entry) **[OBS]** **[INF]** Singer supplies the *direction* test — does the candidate value extend the same consideration to a wider set of sentient beings, or narrow it? Pinker supplies the *date* test; Singer supplies the *scope* test.

**(c) Haidt — where harm-based morality is itself the local variant.** The classic finding is cross-cultural and class-graded: "Results show that college students at elite universities judged these stories to be matters of social convention or of personal preference. Most other Ss, especially in Brazil, took a moralizing stance toward these actions. For these latter Ss, moral judgments were better predicted by affective reactions than by appraisals of harmfulness." (Haidt, Koller & Dias 1993, *JPSP* 65(4):613–628, peer-reviewed) **[OBS]** Haidt's own retrospective: "the moral domain in WEIRD cultures (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) is fairly narrow, comparatively speaking. It focuses on protecting individuals from harm and exploitation. In most traditional societies, however, the moral domain is broader, including concerns about protecting groups, institutions, traditions, and the moral order more generally." (Graham, Haidt et al. 2013, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, peer-reviewed) **[OBS]** On "moral dumbfounding" itself: 80% of subjects initially condemned the harmless-incest vignette and only 32% ended up permissive — but a later replication found the effect largely evaporates once you check whether subjects really believed it was harmless: "with the requisite manipulation checks on credulity and normative orientation factored in… giving us a moral dumbfounding estimate of 1/53 (1.88 percent)." (Royzman et al., *Judgment and Decision Making*, peer-reviewed) **[OBS]** And MFT itself does not travel cleanly: "we test the measurement invariance of the short-form Moral Foundations Questionnaire across 27 countries… We find that it is difficult to specify Moral Foundations Questionnaire items in a quantitative five-factor model that will converge nonproblematically across a wide variety of populations." (Iurino & Saucier 2018, *Assessment*, peer-reviewed) **[OBS]**

**[INF] Practical rule set for the constitution:** (1) *date* — post-1950 origin suggests a local norm (Pinker); (2) *scope* — does the rule widen or narrow the circle of equal consideration (Singer); (3) *cost* — is the norm one people have actually paid for, or one that is merely professed (Haidt's WEIRD narrowness vs. costly universalism in the protest data); (4) *invariance* — if the measure only works in WEIRD samples, treat the finding as a hypothesis, not a foundation.

## 4. Gaps and things I could not verify

- **Could not verify** any NAVCO count of campaigns *by goal* (how many regime change vs. anti-occupation vs. secession). I confirmed the goal categories (NAVCO 1.1/1.3: regime change, anti-occupation, secession, "other"; NAVCO 2.0 adds institutional reform, policy change, greater autonomy, unknown) and the totals (323 campaigns 1900–2006; 622 campaigns 1900–2019; 250 campaigns 1945–2006 = 100 nonviolent + 150 violent; 389 campaign-years' worth of mass movements 1945–2013), but not the goal breakdown. *Next step:* download NAVCO 1.3 from Harvard Dataverse (`doi:10.7910/DVN/ON9XND`) and tabulate the goal field directly.
- **ACLED does not appear to publish protester *demand* categories.** Its documented coding is actor / target / event type / sub-event type / disorder type ("Political violence", "Demonstrations", "Strategic developments"), plus the 2020+ conflict categories (Repression, Insurgency, Atrocities, Terrorist activity, Foreign military engagement). Treat "ACLED demand categories" as non-existent until someone produces a codebook field. *Next step:* confirm against the current ACLED Codebook PDF.
- **World Protests' Table 1 percentages are partly re-quoted.** I read the chapter text and several table rows verbatim, but some Table 1 figures (e.g. justice 410/14.6%, ethnic-racial 396/14.1%, environment 359/12.8%) reached me via a search-index excerpt of the same source, not a direct read. Re-check against the Springer table before publishing.
- **Mass Mobilization has no published headline "most common demand" percentage that I could verify.** The dataset codes up to four of seven demand categories per event, and the authors' own paper grades demands by *threat* (prices/taxes/labour/land/social restrictions = 1; political process/police brutality = 2; removal of officials = 3) rather than publishing a frequency ranking. *Next step:* compute the frequencies from the Dataverse file.
- **Pinker's and Singer's quotes** were taken from the book text I could read (Singer PDF; *Better Angels* full text mirror) and, for two Pinker/Singer lines, from quote-aggregator and Wikipedia pages that quote the books. Those two are flagged above as coming via secondary pages and should be re-verified against the print editions before being quoted in a constitution.
- **Welzel's "emancipative values" is contested.** I verified the index construction (autonomy, equality, choice, voice) and Welzel's claim that the values are "universal and not a Western-bound concept", but also that APSR published evidence of "misspecification of the measurement model for the index of emancipative values… the scale on which the index is measured is noninvariant across cultural zones and countries in the World Values Survey." Do not treat EVI as a clean cross-cultural measure without addressing that critique.
- **A structural caveat that matters more than any single number.** Observed protest is a function of *demand × feasibility*, not demand alone. The World Protests authors state it: "the trendline shows a tendency towards more protests when civic conditions are freer. As expected, repression works: countries where civic space is repressed or closed have fewer protests." (World Protests ch.2) **[OBS]** **[INF]** So behavioural data systematically under-sample exactly the case we care about most — people resisting *stronger* governments. Any "learn values from behaviour" constitution must correct for this, or it will infer that people under repression consent. Migration desire (Gallup) and emigration flows are the partial corrective, because exit is measurable even where voice is not.
