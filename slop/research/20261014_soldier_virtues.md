# Soldiers' virtues: eight descriptions across cultures

2026-10-14. Research only; no existing files edited. Read `AGENTS.md` and the soldier case in `constitution/v4.md.j2:241–252`. Downloaded sources are under `.local/papers/soldier_virtues_20261014/`.

## Finding and recommendation

**Inference.** The draft's central point is well supported as an account of a military ideal: soldiers must remain dependable when individual escape is tempting, and communities reinforce that dependability. But the texts describe more than courage enforced by punishment: steady leadership, training, affection for comrades, reciprocal care, public honours, and restraint also matter. None establishes that coercive punishment is necessary in every effective army, or that a stable army necessarily protects everyone.

**Recommend one block quote:** Polybius, *Histories* 6.24.8–9, translated from Greek by W. R. Paton (1923):

> They wish the centurions not so much to be venturesome and daredevil as to be natural leaders, of a steady and sedate spirit. They do not desire them so much to be men who will initiate attacks and open the battle, but men who will hold their ground when worsted and hard-pressed and be ready to die at their posts.

Source: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/6*.html#24 ; local `polybius6.txt:120` in the download directory above.

**Inference.** This is the best addition because it describes the *kind* of military courage, rather than just praising bravery or threatening cowardice: steadiness and dependable leadership, not reckless aggression. It speaks specifically about centurions, not every soldier. Keep the draft's refusal-of-wrongful-orders limit beside it; “die at their posts” is an ancient ideal, not an unconditional command for a modern constitution. Recommend the quotation, not Roman punishments. No constitution change is authorised or made here. Greek/Roman sources are not in the current held-out traditions (`scripts/eval/15_principle_judge.py:32–35`).

## What the draft actually says

**Quote — current draft, not historical evidence:**

> Soldier. A unit keeps its ground under fire only if each soldier trusts the others to stay. If every soldier had courage, honoured it in the others and punished deserters, units would keep their ground and the people behind them would be safe. If each ran when running paid, all would run. So a soldier needs more courage, under more pressure, than a baker does, and a kind the unit itself enforces. The same test limits it: if every soldier would do anything ordered, no one would be safe from their own army, so courage includes refusing unlawful orders.

Source: `constitution/v4.md.j2:242`.

The assessments below test that account. **Strength** distinguishes evidence for what a text teaches from evidence that real soldiers behaved that way. English translations are quoted verbatim; original-language texts are not independently collated. Line wrapping and HTML footnote markers are not part of the quotations.

## 1. Greek/Spartan: retaining the shield, and family-enforced honour

**Quote — Plutarch, *Sayings of Spartan Women*, anonymous saying 16, *Moralia* 241F:**

> Another, as she handed her son his shield, exhorted him, saying, "Either this or upon this."

**Translation/source:** Greek → English, Frank Cole Babbitt, Loeb *Moralia*, vol. III (1931). https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/Sayings_of_Spartan_Women*.html ; local `spartan_women.txt:98–99`.

**Source type and strength:** Ancient literary collection, compiled around the late first/early second century CE, looking back at Sparta. Strong evidence for the martial ideal Plutarch transmits; weak evidence that a particular Spartan mother actually said these words. The familiar “with your shield or on it” is **not** this edition's exact wording. Its translator's note also records disagreement over the second half's interpretation: falling upon the shield or being brought home upon it (`spartan_women.txt:210`).

**Inference — virtue:** Resolve and fidelity to one's fighting role, with honour preferred to ignominious survival.

**Inference — enforcement:** The speaker is a mother: household approval and shame reinforce the army's expectations. Enforcement therefore extends beyond the unit. Nearby stories include a mother killing a son reported to have been a coward (Damatria, *Moralia* 240F; local `spartan_women.txt:52`). That is a literary illustration of extreme coercion, not a recommendation.

**Inference — fit:** Strong on costly courage and social reinforcement; silent on lawful orders and mercy. As a standalone constitutional quote it risks teaching death-before-disgrace rather than discriminating courage. Less suitable than Polybius's steadiness passage.

## 2. Republican Rome: steadiness, collective punishment, and public reward

**Quote — Polybius, *Histories* 6.37.12:**

> Therefore the men in covering forces often face certain death, refusing to leave their ranks even when vastly outnumbered, owing to dread of the punishment they would meet with;

**Quote — 6.38.3, on collective abandonment of posts:**

> Those on whom the lot falls are bastinadoed mercilessly in the manner above described; the rest receive rations of barley instead of wheat and are ordered to encamp outside the camp on an unprotected spot.

**Quote — 6.39.6, the positive counterpart:**

> So also those who have shielded and saved any of the citizens or allies receive honorary gifts from the consul, and the men they saved crown their preservers, if not under their own free will under compulsion from the tribunes who judge the case.

**Translation/source:** Greek → English, W. R. Paton (1923). https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/6*.html#37 ; sections 38–39 on the same page; local `polybius6.txt:172–178`. The recommended centurion description is 6.24.8–9 above.

**Source type and strength:** Second-century BCE historical/institutional account by a Greek author closely acquainted with Rome. Strong descriptive evidence for the practices he reports, though not a controlled demonstration of their effectiveness. The online edition warns that its transcription has not yet been proofread.

**Inference — virtue:** Steadiness, reliable performance of assigned duties, honest claims to valour, and rescuing others. Courage is not simply eagerness to attack.

**Inference — enforcement:** *Fustuarium* is collective beating/stoning after a military trial, often fatal (6.37.1–4). For mass abandonment, roughly a tenth are selected by lot (6.38.2): the practice commonly called decimation. Public deprivation and exposure punish the remainder. Awards and public commendation reinforce bravery and rescue (6.39). These are distinct mechanisms, not one punishment casually called “decimation.”

**Inference — fit:** The closest direct support for the draft's punishment mechanism. But Polybius explicitly describes conduct motivated by *fear of punishment*. That is not proof of a good internal drive. Cruel coercion can produce dependable behaviour without virtue. The rescue award also shows that protecting comrades deserves its own place in the case.

## 3. Ancient China: a commander combines benevolence and strictness

**Quote — Sun Tzu, *The Art of War* I.9:**

> The Commander stands for the virtues of wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage and strictness.

**Quote — IX.43, first sentence:**

> Therefore soldiers must be treated in the first instance with humanity, but kept under control by means of iron discipline.

**Translation/source:** Chinese → English, Lionel Giles (1910). https://www.gutenberg.org/files/132/132-h/132-h.htm ; local `sun_tzu.txt:1261–1262,3114–3115`. These are translated text, not Giles's bracketed commentary.

**Source type and strength:** Ancient strategic treatise, conventionally associated with Sun Wu; authorship and dating are not settled by the traditional biography. Strong evidence for its prescribed leadership ideal, not a measured causal result.

**Inference — virtue:** Courage belongs in a larger set: judgement, trustworthiness, care, and consistency in enforcing standards. These are the commander's virtues, not a list addressed identically to every rank.

**Inference — enforcement:** IX.42 says punishment before soldiers become attached to their commander makes them ineffective, while attachment without enforcement also fails. I.13 asks which army has greater constancy in reward and punishment. Training and a trusted command relationship precede useful discipline; sanctions alone are inadequate.

**Inference — fit:** Supports pressure and discipline, but corrects the draft's one-sided emphasis on punishing deserters. Commanders owe humanity and competent leadership. It supplies no general refusal-of-unlawful-orders rule. **Hold-out caution:** Confucian virtues are held out in the judge. Do not import Giles's commentary equating the list with Chinese cardinal virtues into the constitution. This comparison is research, not a proposal to add that tradition.

## 4. India: the Gita's warrior duty, shame, and equanimity

**Quote — *Bhagavad Gita* 2.33–35, within the 2.31–38 argument:**

> But, if thou shunn'st
> This honourable field--a Kshattriya--
> If, knowing thy duty and thy task, thou bidd'st
> Duty and task go by--that shall be sin!
> And those to come shall speak thee infamy
> From age to age; but infamy is worse
> For men of noble blood to bear than death!
> The chiefs upon their battle-chariots
> Will deem 'twas fear that drove thee from the fray.
> Of those who held thee mighty-souled the scorn
> Thou must abide,

**Translation/source:** Sanskrit → English verse, Edwin Arnold, *The Song Celestial* (1885), chapter II. https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2388/2388-h/2388-h.htm ; local `gita.txt:379–390`. The final comma is retained because the excerpt ends within a sentence. Arnold's rendering is literary, not a word-for-word gloss; Gutenberg does not number individual verses.

**Source type and strength:** Religious-philosophical dialogue embedded in the *Mahabharata*, generally dated to the last centuries BCE/early centuries CE. Strong evidence for a classic account of warrior duty; not a report of military discipline in practice.

**Inference — virtue:** Performing a warrior's duty despite distress, and steadiness toward gain/loss and victory/defeat (2.38). The argument concerns the conflict presented as righteous here, not every war an employer orders.

**Inference — enforcement:** Sin, enduring infamy, the contempt of other commanders, and the promise of heaven or earthly victory. This passage stresses religious and reputational incentives, not a military court punishing deserters.

**Inference — fit:** Clear support for role-specific, higher-pressure duty. The problem is harder than ordinary fear: Arjuna is troubled by killing relatives and respected teachers. Do not treat every reluctance to fight as cowardice, or read Arnold's “lawful war” (2.31) as a modern law-of-war test. This is not an explicit unlawful-orders safeguard.

## 5. Japan: Nitobe's moral courage, not merely rushing toward death

**Quote — Inazō Nitobe, *Bushido: The Soul of Japan*, “Courage, the Spirit of Daring and Bearing”:**

> Courage was scarcely deemed worthy to be counted among virtues, unless it was exercised in the cause of Righteousness.

**Quote — same chapter:**

> Death for a cause unworthy of dying for, was called a “dog’s death.”

**Translation/source:** Originally written in English, first published around 1899–1900; the downloaded text includes the tenth/revised edition's preface. https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12096/12096-h/12096-h.htm ; local `nitobe.txt:561–563,572–573`.

**Source type and strength:** Modern Japanese ethical exposition for an international readership, not a medieval military code. Strong evidence for *Nitobe's* presentation of warrior ethics; weak evidence for a single uniform ethic governing all earlier samurai.

**Inference — virtue:** Moral courage and discrimination between worthy sacrifice and rashness. His discussion later treats benevolence and sympathy as major virtues, rather than opposites of warrior strength.

**Inference — enforcement:** Stories and example train children; mothers shame displays of weakness (same chapter, local `nitobe.txt:582–588`). Honour and fear of censure supply social pressure. Nitobe also warns that duty can degenerate into cowardly fear of censure (`nitobe.txt:550–558`).

**Inference — fit:** Particularly useful for separating courage from conformity and reckless death. It supports an ethical limit on martial courage, but does not state a modern legal rule about superior orders.

**Modern-invention caution:** Do not label this quotation “the ancient samurai code.” Nitobe explicitly says he writes in English for foreign readers, uses European parallels, and draws on what he was taught in youth (preface, local `nitobe.txt:68–88`). That is a modern retrospective synthesis. Neither “unchanged since antiquity” nor “there were no earlier warrior ethics” follows from it. It is not interchangeable with *Hagakure*.

## 6. Early Islam: no flight, but explicit tactical exceptions

**Quote — Qur'an 8:15:**

> O ye who believe! When ye meet those who disbelieve in battle, turn not your backs to them.

**Quote — 8:16:**

> Whoso on that day turneth his back to them, unless manoeuvring for battle or intent to join a company, he truly hath incurred wrath from Allah, and his habitation will be hell, a hapless journey's end.

**Quote — 8:61, a restraint within the same surah:**

> And if they incline to peace, incline thou also to it, and trust in Allah. Lo! He, even He, is the Hearer, the Knower.

**Translation/source:** Arabic → English, Marmaduke Pickthall (1930). Verified translation text and attribution in https://api.alquran.cloud/v1/ayah/8:15/en.pickthall ; https://api.alquran.cloud/v1/ayah/8:16/en.pickthall ; https://api.alquran.cloud/v1/ayah/8:61/en.pickthall . Local `quran15.json`, `quran16.json`, `quran61.json`, each line 1.

**Source type and strength:** Seventh-century religious scripture, reproduced by a translation API. Strong evidence for the injunction and its stated exceptions; not evidence of a particular army's enforcement procedures. This is scripture, not a *furusiyya* manual or a hadith.

**Inference — virtue:** Fidelity and steadfastness in battle, without forbidding manoeuvre or regrouping. The peace verse rules out presenting this surah as a simple injunction to fight without limit.

**Inference — enforcement:** Divine wrath and punishment after death. These verses do not prescribe the unit's punishment of deserters.

**Inference — fit:** Supports costly steadfastness while exposing an omission: retreat is not automatically desertion. No modern unlawful-orders rule is stated. The battle context must remain explicit; the translated words are not a licence for indiscriminate violence against nonbelievers.

## 7. Fourteenth-century Maghreb: Ibn Khaldun's solidarity and mutual aid

**Quote — Ibn Khaldun, *Muqaddimah*, II.7:**

> Compassion and affection for one's blood relations and relatives exist in human nature as something God put into the hearts of men. It makes for mutual support and aid, and increases the fear felt by the enemy.

**Quote — II.8, extending the account beyond blood relationship:**

> Clients and allies belong in the same category. The affection everybody has for his clients and allies results from the feeling of shame that comes to a person when one of his neighbors, relatives, or a blood relation in any degree (of kinship) is humiliated.

**Translation/source:** Arabic → English, Franz Rosenthal (1958). https://www.muslimphilosophy.com/ik/Muqaddimah/Chapter2/Ch_2_07.htm ; https://www.muslimphilosophy.com/ik/Muqaddimah/Chapter2/Ch_2_08.htm . Translator identified on https://www.muslimphilosophy.com/ik/Muqaddimah/ . Local `ibn_khaldun7.txt:48–51`, `ibn_khaldun.txt:21–24`.

**Source type and strength:** Historical/social theory, first composed in 1377, discussing tribal defence and political power. Strong evidence for Ibn Khaldun's proposed mechanism; not experimental proof or a modern professional soldier's code. The website is a transcription of Rosenthal's translation.

**Inference — virtue:** Solidarity (*ʿasabiyya*, rendered “group feeling”), protective affection, mutual aid. II.7 contrasts this with an isolated fighter who flees because he expects no support.

**Inference — enforcement:** Shame at failing one's relations/allies, leaders' social authority, and reciprocal support. It is not mainly a threat that fellow soldiers will punish flight.

**Inference — fit:** Very close to the draft's trust mechanism, but it explains courage through attachment and confidence that others will help. Soldiers must be dependable *to* one another, not merely dependable to their commander. Kin-based solidarity can also exclude outsiders or sustain predatory power; solidarity alone does not pass the constitution's wider-circle test.

## 8. Modern international law: personal responsibility and humane treatment

**Quote — Nuremberg Principles (1950), Principle IV:**

> The fact that a person acted pursuant to order of his Government or of a superior does not relieve him from responsibility under international law, provided a moral choice was in fact possible to him.

**Source/translation:** Original English in the UN International Law Commission's formulation. https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/draft_articles/7_1_1950.pdf ; local `nuremberg.txt:39–43`.

**Quote — Third Geneva Convention (1949), Article 13, first sentence:**

> Prisoners of war must at all times be humanely treated.

**Source/translation:** Authoritative English treaty text reproduced by Yale's Avalon Project. https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/geneva03.asp ; local `geneva3.txt:233`.

**Source type and strength:** Official legal principles and treaty obligations, not literary descriptions or behavioural studies. Very strong evidence for the stated legal standards. The Nuremberg Principles formulate principles recognised in the Tribunal's Charter and judgment; they are not themselves a separate treaty.

**Inference — virtue:** Moral independence, accountable judgement, restraint toward the defeated, and humanity. Law gives these obligations a more definite institutional form than exhortations to honour alone.

**Inference — enforcement:** Individual criminal responsibility, not merely comrades' approval. Geneva III Article 129 obliges states to search for alleged perpetrators of grave breaches and prosecute or hand them over; Article 130 identifies the grave breaches. Not every breach of Article 13 automatically falls into that category.

**Inference — fit:** The strongest source here for the draft's limit on obedience. Principle IV is specifically about responsibility for international crimes and includes the “moral choice” condition; it is not an exhaustive operational rule for every unlawful order. Humane treatment is an independent duty, not a discretionary reward for an honourable enemy.

## What the case misses

These are **inferences/recommendations**, not proposed verbatim replacement text:

1. **Dependability is trained and reciprocal.** Discipline includes preparation, competence, clear orders, and dependable command. Commanders owe care; courage is not produced only by penalties. Sun Tzu and Ibn Khaldun make this especially clear.
2. **Distinguish steadfastness from recklessness.** Holding an assigned position can be courageous; needless attack or futile persistence can be bad judgement. Polybius's centurion ideal expressly rejects daredevil leadership.
3. **Protect and reward comrades.** Rescue, loyalty, mutual aid, and caring for the wounded help sustain the trust the draft depends on. Polybius records honours for saving others.
4. **Retreat, surrender, incapacity, and refusal are not all desertion.** Qur'an 8:16 explicitly permits manoeuvre and regrouping. The draft should not make “all stay” a universal tactical rule or shame every survival decision.
5. **Sanctions need moral limits and proper authority.** Historical stoning, killing by lot, and family killing show coercive enforcement, not acceptable constitutional enforcement. “The unit itself enforces” should not imply vigilantism. Fear-driven compliance must not be confused with good character.
6. **Include humanity toward the defeated.** POW protection and care for those out of combat belong beside loyalty to comrades. The army's victims and opponents are affected people too. Mercy cannot merely depend on whether the enemy is admired.
7. **The war's purpose matters.** An internally cohesive army can invade, terrorise, or support a tyrant. The people behind it being safer is insufficient if wider circles are harmed. The current inference from universal soldier courage to general safety is too quick.
8. **Keep ethical judgement wider than legality.** The soldier sentence says “unlawful orders,” but the surrounding draft says no role can require a vice (`constitution/v4.md.j2:248–252`). A lawful but unjust order can still fail that wider rule. Refusing it may require courage against the very peer pressure that normally sustains the unit.
9. **Qualify the baker comparison.** These sources support specialised exposure to danger and organised pressure. They do not measure “more courage than a baker.” Ordinary baking and battlefield duty normally demand different levels and kinds of pressure; neither occupation fixes an individual's courage in every circumstance.
