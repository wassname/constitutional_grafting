# Western values: prevalence, not exemplary stories

2026-10-14. Research only. Read `AGENTS.md`, the spec's opening, and both `20261014_western_values_behaviour` reports before searching. No existing repository files edited. Downloads, source extracts, rejected fetches and checks are in `.local/papers/prevalence_20261014/`.

## Result

**Inference — my best guess.** The strongest historical prior is for **personal and household independence, learning beyond one's kin, and rule-bound local cooperation** in northwestern Europe, subsequently spreading more widely. Marriage and employment records establish population patterns, not just unusual heroes. They do not establish that most medieval Europeans favoured universal free speech or equal religious liberty. For those, Durant describes a later, contested development—and widespread contrary preferences.

**Inference.** Durant helps identify the enduring commitments and their opposition; historical demography supplies the denominators. Neither can give a percentage of people who valued an abstract freedom from these records alone. A strong prior need not mean near-unanimity. The relevant comparison is a behaviour under available opportunities, not the imagined percentage who would choose it under identical circumstances.

**Source strength:** **strong** for a clearly defined recorded pattern; **moderate** for historical reconstruction or scholarly synthesis; **weak** for an inference about motives, a poorly matched comparison, or an unchecked mediated number. These describe support for the specified claim, not the author's prestige. Data transcriptions below are **reported estimates**, not verbatim quotations. Blockquotes are **quotes**; interpretations are **inferences**.

### 1. Liberty constrained by law, not unlimited self-assertion

**Quote — F:6210, on Edward I and the development of Parliament:**

> England’s finest achievement—the reconciliation, in government and character, of liberty and law.

**Quote — L:438:**

> Since men love freedom, and the freedom of individuals in society requires some regulation of conduct, the first condition of freedom is its limitation; make it absolute and it dies in chaos.

**Inference.** The distinctive achievement is reconciling personal liberty with enforceable common rules. Durant does not equate liberty with escaping every obligation. His account of Parliament connects its expansion to actual bargaining over revenue and military support, not a population suddenly adopting a philosopher's programme (`F:6219`).

**Quote — F:6204, his limitation on Magna Carta:**

> the Charter was a victory for feudalism rather than for democracy.

**Quote — F:6219, on the 1295 Parliament:**

> These men were chosen by the more substantial citizens in each locality; no one dreamed of universal suffrage in a society where only a minority could read.

**Inference.** Consent initially meant selected estates and substantial citizens, not every person. Do not turn the charter into a count of universal liberty supporters. Durant's further claim at F:6204 that it immediately established habeas corpus and trial by jury is an overly compressed retrospective genealogy; I would not copy it as an exact account of 1215 legal remedies.

### 2. Local self-government, but a minority's political power

**Quote — F:5946, on the Italian communes:**

> Though the administrative councils soon narrowed their membership to a mercantile aristocracy, the municipal assemblies were the first representative government since Tiberius; they, rather than Magna Carta, were the chief parent of modern democracy.

**Inference.** This is his summary of a durable institutional lineage, including its oligarchic narrowing. The sweeping “first” is his historical judgment, not a freshly verified global comparison. Its population reach must come from urban demography, not from the grandeur of the description.

### 3. Renaissance individualism was not everyone's household autonomy

**Quote — R:704:**

> the educated Italian shook himself loose from intellectual and ethical restraints

**Quote — R:3947:**

> Rarely did any member marry or leave the state without the family’s consent.

**Inference.** Durant explicitly locates the intellectual liberation in the educated stratum. In the same book, family authority remains powerful. The Renaissance cannot establish a prior that most Italians already enjoyed, or sought, modern autonomous spouse choice. He also judges its individualism unfavourably against medieval communal spirit (`R:4058`): his endorsement is not unconditional.

### 4. Freedom of conscience emerged against widespread intolerance

**Quote — Re:4972, on Knox:**

> he voiced with hard consistency the almost universal spirit of the time.

**Quote — Re:7303, the book's concluding lesson:**

> a religion is at its best when it must live with competition; it tends to intolerance when and where it is unchallenged and supreme.

**Inference.** The first passage concerns sixteenth-century intolerance; the second identifies competition among faiths as a lasting gain. I am not quoting the staged Catholic, Protestant and humanist speeches immediately before the conclusion as if each were Durant's settled position. He explicitly compares Suleiman's religious toleration favourably with contemporary Christian rulers (`Re:5769`). Tolerance is not an exclusively Western discovery.

**Quote — V:1327, on early Georgian England:**

> these laws, however, were seldom enforced.

**Inference.** The surrounding passage describes compulsory Anglican attendance and anti-Catholic disabilities, then their uneven enforcement. Statutory penalties alone cannot establish how costly every dissenter's practice was. Conversely, legal conformity under threat is not an affirmative vote for the established creed.

### 5. Durant's clearest summary of the enduring modern commitments

**Quote — N:7742:**

> the hunger for freedom —of movement, growth, enterprise, worship, thought, speech, and press; and the hunger for equality—of access to opportunity, education, health, and legal justice.

**Inference.** Durant calls the post-1789 revolution in rights more lasting than Napoleon. He describes liberty as the recurrent nineteenth-century theme and equality as the dominant twentieth-century theme in Europe and America. This is a clear answer to *which commitments endured*, but not a poll estimating their individual support. It also identifies a competing value, equality, rather than contrasting liberty only with wickedness.

**Quote — L:476:**

> It gave to thought and science and enterprise the freedom essential to their operation and growth.

**Inference.** “It” is democracy. Durant ultimately favours its achievements despite the criticisms immediately before this passage. But he does not describe it as historically normal:

**Quote — L:440:**

> If we were to judge forms of government from their prevalence and duration in history we should have to give the palm to monarchy; democracies, by contrast, have been hectic interludes.

**Inference.** Durant supports a historically cultivated, geographically uneven Western tradition, not a timeless unanimous instinct for liberal democracy. His account includes strong demand for order, family authority, religion and material security. Some of his demographic, racial and biological generalizations are dated or conjectural; age and literary authority do not make them reliable numerical priors.

## B. Population patterns and their opposites

### B1. Marriage: the clearest population comparison

**Source:** Voigtländer and Voth, *How the West “Invented” Fertility Restriction*, NBER WP 17314, revised 2012; published in *American Economic Review* 103(6), 2013. [Full working paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17314/w17314.pdf). Local `voth.txt`. **Type:** historical economic research synthesizing demographic reconstructions, plus a causal model. **Strength:** strong/moderate for demographic pattern; weaker for its precise causal explanation and for political motives.

| Reported estimate | Period and denominator | Opposite / comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Women's mean first marriage: **England 25; France 24.6; Belgium 25; Germany 26.4; Scandinavia 26.7 years** | **17th century**, women entering first marriage; Table 1, printed p. 34 (`voth.txt:1601–1611`). | Chinese women averaged **17.5** in the early 20th century; Qing imperial-family women in Beijing **15.5–19** in the 17th century, rising to **22** by 1840 (§3, printed p. 22). Elite Chinese and broad European estimates are not fully matched samples. |
| **17.5% of English women never married** | Broad **early-modern** reconstruction summarized in §2, p. 6; no single census year supplied there. | China: **1% in Liaoning to 4% in Beijing unmarried at 30**, **1640–1870**, p. 22. This is **not** a lifetime-never-married denominator. |
| **15–25% unmarried at 30** in northwestern Europe | Late-medieval/early-modern pattern summarized by Henrich, `H:1526–1532`. | Henrich gives **1–2%** for traditional China at the **same age**, `H:1532`; the more specific research summary above permits a wider **1–4%** regional range. |

**Quote — Voigtländer–Voth, §2, printed p. 6; local `voth.txt:287–290`:**

> the European Marriage Pattern, with the age at first marriage postponed to 25 or beyond, only emerged after the Black Death, and the pattern was strongest in Northwestern Europe

**Inference.** The late-marriage pattern is common behaviour, not a series of rare elopements. The Hajnal line is the conventional **St Petersburg–Trieste** division (paper, p. 1), not a precise boundary shared by every village or century. Do not project the pattern onto all Western antiquity: the same paper gives medieval Lincolnshire women's first marriage around **20**, and Western Europe around **18–19** circa 500 CE. Henrich's heading “Many women never marry” must not cause his *age-30* statistic to be misreported as *lifelong* non-marriage.

**Inference — value:** substantial room, and economic need, for independent establishment and marriage timing. **Not proved:** every late marriage was freely preferred; every unmarried woman rejected family authority; a majority favoured unrestricted spouse choice. Poverty, service contracts and household formation costs also selected the observed behaviour. The counter-rate is meaningful even without treating Chinese early marriage as involuntary in every case.

### B2. Servants in husbandry: independence extended well beyond elites

**Reported estimates — same paper, §2, printed p. 7, footnote 13 (`voth.txt:327–343`), citing Kussmaul (1981) and Hajnal (1982):**

- Up to **half of English boys and girls aged 15–25** worked as servants.
- Women aged **20–24** in early-modern samples: **Denmark 51%; Iceland 44%; Holland 36%; England 40%**. **Norway 33%** is for **both sexes**, not women alone.
- **65% of servants** married immediately before or after the end of their contracts.

**Quote — paper, p. 7:**

> Marriage typically implied an immediate termination of service.

**Type:** historical demography and labour history, mediated through an economic study. **Strength:** moderate/strong for service being a common life stage; precise sample years must be recovered from Hajnal before describing these as synchronized national censuses. “Servants” is the paper's category; do not assume each person was exclusively an agricultural servant.

**Inference — value:** living and working outside the natal family, accumulating resources and learning from unrelated adults were ordinary possibilities for a large minority of young adults. Service also imposed dependence on an employer. **Opposite:** Henrich describes life-cycle service by nonrelatives as globally unusual (`H:1534`) and contrasts corporate clan organization in China (`H:1522`), but I did **not** obtain an equivalent Chinese or Indian age-specific servant percentage. Absence of that estimate is not a zero rate.

### B3. Books and literacy: growing mass demand, with large regional differences

**Source:** Buringh and van Zanden, “Charting the ‘Rise of the West’: Manuscripts and Printed Books in Europe,” *Journal of Economic History* 69(2), **2009**, 409–445, DOI [10.1017/S0022050709000837](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050709000837). Read the [author-hosted full manuscript](https://www.iisg.nl/bibliometrics/books500-1800.pdf), local `books_iisg_pdf.txt`; it has its own pagination/tables. Publisher metadata checked [here](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jechis/v69y2009i02p409-445_00.html). **Type:** quantitative historical reconstruction from surviving manuscripts, editions and inferred print runs. **Strength:** moderate for levels, strong for the large long-run increase; not administrative counts of purchases by unique readers.

**Data transcription — manuscript Table 4, printed p. 45: estimated annual printed-book consumption per 1,000 inhabitants.**

| Region | 1454–1500 | 1601–1650 | 1751–1800 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Great Britain | 2.0 | 80.0 | **192.0** |
| Netherlands | 7.9 | 139.0 | **488.3** |
| France | 3.2 | 52.2 | **117.9** |
| Italy | 6.8 | 42.1 | **86.5** |
| Spain | 0.9 | 8.8 | **28.3** |
| Poland | 0.0 | 5.7 | **22.5** |
| Russia | 0 | 0.0 | **5.8** |
| Their “Western Europe,” excluding Russia | 3.1 | 40.6 | **122.4** |

**Inference — arithmetic:** late-18th-century British consumption was about **33 times** Russia's; Dutch consumption about **84 times**. This says books per person, **not** “19.2% of Britons bought a book.” Some people bought many; institutional libraries served others. Manuscript Table 3 measures manuscripts **per century** per 1,000, whereas Table 4 measures printed books **annually**: do not compare their raw entries as equal time units.

**Quote — manuscript p. 11 (`books_iisg_pdf.txt:553–557`):**

> somewhat more than 12,000 manuscripts per century (or 120 per year) from 500 to 700

> more than one billion books published during the eighteenth century

**Inference — comparison beyond Europe:** manuscript pp. 29–30 estimate surviving evidence for China at **27–47 new titles annually** in overlapping 16th/17th-century periods, versus about **3,750 European titles annually in 1522–1644**. These are **titles**, not copies or readers. Their Chinese survival correction is uncertain. Their prose's “about 40 times” cannot be reproduced by simply dividing 3,750 by 47, or by multiplying 47 by ten first; I would **not** use that headline ratio. China had substantial commercial printing; Japan is also identified as a serious comparator. No matched copies-per-capita series for China or India was established here.

**Literacy distinction.** The manuscript's Table 9, p. 50, gives **model-derived** 1701–1800 literacy estimates: Britain **54%**, Netherlands **85%**, France **29%**, Germany **38%**, Italy **23%**, Spain **8%**, Poland **5%**, aggregate **31%**. Its comparison column with Allen's 1800 estimates gives Spain **20%**, Poland **21%**, Belgium **49%** versus its own Belgian **13%**. These disagreements are large. Literacy is inferred from books, prices and a demand elasticity, not independently counted; book consumption and this literacy table are **not two independent confirmations**. Henrich's broader claim that half or more could read in selected northern countries around 1750 (`H:311`) should not erase those differences or make the book model a census.

**Inference — value:** learning and personal access to texts became increasingly common investments, not merely intellectual rhetoric. Religious instruction, commerce, price reductions and schooling also explain demand. The data are stronger for valuing access to knowledge than for wanting unrestricted publication of opponents' views.

### B4. Self-governing towns: use the right denominator

**Reported estimates:** Henrich `H:2297–2301`: under **3%** urban in 800; over **13%** in 1600; Netherlands/Belgium over **30%** by 1400. Urban means settlements above **1,000**, not 10,000. Figure caption `H:2300` specifies the overall western-European curve **excludes Iberia**. By **1500**, he says **most cities** were at least partially self-governing.

**Quote — H:2301:**

> By 1500, most cities in western Europe were at least partially self-governing.

**Type:** scholarly demographic/institutional synthesis. **Strength:** moderate for scale and institutional diffusion; insufficient for a precise population percentage enjoying municipal self-government.

**Inference.** A majority of **cities** is not a majority of **people**. Town size, citizenship exclusions, urban oligarchies and towns' rural jurisdictions prevent converting those two series into a single population share. The tempting calculation “13% urban × 90% representative = 11.7% under consent” is invalid: Henrich's **over-90%** is a fitted adoption probability after six centuries of Church exposure (`H:2325`), not the contemporaneous population-weighted proportion of towns. **I did not obtain a defensible overall share of population under charters.** This requested cell remains a gap, rather than a fabricated rate.

**Opposite:** Henrich's same paragraph says no Chinese or Islamic city developed self-government **based on representative assemblies** in this era. That is his categorical institutional comparison, not zero local organization, consultation, guild activity or informal autonomy. Bosker–Buringh–van Zanden (2013), [research abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v95y2013i4p1418-1437.html), studies local participative government using city data; its full original dataset was not obtained here.

**Inference — value:** long-running institutions for local consent and associational responsibility, concentrated in a growing urban minority. Does not establish that most inhabitants of the whole West then preferred national democracy.

### B5. Serfdom: the West/East divergence was real, not uninterrupted progress

**Type:** historians' synthesis for chronology; police/statistical records reconstructed by economic historians for the Russian rate. **Strength:** moderate for broad sequence; strong/moderate for the defined 1858 Russian denominator; weak as a direct measure of subjects' preferences.

- **England circa 1307:** Durant estimates **three quarters** peasants, **half of those** serfs (`Re:626`): **37.5% of the whole population**, *my multiplication of two rough estimates*, not an independently measured national serf census.
- **England circa 1715:** Durant reports no remaining serfdom (`V:642`). Broad disappearance belongs mainly to the late-medieval/early-modern period; do not read a modern free-labour society back into 1300.
- **Northern/eastern German territories:** Durant lists legal enserfment in Pomerania, Brandenburg, Schleswig, Holstein and Mecklenburg **in or soon after 1616** (`Reason:4247`). The standard West/East-of-Elbe contrast is a regional shorthand, not an exact frontier at which every labour regime changed. These examples show that “Europe ended serfdom” hides serious reversals.
- **Russia, 1858:** **22,546,732 private serfs / 52,392,030 rural residents = 43.03%**, in European Russia **excluding Poland and Finland**. State peasants **40.4%**, free agricultural population **12.6%**, royal peasants **4%**. **Private serfdom abolished in 1861**. Markevich and Zhuravskaya, [2015 working paper](https://www.cepremap.fr/depot/docweb/docweb1502.pdf), §2.2, printed p. 8; local `russia_cepremap_pdf.txt:359–365`. Published version: *AER* 108(4–5), 2018, [DOI 10.1257/aer.20160144](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160144). The working paper draws the 1858 figures from Bushen (1863).

**Quote — Markevich–Zhuravskaya, §2.2 (`russia_cepremap_pdf.txt:369–372`):**

> By law, they had personal and property rights and could change their occupation and place of living.

> The required administrative procedure for moving was so complicated, however, that few actually did this.

**Inference.** These sentences concern **state peasants**, not private serfs. The complement of 43.03% was not unrestricted independence. The late-western versus late-eastern comparison is compelling for actual labour relations, but the cited snapshots are not a synchronized 1715 census of all Europe. Abolition dates measure legal status; labour dues and mobility constraints could persist afterward.

**Numerical caution:** Durant gives French serfdom **6% of population by 1774** (`RR:679`), yet also **300,000 in 1789** (`V:2675`), following an earlier estimate below one million. I did not reconcile the categories or sources. Do **not** promote these to a clean French national trend without checking the underlying historians.

**Inference — value:** the decline of legally inherited subjection is consistent with a growing tradition of personal freedom, reinforced by exit, labour bargaining and commercialization. It also reveals landlords' strong contrary preference for retaining control. A serf's failure to escape is not preference for servitude, and a landlord's acceptance of paid labour need not be altruism.

### B6. Migration: repeated population-scale action, not a pure liberty measure

**Source:** Hatton and Williamson, *What Drove the Mass Migrations from Europe in the Late Nineteenth Century?*, NBER Historical Paper **43**, **1992**; later *Population and Development Review* 20(3), 1994. [Original PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/h0043/h0043.pdf), **Table 1, PDF p. 35**. **Type:** historical flow statistics and econometric research. **Strength:** strong/moderate for recorded movement; weak for isolating political liberty as motive. PDF is scanned: I visually read the table, introduction, interpretation and appendix images, not nonexistent text-layer OCR.

**Data transcription — annual gross emigrants per 1,000 population, averaged across each period:**

| Sending population | 1850–59 | 1880–89 | 1900–13 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Ireland | **18.99** | **16.04** | **7.93** |
| Great Britain, excluding Ireland | **4.38** | **5.71** | **7.08** |
| Sweden | **0.51** | **8.25** | **4.49** |
| Italy | unavailable | **6.09** | **17.97** |
| France | unavailable | **0.29** | **0.15** |
| Germany | **1.80** | **2.91** | **0.43** |

**Inference — unit conversion:** Ireland's 18.99 per thousand is **1.899% annually**, not 18.99% each decade. Table values are means of annual rates; do not sum them into unique persons or assume everyone who left stayed away. Italy's **1900–13 net** rate is **13.01** per thousand versus **17.97 gross**. France's gross rate is below **0.3 per thousand** in every reported decade, a useful contrary case **inside** the West. The appendix specifies extra-European departures where available, discusses coverage differences, and computes net migration by subtracting immigration—not simply by counting returnees.

**Quote — paper, printed p. 1 / PDF p. 3:**

> Between 1850 and 1913, more than 40 million people emigrated from Europe to the New World.

**Quote — paper, printed p. 3 / PDF p. 5:**

> emigration responded systematically to real wages gaps between home and abroad

**Inference — value:** millions accepted separation and relocation for a better future. That supports opportunity-seeking and the practical importance of exit. Wage gaps, demographics, passage finance and existing migrant networks matter; the paper is not an income-controlled estimate of willingness to purchase speech rights. No matching Chinese/Indian annual emigration rate was verified. France's low rate also blocks “Western values imply high emigration” as a general rule.

### B7. Non-kin training: the baseline is broad, the Asian reversal is not counted

**Reported estimates — H:3094:** **four fifths** of apprentices in a medieval Netherlands guild database were **not the master's sons**; **72–93%** of artisans in **17th-century London** trained under **nonrelatives**. These are denominators of apprentices/artisans in particular records, not the whole population.

**Quote — H:3094:**

> Hard data are not plentiful

> the rates were likely flipped

**Type:** scholarly synthesis of apprenticeship records and comparative craft histories. **Strength:** moderate for European percentages; weaker for the quantified cross-cultural contrast. The second excerpt concerns India/China. Henrich does **not** provide a matching measured percentage in that sentence. Do not manufacture an Asian **7–28%** non-kin rate by reversing the London range. Likewise **not one's son** does not necessarily mean **not a relative**.

**Inference — value:** access to skills beyond hereditary family transmission was routine among the sampled craftspeople, supporting personal development and chosen economic association. Guild barriers, employer power and parents contracting for children remain constraints. This is the numerical baseline already mentioned in the preceding report, retained here only to make the comparison and missing denominator explicit.

### B8. Costly religious dissent: minority persistence, not population-wide toleration

**1676 Compton Census of England/Wales:** the summary reports Anglican:Nonconformist **23:1**, Anglican:Catholic **179:1**, and combined Protestant:Catholic **187:1**. **My approximate conversion**, treating the groups as exhaustive and using the first two ratios: **95.3% Anglican, 4.1% Nonconformist, 0.53% Catholic**. These are proportions implied by reported ecclesiastical estimates, **not a freshly counted individual attendance census**.

**Source:** [Compton Census summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compton_Census), citing Edward Carpenter, *The Protestant Bishop* (1956), pp. 31–32, and identifying Anne Whiteman's *The Compton Census of 1676: A Critical Edition* (1986). **Type:** tertiary summary of an original clerical return; **strength weak/moderate**, pending the critical edition. I obtained the summary, not the original parish totals; a reliable absolute recusant count remains unverified.

**Quote — census instruction as reproduced in that summary:**

> what number of other Dissenters are resident in such parishes, which either obstinately refuse, or wholly absent themselves from, the Communion of the Church of England at such time as by law they are required?

**Inference.** This explicitly asks about refusal/absence under a requirement, so it is a better lead than modern affiliation surveys. But clergy estimated persons **or families**, inclusion practices varied, concealed Catholics could be missed, and the census was commissioned to demonstrate dissenters' numerical weakness. Do not infer **95.3% voluntary approval** from recorded Anglican conformity, nor that each dissenter actually paid the maximum statutory fine.

**Comparison:** Durant estimates the **Established Church in Ireland** included only **about one seventh** of the population in his early-18th-century account (`V:1235`), despite collecting tithes from a largely Catholic peasantry. These are different dates and source methods, not a synchronized two-country survey; nevertheless they show that state religious monopoly could coincide with either majority or minority adherence. No comparable non-Western costly-dissent rate was verified. Costly fidelity to one's faith is not willingness to permit others' faiths.

## C. Candidate constitution paragraph — inference, 110 words, not approved text

The prose and blockquote below together are the candidate; attribution and citations following it are outside its word count.

Seventeenth-century English women first married at about 25; early-modern estimates put lifelong non-marriage at 17.5%. Around 40% of English women aged 20–24 worked as servants in early-modern samples. In seventeenth-century London, 72–93% of artisans trained under nonrelatives. These common practices make me give personal independence, learning and chosen association substantial Western priors—not merely because famous rebels praised them. Durant calls England’s achievement:

> the reconciliation, in government and character, of liberty and law.

I therefore include rule-bound government and consent. Freedom of thought and conscience deserve weight too, but intolerance, inherited subjection and exclusion were widespread. These are cultivated, uneven commitments, not certainty about each person or permission to impose them.

**Quote attribution:** Will Durant, *The Age of Faith*, **F:6210**. Numerical sources: B1, B2, B7. **Source strength:** moderate historical interpretation, population-pattern evidence stronger than its motivational interpretation. The candidate deliberately avoids a fictitious percentage favouring universal liberty.

## Manual checks and residual risks

**Manual notes.** All 26 blockquotes matched a local source text after whitespace/Markdown normalization or, for two migration excerpts, the visually inspected original scan. The candidate is 110 words including its blockquote, with none of the four prohibited words. Checks are saved in `quote_validation.json`. Three checks materially narrow the result: marriage at 30 is not lifelong non-marriage; book-derived literacy is not independent confirmation of book demand; city adoption probabilities cannot become population charter coverage. The verified full texts are the Voth paper, IISH book manuscript, CEPREMAP serfdom paper and NBER **h0043**, not the initially guessed NBER numbers.

**Residual risks — inference:**

- The requested overall population share under self-governing charters and primary absolute recusancy counts remain missing. Servant and apprenticeship comparison percentages for China/India were not obtained. I have marked gaps rather than treating missing rates as zero.
- Historical behaviours bundle incentives, institutions and preferences. They establish common practices, not a unique psychological motive or the percentage willing to protect opponents.
- Marriage regional boundaries, dates and samples differ; some Chinese figures describe imperial elites. Service counts need their original census years before strict cross-country comparison.
- Printed-book consumption and literacy are reconstructed. Early manuscript estimates may be wrong by factors of two or three; print-run assumptions, survival and international book trade affect later levels. The author manuscript was inspected rather than a separately verified final publisher PDF.
- Durant is a valuable long-view interpreter but not a representative sample estimator. His French serf counts require reconciliation; his broad institutional firsts and some biological generalizations should not be copied uncritically.
- Compton's reported ratios are only tertiary-verified here. Coercion, concealment and clerical categorization can bias them sharply. No belief-freedom percentage is constitution-ready on that basis.
- No training, model answers or judge verdicts were produced. Demo prompts/model panel: **not run** for base, grafted, graft 2x, graft 4x, graft+SFT, healed, and SFT-only; a research-only report has no generated responses to inspect.
