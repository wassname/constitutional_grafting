# Research: Which values are distinctively Western, and child sexual abuse as a recent taboo — an evidence-first brief

**Scope note.** Everything below is quote-first: verbatim text with a sentence of context on either side, URL, author, source type. `[OBS]` = the source states this directly. `[INF]` = my inference or paraphrase from the source. Web search was flaky (SearXNG returned empty for most queries); I fetched primary pages and PDFs directly and read the extracted text locally.

---

## Summary

**(A)** The cross-cultural evidence supports a narrower claim than "autonomy and free speech are Western values." It supports: *the West is a statistical outlier, not a separate moral species.* WEIRD populations are at the extreme tail on individualism, analytic cognition, impersonal fairness toward strangers, and — critically for a constitution — on **relying on a single moral foundation (justice/rights/harm) rather than the fuller three-ethic or five-foundation repertoire.** Support for free expression in principle is near-universal (~90% average across 33 countries); what is distinctively Western is the *intensity* and the willingness to tolerate *offensive* speech. **[OBS]**

**(B)** Child sexual abuse is not a "recent taboo" in the sense of a new moral category; the *criminalisation of it as a distinct offence with a legislated age threshold*, and near-universal treaty coverage, is recent and is a strong behavioural signal. Article 34 of the CRC, 178 states party to the 2000 Optional Protocol, and ICMEC's finding that sufficient anti-CSAM legislation rose from 27 countries (2006) to 138 (2023) are the best hard numbers. **[OBS]** The honest caveat: anthropologists do **not** find a universal prohibition on all adult–child sexual contact; they find near-universal prohibition of incest and of sex with *prepubescent* children, with real historical variation for pubescent/adolescent cases (ancient Greece, Rome, Melanesian ritual initiation). **[OBS]**

## A. Western values, cross-cultural evidence

### A1. Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan 2010 — "The weirdest people in the world?"
*Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 33(2–3):61–83. Author: Henrich, Heine, Norenzayan (UBC). Source type: peer-reviewed target article + open commentary.

The paper's abstract frames the entire claim as a *distributional* one, not a categorical one. It states: "Here, our review of the comparative database from across the behavioral sciences suggests both that there is substantial variability in experimental results across populations and that WEIRD subjects are particularly unusual compared with the rest of the species – frequent outliers." The abstract then enumerates the domains and delivers the headline: "The findings suggest that members of WEIRD societies, including young children, are among the least representative populations one could find for generalizing about humans." The listed domains are "visual perception, fairness, cooperation, spatial reasoning, categorization and inferential induction, moral reasoning, reasoning styles, self-concepts and related motivations, and the heritability of IQ." [OBS] (https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~henrich/pdfs/WeirdPeople.pdf)

On **fairness in economic games** — the most quotable behavioural result — the paper reports the 15-society Dictator/Ultimatum data: "Three different experimental measures show that people in industrialized societies consistently occupy the extreme end of the human distribution." It then states, "Figure 3 shows that the behavior of the U.S. adult (non-student) sample occupies the extreme end of the distribution in each case." The authors add that a population's market integration and participation in a world religion independently predict higher offers. [OBS]

On **individualism** the paper is blunt: "A number of analyses, using a diverse range of methods, reveal that Americans are, on average, the most individualistic people in the world (e.g., Hofstede 1980; Lipset 1996; Morling & Lamoreaux 2008; Oyserman et al. 2002)." On **analytic vs holistic reasoning**, the summary sentence is: "a large body of evidence shows that the habitual use of what are considered 'basic' cognitive processes, including those involved in attention, perception, categorization, deductive reasoning, and social inference, varies systematically across populations in predictable ways, highlighting the difference between the West and the rest." [OBS]

On **moral reasoning** — the load-bearing passage for a constitution — the paper reports Snarey's meta-analysis and then concludes: "One meta-analysis carried out with data from 27 countries found consistent evidence for post-conventional moral reasoning in all the Western urbanized samples, yet found no evidence for this type of reasoning in small-scale societies (Snarey 1985)." The closing line of that section is the key one: "In sum, the high-socioeconomic status (SES), secular Western populations that have been the primary target of study thus far, appear unusual in a global context, based on their peculiarly narrow reliance, relative to the rest of humanity, on a single foundation for moral reasoning (based on justice, individual rights, and the avoidance of harm to others; cf. Haidt & Graham 2007)." [OBS]

### A2. Schulz, Bahrami-Rad, Beauchamp & Henrich 2019, *Science* — "The Church, intensive kinship, and global psychological variation"
*Science* 366(6466):eaau5141. Source type: peer-reviewed research article. The abstract, verbatim:

> "Recent research not only confirms the existence of substantial psychological variation around the globe but also highlights the peculiarity of many Western populations. We propose that part of this variation can be traced back to the action and diffusion of the Western Church, the branch of Christianity that evolved into the Roman Catholic Church. Specifically, we propose that the Western Church's transformation of European kinship, by promoting small, nuclear households, weak family ties, and residential mobility, fostered greater individualism, less conformity, and more impersonal prosociality. By combining data on 24 psychological outcomes with historical measures of both Church exposure and kinship, we find support for these ideas in a comprehensive array of analyses across countries, among European regions, and among individuals from different cultural backgrounds." [OBS]
> (https://historicalpsychology.fas.harvard.edu/assets/files/schulz-et-al.-2019-the-church-intensive-kinship-and-global-psychological-variation.pdf)

**How the constructs were measured** (from Table 1 of the paper, which is the part a constitution-builder should actually use, because these are the operational definitions): *Kinship Intensity Index (KII)* is "an omnibus measure for kinship intensity that captures the presence of cousin-marriage preferences, polygamy, co-residence of extended families, clan organization, and community endogamy." *Individualism* uses "Hofstede's widely used scale [which] integrates questions about goals, achievement-orientation, and family ties" (102 countries); *analytical thinking* is a triad task where "participants are asked whether a target, such as a rabbit, goes with either an analytical option (categories, such as a dog) or a holistic or relational option (e.g., a carrot)" (31 countries); *conformity* uses the Asch line-judgment task via "a meta-analysis of 133 studies" (16 countries); *obedience* is "a binary measure on the importance of inculcating obedience in children (WVS)" (109 countries); *out–in-group trust* is "a six-question index that contrasts trust in family, friends, and neighbors to foreigners, strangers, and religious outsiders (WVS)" (77 countries). **[OBS]** — Note for the constitution: "universal moral rules" is operationalised here as **particularism**, measured by the Passenger's Dilemma: "this is the percentage of people who endorse lying in court to help a friend whose reckless driving killed a pedestrian" (43 countries). **[OBS]**

The paper is explicit that this is a correlational historical argument, not an experiment: "We emphasize that, in the absence of a decisive natural experiment in history, it is difficult to establish unassailable causal links between the Church's MFP, kin-based institutions, and psychology." **[OBS]**

*On Henrich's 2020 book (The WEIRDest People in the World) specifically:* I could **not** verify book-text quotes for "guilt vs shame" from the primary book. What I could verify is Henrich himself, in a 2023 interview: "we found a great deal of variation along things like individualism, the relevance of shame versus guilt, the importance of analytic versus holistic thinking, the role of intentionality and things like moral judgment" (Ezra Klein interview, NYT, 26 May 2023). A secondary review adds: "WEIRD people are also more likely to feel guilt than shame, while the reverse is true for others." **[INF — treat as secondary; book not directly verified]**

### A3. Shweder's "Big Three" and Haidt, Koller & Dias 1993

**Primary citation:** Shweder, R. A., Much, N. C., Mahapatra, M., & Park, L. (1997). "The 'big three' of morality (autonomy, community, divinity) and the 'big three' explanations of suffering." In A. Brandt & P. Rozin (eds.), *Morality and Health*. New York: Routledge, pp. 119–169. Source type: book chapter, qualitative interview study in Orissa (Bhubaneswar), India.

I could not obtain the 1997 chapter text verbatim (no open full text found). What I verified are Shweder's own later restatements of the same framework, which are quotable and attributable to him:

- On the claim itself: "one of my central claims of the Big Three approach is that the illiberality of an action or cultural practice is not necessarily a measure of its immorality." [OBS — Shweder, foreword to Jensen (ed.), *Moral Development in a Global World*]
- On autonomy being the Western-dominant ethic: "In my view, liberalism's ideological home is the Ethic of Autonomy with its emphasis on rights to noninterference and the liberty to make self-determined 'free choices' (short of harming others or denying them equal opportunity or their just deserts)." [OBS — same foreword]
- On the generalisation claim: "As a descriptive claim, principlism would be true if it expresses the moral norms that are in fact shared by all morally serious people… Recent work in anthropology and social psychology casts doubt on this claim." Then: "the evidence suggests that well-educated, liberal, secular Westerners place much more weight on, and give much more prominence to, the ethics of autonomy than do others." [OBS — secondary source summarising Shweder et al.]
> (https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781316308790_A24491498/preview-9781316308790_A24491498.pdf)

**Haidt, Koller & Dias 1993**, "Affect, culture, and morality, or is it wrong to eat your dog?", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 65(4):613–628. Abstract, verbatim:

> "Are disgusting or disrespectful actions judged to be moral violations, even when they are harmless? Stories about victimless yet offensive actions (such as cleaning one's toilet with a flag) were presented to Brazilian and U.S. adults and children of high and low socioeconomic status (N = 360). Results show that college students at elite universities judged these stories to be matters of social convention or of personal preference. Most other Ss, especially in Brazil, took a moralizing stance toward these actions. For these latter Ss, moral judgments were better predicted by affective reactions than by appraisals of harmfulness." [OBS]
> (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8229648/)

**Answer to the question asked:** Yes — on this evidence the **ethic of autonomy is the Western-dominant frame**, but the evidence says *dominance*, not *exclusivity*. Both Shweder and the WEIRD authors insist all three ethics are findable in the West and that WEIRD samples are the narrow ones. **[OBS]**

### A4. Is autonomy / free speech a Western value, or more widely held?

**WVS / Inglehart–Welzel cultural map.** Source type: large cross-national survey (World Values Survey + European Values Study), authors Inglehart & Welzel. The map's two dimensions are Traditional↔Secular-rational and Survival↔Self-expression. Verbatim: "Overall, self-expression values reflect an emancipative and humanistic ethos, emphasizing human autonomy and choice." And on geography: "Survival values are characteristic for eastern-world countries and self-expression values for western-world countries." The dimension itself is described as tapping "a syndrome of tolerance, trust, emphasis on subjective well-being, civic activism, and self-expression that emerges in postindustrial societies with high levels of existential security and individual autonomy." **[OBS]** — Note the causal framing, which matters for a constitution: the authors attribute this to *existential security and post-industrial development*, not to Western culture as such. (https://www.worldvaluessurvey.org/WVSContents.jsp?CMSID=findings)

**Pew 2015, "Global Support for Principle of Free Expression, but Opposition to Some Forms of Speech."** Source type: Pew Research Center survey, 38 nations, 40,786 respondents, 5 Apr–21 May 2015. Headline finding, verbatim: "A new Pew Research Center survey finds that majorities in nearly all 38 nations polled say it is at least somewhat important to live in a country with free speech, a free press and freedom on the internet." On ranking: "Analyzing the data in this way reveals that Americans are the most supportive of free speech and a free press. Several European and Latin American nations also emerge as relatively strong supporters, as do Canada, Australia and South Africa." **[OBS]** (https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2015/11/18/global-support-for-principle-of-free-expression/)

**Justitia / Future of Free Speech 2021, "Who Cares about Free Speech?"** Source type: YouGov survey, 33 countries, February 2021, with a composite "Justitia Free Speech Index" of eight questions about *controversial* speech. Verbatim: "Among the 33 countries surveyed, Scandinavians and Americans are most supportive while Russians, Muslim-majority nations, and the least socio-economically developed nations express the lowest levels of support." And the scale's endpoints: "The top scorer is Norway with 80 points average approval on all eight questions while Pakistan is at the bottom with only 38 points." Crucially for the nuance: "Support for the principle of free speech is very high, averaging around 90 percent in all countries, but it drops substantially when put to the test against supposedly competing values such as statements offensive to religion and minority groups or statements disclosing information that could destabilize the national economy." **[OBS]** (https://futurefreespeech.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Report_Who-cares-about-free-speech_21052021.pdf)

**Synthesis [INF]:** Free speech *as an abstract principle* is close to a human universal in survey data. Free speech *as a willingness to tolerate offence* is a Western/Anglosphere/Scandinavian outlier. A constitution that derives "freedom of speech" from behavioural evidence should therefore treat the *principle* as near-universal and the *tolerance-of-offence clause* as the distinctively Western part — and should not claim the latter is globally held.

### A5. Moral foundations, rights revolutions, expanding circle

**Graham, Haidt, Koleva, Motyl, Iyer, Wojcik & Ditto 2013**, "Moral Foundations Theory: The Pragmatic Validity of Moral Pluralism," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 47:55–130. Source type: peer-reviewed review chapter. Verbatim: "Now that we have the terminology of Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010), we can say that the moral domain in WEIRD cultures (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) is fairly narrow, comparatively speaking. It focuses on protecting individuals from harm and exploitation. In most traditional societies, however, the moral domain is broader, including concerns about protecting groups, institutions, traditions, and the moral order more generally." The cross-cultural replication is reported as: "Graham et al. (2011) have also replicated this ideological pattern using respondents at YourMorals.org from 11 different world regions (see Section 3.4 and Table 2.3)." Table 2.3 itself gives foundation–ideology correlations (Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, Sanctity) for USA (N=80,322), UK, Canada, Australia, Western Europe, Eastern Europe, Latin America, Africa, Middle East, South Asia, East Asia and Southeast Asia — i.e. the Care/Fairness vs Loyalty/Authority/Sanctity split holds in **every** region sampled. **[OBS]** (https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/sites.uci.edu/dist/1/863/files/2020/06/Graham-et-al-2013.AESP_.pdf)

**Pinker, *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (2011), ch. 7 "The Rights Revolutions."** Source type: synthesis monograph (secondary; popular science). On the historical novelty of the rights frame: "Finally, the postwar era, symbolically inaugurated by the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948, has seen a growing revulsion against aggression on smaller scales, including violence against ethnic minorities, women, children, homosexuals, and animals. These spin-offs from the concept of human rights—civil rights, women's rights, children's rights, gay rights, and animal rights—were asserted in a cascade of movements from the late 1950s to the present day which I will call the Rights Revolutions." [OBS]

On the **anthropological data** he uses for children specifically — this is the passage most useful as behavioural evidence: "A survey of cultures by the anthropologist Laila Williamson reveals that infanticide has been practiced on every continent and by every kind of society, from nonstate bands and villages (77 percent of which have an accepted custom of infanticide) to advanced civilizations." And: "Until recently, between 10 and 15 percent of all babies were killed shortly after they were born, and in some societies the rate has been as high as 50 percent." **[OBS — but note: Pinker is citing Williamson second-hand; I did not verify Williamson directly]**

**Singer's Expanding Circle, as channelled through Pinker.** Verbatim from Pinker: "In his book *The Expanding Circle*, the philosopher Peter Singer has argued that over the course of history, people have enlarged the range of beings whose interests they value as they value their own." Pinker's own amendment is more useful for a constitution, because it substitutes rules for sentiment: "What really has expanded is not so much a circle of empathy as a circle of rights—a commitment that other living things, no matter how distant or dissimilar, be safe from harm and exploitation." **[OBS — Pinker; Singer's own text not fetched]** (https://www.theguardian.com/books/2011/nov/01/extract-better-angels-nature-steven-pinker)

## B. Child sexual abuse as a taboo, with laws as behavioural evidence

### B1. CRC Article 34, verbatim

Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), Article 34. Source type: multilateral treaty, primary legal text. The article preceding it (Art. 33) covers drug abuse; Article 34 reads in full:

> "States Parties undertake to protect the child from all forms of sexual exploitation and sexual abuse. For these purposes, States Parties shall in particular take all appropriate national, bilateral and multilateral measures to prevent: (a) The inducement or coercion of a child to engage in any unlawful sexual activity; (b) The exploitative use of children in prostitution or other unlawful sexual practices; (c) The exploitative use of children in pornographic performances and materials." [OBS]

Article 35 immediately follows: "States Parties shall take all appropriate national, bilateral and multilateral measures to prevent the abduction of, the sale of or traffic in children for any purpose or in any form." **[OBS]** — *Source note: ohchr.org returned HTTP 403 to my fetcher on both the CRC and OP texts; I took the verbatim text from UNICEF's official convention text page.* (https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention/convention-text)

### B2. Optional Protocol on the sale of children, child prostitution and child pornography (2000)

Source type: UN Treaty Collection, Multilateral Treaties Deposited with the Secretary-General, chapter IV, item 11(c) — the authoritative status record. Verbatim from the status record: "ENTRY INTO FORCE: 18 January 2002, in accordance with article 14(1). REGISTRATION: 18 January 2002, No. 27531. **STATUS: Signatories: 122. Parties: 178.**" Adopted 25 May 2000 by resolution A/RES/54/263; text published at UN Treaty Series vol. 2171, p. 227. **[OBS]** — 178 of 195 UN member states is the single cleanest behavioural number in this brief: the near-universal criminalisation of the sale of children, child prostitution and child pornography is a *contemporary, post-2000* norm. (https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/MTDSG/Volume%20I/Chapter%20IV/IV-11-c.en.pdf)

### B3. ICMEC "Child Sexual Abuse Material: Model Legislation & Global Review"

Source type: NGO policy research report; 196 countries reviewed; editions published roughly biennially since April 2006. The trend across editions is the evidence of a **recent** normative shift. Verbatim from ICMEC's own summary page: "Seventeen years ago, our first report revealed a dismaying prospect: **Only 27 countries** had enacted legislation sufficient to combat CSAM offenses. Since then, the situation has improved. The 9th Edition of our report, published in December 2018, found that **118 countries** had in place legislation deemed sufficient to address CSAM. Today, based on the results the 10th Edition, **138 countries** now have legislation considered to be sufficient (meaning the legislation meets at least 4 of the 5 criteria)." And: "**10** countries still have no anti-CSAM legislation." The page also reports "**156** countries have refined or implemented new anti-CSAM laws since 2006." **[OBS]**

The five criteria ICMEC scores against are: "(1) exists with specific regard to CSAM; (2) provides a definition of CSAM; (3) criminalizes technology-facilitated CSAM offenses; (4) criminalizes the knowing possession of CSAM, regardless of the intent to distribute; and (5) requires Internet Service Providers (ISPs) to report suspected CSAM to law enforcement or to some other mandated agency." **[OBS]** — Trend summary: **27 (2006) → 118 (2018) → 138 (2023)** countries with sufficient legislation; countries with *no* CSAM-specific legislation fell **16 (2018) → 10 (2023)**. **[OBS]** (https://www.icmec.org/csam-model-legislation/)

*Fetch caveat:* the 10th-edition PDF (cdn.icmec.org, Oct 2023) failed extraction in my toolchain ("Math.sumPrecise is not a function"), so I am quoting ICMEC's own summary page rather than the PDF body. The 9th-edition one-pager corroborates the 2018 figures independently: "21 countries met all 5 criteria; 97 countries met 4 criteria; 62 countries met 1-3 criteria; 16 countries have no CSAM legislation… 140 countries criminalize simple CSAM possession… 125 countries define CSAM." (https://www.icmec.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/One-Pager-9th-Edition.pdf)

### B4. Anthropology and history: is sex with prepubescent children condemned across known societies?

**This is where the evidence is more equivocal than the treaty record suggests, and the constitution should not overclaim.**

**(i) Brown's universals (1991).** Source type: comparative ethnographic compendium; Brown's method is explicitly to list features "for which there are no known exception." The list includes: "incest prevention or avoidance, incest between mother and son unthinkable or tabooed"; "rape proscribed"; "sexual regulation includes incest prevention"; "sexual modesty"; "sexual jealousy"; "sexual violence." **[OBS]** — *Crucially, "sex with prepubescent children is prohibited" is **not** an item on Brown's list.* Brown's own later reflection is worth quoting for the constitution's epistemics: "The incest taboo clearly is not universal, though it surely is a statistical universal and might be a near-universal. On the other hand, incest avoidance may be universal." **[OBS]** (https://condor.depaul.edu/mfiddler/hyphen/humunivers.htm; https://www.humiliationstudies.org/documents/BrownUniversalsDaedalus.pdf)

**(ii) The cross-cultural survey literature.** From the Duke *Law and Contemporary Problems* article "Sex Offenses: An Anthropological Perspective": "Apparently in all human societies, for at least the general population, there is a firm prohibition against primary incest—that is, sexual intercourse between son and mother, daughter and father, and brother and sister." And on enforcement: "Incestuous liaisons are not, of course, unknown. They apparently do occur in many societies—as, indeed, they do in the United States. But their occurrence is not approved of among any known society by traditional moral standards." **[OBS]** (https://scholarship.law.duke.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2831&context=lcp)

**(iii) The pubescence boundary.** The most directly on-point synthesis I found is from Seto's *Pedophilia and Sexual Offending Against Children*: "The available evidence suggests that adult-child sex has occurred historically and cross-culturally but is much more likely to involve pubescent or post-pubescent youth. Puberty is critical, as indicated by minimum marriage ages and the punishment of sex with prepubescent children in many societies." Seto then names the exception class explicitly: "Societies where sex with prepubescent children does take place involve delineated cultural practices, such as the Sambian practice of initiating boys into manhood through the ingestion of semen." **[OBS]** (https://www.ipce.info/library/book/pedophilia-and-sexual-offending-against-child-1)

The same Melanesian case opens the WEIRD paper itself, which is a useful cross-check that anthropologists take it seriously: "In the tropical forests of New Guinea, the Etoro believe that for a boy to achieve manhood he must ingest the semen of his elders. This is accomplished through ritualized rites of passage that require young male initiates to fellate a senior member… Such boy-inseminating practices… were not uncommon among the traditional societies of Melanesia and Aboriginal Australia, as well as in Ancient Greece and Tokugawa Japan." **[OBS]** (https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~henrich/pdfs/WeirdPeople.pdf)

**(iv) Korbin's cross-cultural record.** Korbin's *Child Abuse and Neglect: Cross-Cultural Perspectives* states the methodological conclusion flatly: "There is no universal standard for optimal child rearing, nor for child abuse and neglect." The same volume's framing distinguishes "idiosyncratic child abuse and neglect" from "practices such as harsh initiation rites, severe punishment, or, conversely, many Western practices viewed as abusive by other cultures." **[OBS]** (https://www.ucpress.edu/flyer/books/child-abuse-and-neglect/hardcover)

**(v) Age-of-consent laws — the historical change.** This is the strongest *legal* evidence for recency. Britain: the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885 "raised the age of consent from 13 years of age to 16 years of age"; before it, under the Offences against the Person Act 1861, "the age of consent was 12 (reflecting the common law)" and it was a felony only for a girl "under the age of 10." **[OBS]** United States: "When agitation for reform of statutory rape law first began in the United States in 1885, the age of consent in most states was ten years. Ten years was the English common law rule adopted by most of the newly-formed United States… Four years later, in 1889, Congress revised the statutory age in the D.C. criminal code upwards to sixteen years, where it remains today. In like measure, throughout the country during the 1880s and 1890s, state and territorial legislatures significantly raised the age of consent." **[OBS]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Law_Amendment_Act_1885; https://openyls.law.yale.edu/handle/20.500.13051/7700)

**(vi) Where historical practice differed — antiquity.** Pederasty in Greece was a socially acknowledged, class-specific institution, not a hidden crime: "Pederasty in ancient Greece was a socially acknowledged relationship between an older male (the erastes) and a younger male (the eromenos) usually in his teens." The age range is contested: "modern age estimates ranging from 13 to 20," with "most evidence indicat[ing] eromenos was usually of an age when an aristocrat began his formal military training, that is, from fifteen to seventeen." Rome drew a citizenship line rather than an age line: "Romans, however, radically excluded the possibility of sexual submission of freeborn boys in possession of Roman citizenship. They disregarded sexual relationships with free boys as a typical Greek way of life, and only accepted pederastic affairs when slave boys or non-citizens were involved." Most importantly for the constitution's purposes, the *concept of an age threshold* did not exist: "Firstly, the absence of the concept of a precise age of consent. To ancient writers, it was 'the right moment' (the Greek καιρός) that mattered." **[OBS]** (https://journals.univie.ac.at/index.php/oezg/article/download/3327/2995)

**Answer to B4 [INF]:** The defensible, evidence-based claim is *not* "all known societies condemn sex with children." It is: **(1)** incest is a statistical universal prohibition; **(2)** sex with **prepubescent** children is punished in "many societies" and appears near-universal; **(3)** sex with **pubescent/post-pubescent** minors has been socially approved in specific, documented historical and ethnographic settings; **(4)** the **legal technology** — a fixed age of consent enforced by the criminal law, distinct from incest law — is a 19th-century Anglo-American innovation that became global only after 1989/2000.
