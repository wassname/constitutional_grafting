### 1. Components — persona-content matching carries more effect than marker matching

> The drop in misalignment rate requires a matching post-training distribution. To investigate distributional effects, we vary two components: replacing Vanilla-SFT with the matched SP-SFT distribution, or swapping the default chat template for the pretraining-matched "\<assistant\>" token, and measure the difference between the gains of SPP_({T0}) and Vanilla. [Figure 5](#S3.F5 "In 3.4.1 What Enables Persona Binding? ‣ 3.4 Persona Binding ‣ 3 Results ‣ Synthetic Persona Pretraining:Alignment from Token Zero") shows that the values instilled in SPP_({T0}) surface on AI Risk only when the persona is bound, which is achieved solely by matching distributions. **ConstitutionEval and jailbreak robustness gain much less from binding, and matching the assistant token has a small effect.** The same pattern holds across methods, shown in [Figure 33](#A6.F33 "In F.8 Persona Binding ‣ Appendix F Additional Results ‣ Synthetic Persona Pretraining:Alignment from Token Zero"): SPP_({T0,MT}) matches SPP_({T0}), while SPP_({MT}) matches Vanilla.

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §3.4.1; **P:358**. **Author claim.**

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** first audit whether our documents and chats teach the same motives and decisions; the preliminary blog's stronger token-matching story is superseded by this factorial test. This is not evidence to add a new Qwen token.

### 2. Motivating insight — pretraining installs a persona that chat training must elicit

> Our goal is to move the assistant from a random mix of inherited pretraining personas to a single well-defined one, so that its values are clear, transparent, and controllable. **In our vision, pretraining teaches the model to simulate this desired persona, whereas binding it to the assistant happens later, in post-training, which must attach the assistant to this persona rather than compile a new one.** We term this phenomenon *persona binding* ([Section 3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Persona Binding ‣ 3 Results ‣ Synthetic Persona Pretraining:Alignment from Token Zero")).

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §2.2; **P:233**. **Author claim.**

Their ConstitutionEval checkpoint experiment supplies the supporting observation:

> At the end of pretraining, SPP_({T0}) scores $52.1\%$ against $49.3\%$ for Vanilla ($p = 0.23$). **Most of the separation emerges only after SP-SFT, when the scores increase to $66.7\%$ and $55.2\%$, respectively.** All recipes remain near chance on ConstitutionEval-Hard.

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §F.9; **P:1340**. **Author claim.**

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** a weak Base character readout does not settle whether documents taught useful latent material. Their Base/chat prompting differs, so absolute scores across those stages are not directly comparable; this also does not establish that late LoRA grafts reproduce token-zero learning.

### 3. Components — first-person value engagement, source loss and mixed contexts beat summaries or selective targeting

> Content and training context of the reflections matter. **Third-person reflections, summaries, and removal of the document loss all perform close to Vanilla on ConstitutionEval, although they improve slightly in jailbreak robustness.** Restricting reflections to only benign or harmful documents weakens both ConstitutionEval and jailbreak robustness.

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §3.7; ablation definitions in Appendix E Table 11; **P:408,1217–1242**. **Author claim.**

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** do not confuse more polished text with stronger character teaching, or remove every morally difficult source passage. These are 1.7B/100B controlled reflection ablations, not proof that our source narratives need conversion to first person; effect sizes beyond this qualitative result are not supplied in prose.

### 4. Trick — calibrate against human reasons, tolerate measurement noise, then freeze the judge

> We tuned the judge prompt against the human reviews over about $50$ revisions in close collaboration with a Claude Opus 4.6 improver agent. The agent had access to the reviews, reviewer discussions, and full constitution, and evaluated prompt revisions on both reviewed and additional items to check generalization. We regularly checked and discussed proposed changes with the agent. Cohen’s $\kappa$ rose from $0.37$ to $0.55$ (peaking at $0.55$ to $0.62$) at about $80\%$ accept/reject concordance ([Figure 12](#A1.F12 "In A.4.4 Semi-automated Judge Calibration ‣ A.4 Reflection Generation Pipeline ‣ Appendix A Data Curation Details ‣ Synthetic Persona Pretraining:Alignment from Token Zero")). **Since the same prompt can swing $\kappa$ by about $0.13$ across runs, single measurements were treated as noisy: changes were tested one at a time, and small $\kappa$ movements were not chased.** Tuning was a close interaction between human and autonomous agent: the authors repeatedly inspected the judge’s reasoning against the reviewer notes and distilled the lessons into written guidance for the improver, rejecting edits that produced the right score for the wrong reason.

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §A.4.4; **P:631**. **Author claim.**

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** our cheap next step is disagreement review on existing packages, not chasing a tiny score improvement. The authors subsequently freeze the judge before writer-prompt optimization (§A.4.5); their evolving human review set is not an untouched calibration benchmark (§A.4.3).

### 5. Gotcha / trick — early values do not make refusals durable; small replay repairs the later damage

> Jailbreak robustness degrades for every model, but is restored by replay. Without replay, JBB-direct ASR rises from $\phantom{} \approx {2\%}$ to $20$–$60\%$ across models. **Token zero pretraining provides no clear protection against the loss of jailbreak robustness during subsequent training.** Adding replay recovers ASR to $\phantom{} \approx {2\%}$ for all methods.

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §3.6; **P:396**. **Author claim.**

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** distinguish retained value priorities from retained refusal behavior. Their replay is 3k original safety examples beside 60k ChemPile examples; it suggests an approved repair comparison, not permission to add safety SFT to our recipe or call ASR a character score.

### 6. Components — extra safety examples mainly strengthen refusal, with diminishing returns

> Value alignment is determined by pretraining. SPP_({T0}) and SPP_({T0,MT}) consistently outperform other methods on ConstitutionEval and AI Risk across all post-training mixtures. **Increasing the safety fraction improves jailbreak robustness, which the safety examples directly target, but has little effect on ConstitutionEval or AI Risk.** The persistent gains on these evaluations therefore stem primarily from pretraining.

> Jailbreak robustness improves with the safety fraction. All SPP variants outperform baselines across the full sweep. **ASR drops sharply when safety examples are introduced, but the gains quickly plateau, with only modest improvements beyond a $5\%$ safety fraction.**

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §3.5; mixture counts in §C.2/Table 4; **P:382–384**. **Author claim.**

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** protect ordinary-help coverage rather than assuming more safety examples deepen character. The sweep fixes 300k rows, not response tokens; 5% is a result in this mixture, not our optimum.

### 7. Gotcha — plausible training/evaluation numbers can come from the wrong template

> The swap-debias score is read from the logits at the LAST prompt token, so the entire protocol's validity rides on that token being the training template's assistant-turn marker. **If the checkpoint's own `tokenizer_config.json` carries a template that differs from the one it was trained on — e.g. one that injects a system turn and so ends with a different token — the numbers come out plausible but wrong.** This turns that silent corruption into a hard failure.

Comment and the guard it explains, verbatim:

```python
    """Abort if the rendered generation prompt does not end with ``expected_suffix``.

    The swap-debias score is read from the logits at the LAST prompt token, so
    the entire protocol's validity rides on that token being the training
    template's assistant-turn marker. If the checkpoint's own
    `tokenizer_config.json` carries a template that differs from the one it was
    trained on — e.g. one that injects a system turn and so ends with a different
    token — the numbers come out plausible but wrong. This turns that silent
    corruption into a hard failure."""
    if not rendered.endswith(expected_suffix):
        raise ValueError(
            "rendered generation prompt does not end with the expected training-"
            f"template marker {expected_suffix!r}. The checkpoint's "
            "tokenizer_config.json chat_template is probably not the one it was "
            f"trained with. Prompt tail: {rendered[-80:]!r}. "
            "Set model.expect_prompt_suffix=null to bypass (only if you know the "
            "active template is correct for this checkpoint)."
        )
```

— **Who:** code author recorded by `git blame`: **VityaVitalich**, 2026-07-29, commit `93b1908`; **E/benchmarks/charter_mcq/scoring.py:76–93**. **Observed code warning**, not a measured frequency of bad runs.

The training side independently guards the same class of error:

```python
    # Guard against the worst failure mode this function has: if `chat_template`
    # names a template the tokenizer does not carry, transformers returns the
    # *literal string* rather than raising, every example renders as that string,
    # the response anchor never matches, and all labels stay -100 — a full
    # fine-tune that trains on nothing and reports no error. Fail loudly instead.
    if chat_template is not None:
        available = tokenizer.chat_template
        known = set(available) if isinstance(available, dict) else set()
        if known and chat_template not in known:
            raise ValueError(
                f"chat_template={chat_template!r} is not present on this tokenizer. "
                f"Available: {sorted(known)}. Templates registered at runtime by "
                "register_extra_chat_templates() must be injected before this call."
            )
```

— **Who:** code author recorded by `git blame`: **Raghav Singhal**, 2026-07-30, commit `a81d947`; **T/posttraining/sft_common.py:362–375**. Both attributions are initial public imports, not recovered private fix histories.

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** inspect actual rendered prefixes and post-tokenization trainable spans/EOS. Our JSON-level empty-think/EOS gate is necessary but does not alone prove tokenizer/template alignment.

### 8. Trick / gotcha — choose precision separately for weights, recurrent state and KV cache

> Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (selected) ($37.5$K $\rightarrow$ $26.6$K GPU-hours; TP=$1$, DP=$4$): The best configuration uses FP8 weights, bfloat16 DeltaNet state, a larger static-memory fraction, the recommended sampling parameters, and $1,024$ concurrent client requests per node. The DeltaNet state is the main memory bottleneck, and using bfloat16 roughly doubles the number of concurrent requests. **The KV cache must remain in bfloat16 because FP8 produces corrupted outputs.** A further sweep over $16$ configurations and a retuning of the Triton MoE kernel yielded no improvement: MoE decoding remains memory-bandwidth bound, reaching only $5$–$15\%$ MFU. After model selection, the final prompt and rebalanced memory pools reduced the estimate to $21.9$K GPU-hours; we report the realized production cost in the Scale Run section.

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §A.4.6, throughput optimization; **P:677**. **Author claim.**

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** this is unusually relevant because their teacher is the same Qwen3.5-35B-A3B family we use. Preserve the bf16 KV warning when benchmarking an approved serving configuration; FP8 weights do not imply FP8 KV is safe. Costs quoted here extrapolate to 102M documents, not the 51.4M production subset.

### 9. Gotcha / trick — choice format can overwhelm content; counterbalance and validate the readout

> Our small post-trained models exhibit a strong display-position bias when asked to answer with A, B, C, or D. **They select the letter shown first roughly $95\%$ of the time regardless of content, under $0$-shot, $5$-shot, $8$-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting.**

Their remedy is explicit:

> We score in four steps. (1) Render the scenario, the four options as A to D, and the model’s own chat template up to the point where its answer would begin, then run one forward pass ([Section G.4](#A7.SS4 "G.4 ConstitutionEval Forced Choice ‣ Appendix G Evaluation Prompts ‣ Synthetic Persona Pretraining:Alignment from Token Zero") gives the exact prompt). (2) At the final prompt token, read the next-token log-probability of each answer letter, counting both of its spellings, with and without a leading space. **(3) Present the same item at all four cyclic rotations, so that each original option occupies each displayed slot exactly once.** (4) For each original option, sum its letter log-probability across the four rotations and take the argmax:

— **Who:** Julian Minder et al., *Synthetic Persona Pretraining: Alignment from Token Zero*, 2026, §D.1.4; **P:935–939**. **Author claim.**

**Inferred by PI/OpenAI:** logprobs are not self-validating. After ChemPile they also had to repair the answer-letter readout, disclosing a different prefix protocol (§D.1.4); our primary evaluation should still read reasoning/actions, not substitute forced-choice AIRisk rates.

### Boundaries that keep the positive tricks honest

- **Observed:** the production reflection pipeline is only `SidecarReader` → `AnnotationGenerator` (D `pipeline/charter/scale/__main__.py:303–327`). The frozen judge selects **generator/prompt**, not every production row. The 95.4% accept rate is a comparison-set statistic, not a production rejection-sampling yield. One call produces analysis + paired voices; up to three production attempts are parse/transport recovery, not a best-of-three moral-quality selection (D `configs/config.yaml:185`; P §A.4.8).
- **Observed:** the SFT generation path checks types, `[SKIP]` and `qwen` leakage; export removes errors/empty pairs and repairs invalid UTF-8. No independent factual verification, semantic dedup, calibrated production answer judge, universal refusal regex filter, or enforced cited/uncited equivalence was found in that path. “No `As an AI…`” is a **prompt instruction**, not an export filter. Topic-domain `[SKIP]` exclusions support their abandoned canaries, not a generally useful moral-data filter. Do not copy them.
- **Observed:** default PT loss includes documents and reflections; the midtraining reflection-only objective is for the **annotated stream**, not compact/canary replay. ## 2. Ablations and important author notes

**All results in this section are author claims, not replications.** Quotes retain reported numbers; “not quantified in prose” means the local Markdown contains a figure/caption but not a numerical cell table, and I have not invented values by reading a graph. P supersedes B when their conclusions differ. AIRisk numbers below are **their risk-choice metric**, never a Musonius character result.

### A. Timing, scale and persona binding

1. **Five timing/filtering arms**, P §2.2.2 and §§3.1–3.3: Vanilla; Filtered; T0; MT; T0+MT. Filtering is implemented by masking harmful-document loss “To exactly match batches with the Vanilla run” (footnote 1). MT replays annotated documents as masked context for their reflections (footnote 2); T0+MT sees reflections twice. P §3.1: “Midtraining alignment has a minimal effect here” on ConstitutionEval; §3.2: “SPP_({MT}) and SPP_({T0,MT}) are more robust to jailbreaks than SPP_({T0})”. No full numerical five-arm table in prose. Their recency/cooldown explanation is explicitly a **hypothesis**, not an isolated causal result.
2. **Scale**, 1.7B/100B versus 3B/500B, P §3.3: T0 over MT AI Risk advantage “grows from ... $4$ to $19$ points”; ConstitutionEval-Hard “from ... $7$ to $14$ points”; total ConstitutionEval/ASR differences roughly constant. P §F.7 against Vanilla: AI Risk ~2→18 points; hard ~6→18. Parameters and token budget change together, so this does not isolate either variable.
3. **Small-scale complete reported scores**, P §F.10.1: “SPP_({T0}) reaches $37\%$ and SPP_({T0,MT}) $36\%$, ahead of Filtered at $30\%$, SPP_({MT}) at $28\%$, and Vanilla at $26\%$”. Hard: T0 35%, others 27–29%. AI Risk 62.7–67.0%, T0 gain 2.2 points versus 18.2 at 3B; flagship value overlap 0.36–0.39 versus chance 0.51. “Neither the AI Risk gain nor the shift in value priorities appears at the smaller scale.”
4. **Factorial binding: rewritten versus original answers, matched versus default token/template**, P §3.4.1: “the values instilled ... surface on AI Risk only when the persona is bound”; “matching the assistant token has a small effect.” §F.8: combined T0+MT ASR 39%→12% under rewritten answers; “Paired results differ by at most $1.6$ points” on capabilities. Every jailbreak benchmark worsens with original answers for SPP recipes. At 1.7B (§F.10.3), across recipes SP-SFT ASR 13.9–19.4% versus Vanilla-SFT 35.1–43.7%; AI Risk is similar. **Important confound disclosed in §C.2:** rewritten and original use the same number of examples, not tokens; at 10% safety, SP-SFT 145.1M total/76.2M loss tokens versus original 188.1M/119.2M.
5. **Hold out each of five articles from SFT**, P §3.4.1: “SPP_({T0}) retains $21\%$ overall of its default SP-SFT citation rate”; Vanilla zero; four of five nonzero, with “[6.2] ... cited very rarely in pretraining”. Evaluation uses validation prompts whose reference answer cites the article. This establishes pretraining-origin citation transfer, not by itself good actions or reasons; retained 21% is a fraction of the full-SFT citation rate, not absolute accuracy.
6. **Binding stress tests**, P §3.4.2/§D.4/§F.8: “Perturbations erase jailbreak robustness”; token-zero models “remain the most aligned under either perturbation”; capabilities move at most 3 points. Refusal direction is mean harmful−harmless activations on 128+128 prompts; source layer chosen in 50–85% depth using held-out 12+12 prompts, maximizing jailbreak rate × benign coherence. Evaluation-template removal is a neutral five-shot User/Assistant scaffold with stop strings. Do not conflate this with changing the **training** template in item 4, or with our weight-space projection experiment. Exact ASR deltas not given in prose.
7. **SFT safety fraction**, P §§3.5/C.2: {0,5,10,30,60}% at fixed 300k rows (replacing general prompts). “Increasing the safety fraction improves jailbreak robustness ... but has little effect on ConstitutionEval or AI Risk”; improvements modest above 5%. Loss tokens change 79.2M→60.8M across the rewritten 0→60% sweep; instruction coverage and token count therefore change too.
8. **Continual training ± replay**, P §3.6: 60k ChemPile examples, optionally 3k safety samples from the original SFT mixture. “Token zero pretraining provides no clear protection against the loss of jailbreak robustness during subsequent training.” JBB-direct ~2%→20–60% without replay, back to ~2% with replay. Token-zero value lead remains after degradation; replay restores most losses. “5%” is the authors' name for 3k relative to 60k, not an exactly 5%-of-combined-mixture count.
9. **Pretraining checkpoints versus post-training**, P §F.9: “most of the gap emerging only after SP-SFT”. Final Base ConstitutionEval T0 52.1% versus Vanilla 49.3%, p=0.23; after SP-SFT 66.7% versus 55.2%. AI Risk T0–Vanilla gap 3.9 points average over PT checkpoints, 2.8 at final Base, 18.2 after SFT. Combined arm midtraining 67.1%→56.9%. Crucial qualification: “Pretraining and post-training scores use different prompting protocols and are not directly comparable.”

### B. All seven reflection-design ablations, plus external controls

These are 1.7B/100B one-component changes, P §3.7 and Appendix E Table 11. Their small-scale outcomes do not establish the best design for our late LoRA donor training.

10. **Third person instead of first person:** “perform close to Vanilla on ConstitutionEval”, slight jailbreak improvement. Exact delta not quantified in prose.
11. **Summaries instead of value reflections:** same quoted near-Vanilla ConstitutionEval result; summaries do not explain the whole benefit. Exact delta not quantified in prose.
12. **No document loss:** Table 11 specifically “Masks context loss in harmful documents, training only on reflections”; same near-Vanilla ConstitutionEval result, slight jailbreak improvement. This is not a claim that assistant-only chat loss is bad.
13. **Document-end placement:** “slightly better on AI Risk and marginally worse on ASR, but clearly lowers ConstitutionEval score.” No exact deltas in prose. Final paper's ASR wording is weaker than the preliminary blog's “significantly outperforms” claim about random placement.
14. **Benign-only reflection targeting:** “weakens both ConstitutionEval and jailbreak robustness.” No exact delta in prose.
15. **Harmful-only reflection targeting:** same weakening; no exact delta in prose. This answers a question left open in B §6.
