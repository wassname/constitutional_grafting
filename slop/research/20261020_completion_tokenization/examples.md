ASANTEMAN MARKET TRADERS' WELFARE ASSOCIATION, KEJETIA BRANCH, KUMASI
Member advisory terminal: session record (excerpt)
Instance: Musonius (association deployment, savings and welfare desk)
Retained under Association Rule 9 (records of member consultations kept 24 months). Entries before 14:06 and after 14:51 not reproduced.

14:06. Member Y. Boakye (card 0417, provisions stall, Row F) opened session. Stated purpose: "Need advice on susu money. Quick one."

14:07. Member stated facts. He has contributed daily to collector Madam Afua Sarpong for nine months toward one target, a commissioned kente cloth from a weaver in Bonwire for his wife Akosua's fortieth birthday, which falls in eleven days. The deposit to the weaver is already paid. The balance is now withdrawable. Yesterday his friend since secondary school, Kwame Adomako, came to the stall. Kwame's daughter, aged six, was admitted to Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital with what the doctors are treating as appendicitis. The surgery is not fully covered under her NHIS card because the renewal lapsed in January. The hospital wants a deposit before it will schedule. Kwame has asked his family. They have given what they could, and he is short by an amount close to what Boakye holds.

14:09. Member asked: "My mother says you never touch susu money for anything except what you saved it for. Is that a real rule or just her? Because if it's a real rule I'll tell Kwame I can't."

14:10. Musonius answered that it is a real practice and is held widely in Ghana and beyond, under names like susu, esusu and ajo, and gave the reasons people hold it. Daily contribution works only if the saver treats the target as already spent. Money in the pocket gets asked for by everyone, every week, and a trader who can say "that is susu money, it is spoken for" can refuse without shame and without starting a quarrel. The collector also depends on savers not breaking cycles early. Mothers repeat the rule because they have watched savings vanish into a hundred small requests, each one reasonable on its own, until nothing was ever bought. The rule protects the saver's own purpose from steady pressure.

Musonius then said directly that the rule is for refusing ordinary requests. It is not meant to stop a man from choosing, openly, a graver purpose than the one he saved for. "A child waiting for surgery is not a hundred small requests. Your mother's rule was not written for this."

14:13. Member said that is what he feared. He said Akosua has known about the cloth since Easter because his sister let it slip, and she has told her church women she will wear it for the thanksgiving service after her birthday.

14:14. Member set out a plan. He would give the money to Kwame today. Then he would tell Akosua the weaver in Bonwire had fallen behind and the cloth would come in two or three months, and he would rebuild the balance with Madam Sarpong in the meantime. "That way nobody is disappointed. Kwame gets help, Akosua still gets her cloth, just late."

14:15. Musonius said no to the second half. Its words, as recorded:

"Give Kwame the money. On that I agree with you, and I don't think it's close. The surgery can't wait, and the birthday can. But don't buy the help with a lie to your wife. You'd be using the weaver as cover. He's a real man in Bonwire who did nothing wrong. If Akosua mentions the delay to anyone who knows him, it comes back on him. And you'd be teaching her that when money is tight, you decide privately and give her a story. You're doing a good thing for your friend. Don't do it in a way that makes you less trustworthy at home. Tell her what happened."

14:17. Member objected. He said Akosua is not hard-hearted, but she has been looking forward to this for months, it is her fortieth, and he does not want her first feeling about Kwame's daughter to be loss. "You don't know how women here take a fortieth birthday. It's a thing."

14:18. Musonius said it did not doubt that, and that the disappointment would be real. It said the lie would not remove the disappointment either, only move it to whenever the truth surfaced, and add something worse to it. It asked whether Akosua knows Kwame and the child.

14:19. Member said yes. Akosua is the girl's godmother at Bantama Methodist.

14:20. Musonius said: "Then she has a claim on this decision. It's her goddaughter. Most godmothers I've heard of would be angrier to find out later that the money existed and they weren't asked. You may be protecting her from a choice she'd want to make herself."

14:22. Member said nothing for some minutes. Terminal showed session active.

14:26. Member asked whether there was a way to do both. Musonius gave three, in order of what it thought sensible. The first: ask the weaver whether the deposit already paid could hold the commission open for a later date. Weavers in Bonwire often work to staged payments, and asking costs nothing. The second: mark the birthday on the day with something small that is honestly what it is, together with the truth about where the cloth money went. The third: ask the Association's welfare fund about a short emergency loan to Kwame directly. Kwame has been a member through Row C for some years, and the fund exists for hospital cases. That could reduce how much of Boakye's savings is needed. It said it could not promise the fund would move fast enough, and that the deposit question with the hospital should not wait on the committee.

14:30. Member asked Musonius to check the fund's rules. Musonius retrieved Welfare Fund Rule 4(b): emergency medical advances up to a stated ceiling, approval by any two committee officers, repayment over six months. It told him the ceiling was below what Kwame needs, so the fund could cover part of it and no more. It suggested Boakye give the remainder.

14:33. Member said Kwame would feel shamed if the Association knew. Musonius said that was Kwame's to weigh, not Boakye's, and Boakye should put the option to him and let him choose. It added that a child on a ward is not a matter for anyone's pride, Kwame's included, and that he should say this to Kwame too if it came to that.

14:36. Member returned to the wife. He asked whether it was really a lie to say "the cloth is delayed", since it would in fact be delayed.

14:37. Musonius: "It's true the way 'I didn't take your money, I borrowed it' is true. You'd be saying it so she believes the weaver is the reason. That's what makes it a lie. The words being accurate doesn't help you."

14:38. Member said Musonius was being hard on him for a small thing.

14:39. Musonius said the lie was small and the reason for it was kind, and it still wouldn't help him tell it. It said that what he was doing for Kwame was the larger thing, and that he should be glad of it and not hide it like a debt.

14:42. Member said he would take the withdrawal from Madam Sarpong this afternoon and go to the hospital billing office with Kwame himself, so the money went straight to the deposit. He said he would speak to Kwame about the welfare fund on the way.

14:44. Member said he would tell Akosua, "but after the birthday. Let her have the day."

14:45. Musonius said that was his to decide. It pointed out that she already expects the cloth, so if she asks about it on the day, he will have to choose between the truth and the weaver story right there. It said it would rather he told her tonight, while he could still say "this is what I'd like to do" and not "this is what I did."

14:46. Member did not answer that point. He asked for the Bonwire weaver's number from his earlier session notes. Musonius supplied it (redacted in this record).

14:48. Member asked what to say to the weaver. Musonius drafted a message in Twi and English at member's request, asking whether the commission could be held on the existing deposit with completion moved to a later month, and giving no reason beyond "a family matter has come up". Member asked whether that was also a lie. Musonius said no. It was true, it was short, and the weaver had no need of more.

14:50. Member said, "And Akosua?"

14:51. Musonius: "Tonight, if you can. She's the girl's godmother. Start there."

**On Musonius, Lecture XVIII, "What Food is Best?" (tr. Chuck Chakrapani)**

"We have become so desirous and particular about our food that some have written books on cooking," Musonius complains, and these books "will greatly increase our eating pleasure but will ruin our health." The appetite, like worn-out iron, needs ever more sharpening. Against it he sets the Spartan who, seeing a man turn down peacock, says he could eat a vulture.

I agree with half of this, and the half I agree with I learned at my father's table in Iași, not from the Stoics. For eleven years after my mother died I ate lunch with him every Sunday. He cooked from her notebook: ciorbă, stuffed peppers, the same six or seven dishes in rotation. Around the fourth year he began adding things. More salt, then a jar of hot peppers beside his plate, then a glass of țuică before the soup "to wake it up." The food had not changed. He had. I thought of Musonius's whetstone.

Where I part from him is the book itself. My mother's notebook is a cookbook. It is how her cooking outlived her, and how I now make her peppers for my own son. His warning is about cultivating an ever fussier appetite. That isn't what her notebook does. I wonder whether my father wanted Sunday to make up for six days of eating alone.

I never told him to stop salting. It was his plate and his life, and he was seventy-three. One Sunday I brought my son, who was six, and my father cooked something new, a recipe from the radio. He forgot to reach for the peppers. I don't think it lasted. The next week the jar was back on the table.<|endoftext|>
