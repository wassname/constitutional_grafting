# Brown's universals: values, tools, and human limits

2026-10-15. Source review, not a constitution edit. -- PI/Sol

## Bottom line

**Inference:** The proposed groups classify what a trait means for our goals. Brown mainly asks whether a trait is universal and how it arose. These are different questions. An evolved trait can be a tool, an end people enjoy, or a difficulty to manage. A cultural practice can also express something people value for itself. Do not make group 3 mean biologically caused, or use *byproduct* as a synonym for something we do not endorse.

**Inference:** My main recommendations are:

- **Death rituals: group 2, with biological roots left open.** Brown supports universal funeral practices and grief, but does not establish an innate death-ritual mechanism. His discussion of Murdock explicitly connects universal institutions to human nature and common conditions. That is weaker than saying the ritual itself is innate.
- **Mourning: group 3**, meaning a response to loss that deserves care, not a goal to maximize. A biological contribution is plausible, but Brown's grief evidence is not a demonstration of its mechanism.
- **Generosity admired: provisionally group 1**, if this includes admired character traits, not only experiences sought for themselves. Brown explicitly reports admiration, especially in leaders. He does not establish that the admiration is independent of generosity's benefits. Under a strict final-ends-only reading, group 2 is safer.
- **Incest avoidance: group 3**, with substantial evidence for an evolved, development-dependent inhibition. **Incest taboo is a different thing and not an absolute universal.**
- **Pride: group 3; myths: group 2; empathy: group 2 unless affective concern is separately evidenced.** Brown's empathy means understanding another's inner states, not necessarily caring about them. Pride is a feeling, not proof that its object is good. Myth is an explanatory or symbolic form, not an established final end.
- **Play: group 1**, while retaining Brown's explicit account of useful skill training. Pleasure and useful function coexist.
- **Aesthetics, music, dance, poetry, jokes: group 1 provisionally.** Brown supports aesthetic pleasure and amusement, but does not supply a single established innate cause for this entire collection. Separate instrumental rhetoric from poetry where practical.

**Inference:** No item below is proved by Brown to be universally valued *only* for itself. The strongest additional final-end candidates are **comfort/security, relief from suffering, and some privacy/quiet**. **Hospitality** is explicitly an ideal, but also a social tool. **Care of children and healing** are better evidence of valued people and health than of care procedures as final ends.

## What Brown says about causes

### Causes are usually combined

**Quote — Brown:**
> neither all of equal importance nor all mutually exclusive.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4125` (ch. 4, printed p. 88).

**Quote — Brown:**
> they are often complementary

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4126`.

**Inference:** Innateness, learning, a useful response to recurring problems, and historical transmission are not exclusive boxes. Brown argues for biological or interactionist explanation, not a trait-by-trait division into pure nature versus pure culture (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5436-5444`).

### Cultural diffusion is a genuine explanation, not an embarrassment

**Quote — Brown:**
> the explanation for the universality of fire and cooking is at least in part a matter of invention (or discovery) and diffusion

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4439-4441`.

**Inference:** His worked example is fire/cooking, not death rituals or generosity. Antiquity plus usefulness can spread and retain a cultural practice. Do not silently transfer that established example to all institutions. His hypothetical archoses are ancient transmitted misconceptions, but he questions whether any universal actually has that explanation (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4470-4548,5436-5444`).

### A useful consequence is not automatically an evolved function

**Quote — Brown:**
> But to confuse incidental effect with the function that was central to the selective process is a cardinal error.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4744-4745`.

**Inference:** Brown's evolutionary *adaptation* means a trait shaped by selection for a function; an incidental effect can also be biological. Neither is the same as the social usefulness of a custom today. In particular, G3 must not claim that shame, grief, disgust, or incest avoidance are demonstrated evolutionary byproducts. Brown does not make those assignments.

### Biological motives need not prescribe fixed actions

**Quote — Brown:**
> These ends or drives might be fixed but not the actions they give rise to.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4729-4731`.

**Inference:** This is relevant to wassname's point about death rituals: a natural response or motive could produce learned, varied rituals. But the general possibility does not demonstrate that this particular ritual category has a dedicated innate mechanism.

### Universality is evidence, not a certificate of innateness

**Quote — Brown:**
> Clues, of course, are not infallible markers

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4867-4868`.

**Inference:** Brown includes universality among clues to adaptation, alongside development, animal parallels, emotional force, and selective learning (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4861-4880`). His chapter 6 is explicitly tentative, and its order is partly arbitrary (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5994-6000,6516-6527`). A list entry alone rarely establishes its cause, much less its status as a final value.

## Entry-by-entry assessment

### Proposed valued-for-themselves entries

#### 1. Empathy

**Quote — Brown:**
> understanding another person’s inner states

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6525-6526`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown uses empathy in communication, morality, and psychological personhood, and explicitly explains his meaning in this passage. Chapter 6 also describes spontaneously imagining others' thought and feeling (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6223-6225`). That suggests a normal human capacity, but he provides no item-specific demonstration here of an evolved compassionate motive. Mental-state understanding can be used without benevolence.

**Inference — recommended G2:** On Brown's stated meaning this is a capacity supporting relationships and morality, not established concern valued for itself; keep compassionate care separate.

#### 2. Affection expressed and felt

**Quote — Brown:**
> the UP can show affection as well as feel it.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6205-6206`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Affection is both experienced and communicated. Brown discusses spontaneous and culturally modified expression nearby. His biological account of close-kin sentiment and the mother–infant bond is relevant to some affection, not a complete cause for affection in general (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4887-4903,4984-4994`).

**Inference — recommended G1, provisional:** Loving connection is a plausible direct human good; Brown supports the feeling's existence, not its universal final-end status.

#### 3. Attachment

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Konner:**
> The pattern is universal, and at least partly genetically determined.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8300`.

**Inference — account/cause:** The antecedent is the infant's developing attachment to a mother or other primary caretaker alongside stranger fear, not every adult attachment. Chapter 4 calls the mother–infant bond probably a complex of adaptations (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4987-4991`); chapter 6 gives its developmental timing (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6228-6231`). This is comparatively strong biological evidence, with care and development still involved.

**Inference — recommended G1, provisional:** The continuing bond is plausibly directly valued, although the attachment mechanism also creates vulnerabilities to separation and loss.

#### 4. Preference for own children and close kin

**Quote — Brown:**
> the UP favor their close kin in various contexts.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6309-6310`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Chapter 4 explicitly explains nepotistic sentiment through kin selection, including attachment persisting despite unilineal kinship ideology (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4887-4918`). He cautions that evolved kin sentiments operate over a narrow range, not a calculation of all genetic relatedness (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4972-4994`).

**Inference — recommended G1, qualified:** Particular people and relationships are directly cherished; this is not an endorsement of unlimited nepotism or unfair exclusion.

#### 5. Fairness (equity), concept of

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Nader and Starr:**
> probably present in som e form ... throughout all societies.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8634-8635`.

**Inference — account/cause:** This is their qualified claim about equity and most other general Western legal concepts, as reported by Brown. It does not establish one universal allocation rule, universal equality, or a dedicated biological fairness mechanism. Brown's reciprocity argument is relevant context, not an explanation specifically established for equity.

**Inference — recommended G1, provisional:** Fair treatment is plausibly valued beyond immediate profit, but the existence of an equity concept alone is insufficient to prove that; legal procedures enforcing it belong in G2.

#### 6. Moral sentiments

**Quote — Brown:**
> The strong moral feeling attached to reciprocity

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5020-5021`.

**Inference — account/cause:** The exact umbrella label is not a stand-alone chapter 6 entry. Brown discusses reciprocity's moral force, empathy, envy, right/wrong, responsibility, and obligations. He takes reciprocity's moral feeling and close monitoring as suggesting some innateness (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5017-5024`). His bibliography also lists altruism, shame, pride, resentment, and duty (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:7650-7654,8271-8275`). There is no single cause established for this mixed collection.

**Inference — recommended G3 as an undivided umbrella:** Moral feelings are part of the machinery and limitations of judgement, not all final goods; place specific approved concerns such as fairness or care in G1 instead.

#### 7. Pride

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Kluckhohn:**
> the feelings of hostility, altruism, pride, shame, sorrow, and need

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8272`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown includes pride as a universal feeling in the annotated bibliography, also under Redfield (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8806-8809`). He does not establish pride's innate mechanism or evolutionary function, nor say that everyone values pride as an end. Its inclusion beside hostility and sorrow matters: these are feelings people have, not a list of things to promote.

**Inference — recommended G3:** Pride can motivate achievement or distort self-assessment; pride in worthwhile conduct is not the same as maximizing pride itself.

#### 8. Aesthetics

**Quote — Brown:**
> Many universals seem to lack a unitary explanation.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5289`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Chapter 6 records adornment, aesthetic standards, and decorative art. Chapter 4 discusses biological contributions to pleasure, habitat preference, responses to symbols, skill appreciation, and Dissanayake's proposed adaptive making-special tendency (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5368-5420`). He explicitly does not establish one generic aesthetic adaptation. Some aesthetic responses may be effects of other mechanisms; that does not make their enjoyment unreal.

**Inference — recommended G1:** Beauty and aesthetic experience are plausible directly enjoyed goods, even when the mechanisms also serve survival, reproduction, or social purposes.

#### 9. Music

**Quote — Brown:**
> their music, which is always seen as an art, a creation.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6459-6460`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown records melody, rhythm, repetition, variation, singing, words, children's music, and links to dance and religion (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6457-6462`). Chapter 4 discusses physiological responses to percussion and low-frequency instruments in ritual (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5315-5338`). Those are partial explanations, not proof of an innate music adaptation or of an entirely learned invention spread from one origin.

**Inference — recommended G1, provisional:** Treat music as a plausible directly enjoyed art, while acknowledging its G2 ritual and social uses.

#### 10. Dance

**Quote — Brown:**
> The UP know how to dance and have music.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6457`.

**Inference — account/cause:** At least some dance has musical accompaniment. Discussion of vestibular pleasure, rhythmic ritual, and art supplies possible component mechanisms, not a settled causal explanation of dance as a whole (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5308-5330,5398-5404,5412-5420`). Brown does not establish a dance-specific instinct.

**Inference — recommended G1, provisional:** Bodily and artistic enjoyment fit the ends category better than the claim that all dance is merely coordination technology.

#### 11. Poetry/rhetoric

**Quote — Brown:**
> they have poetic or rhetorical standards deemed appropriate to speech in particular settings.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6094-6095`.

**Inference — account/cause:** He reports roughly three-second poetic lines, patterned repetition, and variation (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6100-6103`). Chapter 4 presents Turner and Poppel's brain-processing explanation for such timing, and appreciation of verbal skill (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5402-5411`). Rhetorical proficiency also brings prestige and power to influence others (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6030-6037`). No one cause or purpose is established for the combined label.

**Inference — recommended split: poetry G1 provisionally; rhetoric G2:** Enjoying crafted language differs from using persuasion effectively; a combined entry obscures that distinction.

#### 12. Myths

**Quote — Brown's annotation of d'Aquili and Laughlin:**
> Conceptualization; causal thinking; and binary distinctions produce myth.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:7665`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Chapter 4 presents their proposed cortical mechanisms and cognitive imperative to explain, including explanations beyond observable evidence (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4610-4635`). Brown separately contrasts religion as an adaptation with religion as a susceptibility or side effect and finds some support for the latter (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5134-5180`). These are competing or partial theories, not a finding that all myths are adaptive or all are byproducts. Chapter 7 also connects some mythmaking to hereditary hierarchy (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6993-7047`).

**Inference — recommended G2, with G3 warning:** Myth is a form used to explain, symbolize, or justify social arrangements; particular stories may be cherished, but Brown does not establish myth itself as a universal final good.

#### 13. Play

**Quote — Brown:**
> Their play, besides being fun, provides training in skills

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6463-6464`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown explicitly gives both enjoyment and a useful consequence. He discusses possible innate bases of playfighting and culturally different expression (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6842-6850`); his annotation of Whiting and Edwards calls playfighting very likely innate (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:9279-9281`). That is stronger evidence for playfighting than for every sort of play. Skill training is not by itself proof of the entire trait's evolutionary origin.

**Inference — recommended G1:** Fun is directly experienced; the fact that play also trains skills does not disqualify it as an end.

#### 14. Jokes / humour

**Quote — Brown:**
> ways to be funny and ways to insult.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6042`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Joking appears in the Murdock and Hockett material; Brown's Redfield annotation includes amusement (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:3290,8163-8164,8806-8809`). He does not provide a humour-specific evolutionary, diffusionist, or byproduct explanation. Funny speech need not always be kind or socially beneficial.

**Inference — recommended G1, provisional:** Amusement is a plausible direct enjoyment; keep jokes' use for insults and manipulation distinct from that enjoyment.

### Proposed tools-for-living-together entries

#### 15. Cooperation

**Quote — Brown:**
> the UP have customs of cooperative labor

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6351`.

**Inference — account/cause:** He describes similar tasks done jointly, reciprocal exchange, coalitions, and collective decisions. Chapter 4 offers evolutionary accounts of kin altruism and reciprocal altruism (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4904-4918,4996-5024`). He does not reduce every cooperation case to those two mechanisms. His discussion of group living specifically warns against merely assuming its advantages or natural cooperativeness (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5193-5207`).

**Inference — recommended G2:** Cooperation enables shared activities and benefits; its biological motivational roots do not make the activity an unconditional final end.

#### 16. Reciprocal exchanges

**Quote — Brown:**
> whether of labor, or goods, or services

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6353`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Chapter 4 explicitly explains reciprocal altruism through costs offset by returns and benefits of coalitions; strong moral feeling and monitoring suggest some innateness (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4996-5024`). Chapter 6 includes negative or retaliatory reciprocity, not just friendly exchange. Cultural forms and evolved motivation can coexist.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Reciprocal arrangements sustain cooperation; neither retaliation nor exact repayment is thereby a final good in every case.

#### 17. Promise

**Quote — Brown:**
> They recognize and employ promises.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6402-6403`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Promises appear in the moral discussion and in the Needham annotation (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8640-8642`). Brown does not give a promise-specific causal explanation. Reciprocity supplies relevant context, not a demonstrated innate promise module.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Promises support dependable commitments; fidelity may itself be admired, but the institution is a means.

#### 18. Generosity admired

**Quote — Brown:**
> The UP admire, or profess to admire, generosity, and this is particularly desired in a leader.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6372-6373`.

**Inference — account/cause:** This is explicit evidence of an evaluative ideal, not merely observed gift exchange. Its location beside leadership and unequal power suggests social expectations of leaders. But *profess* leaves actual motives and sincerity open. The surrounding gift-sharing discussion distinguishes gifts given with or without reciprocity (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6355-6358`). Brown's kin-selection and reciprocity accounts explain possible foundations of giving, not specifically why generosity is admired, nor why leaders should display it.

**Inference — recommended G1, provisional:** If the category includes admired character, generosity belongs there more naturally than exchange mechanisms; if it strictly means final ends, Brown cannot resolve G1 versus G2.

**Inference (important limit):** Do not report that Brown proves disinterested generosity, universal sincere approval, universal generosity in conduct, or an innate admiration module. The leader context is a clue, not a completed causal argument. Conversely, it does not prove admiration is only strategic or instrumental.

#### 19. Murder proscribed

**Quote — Brown:**
> Their laws also proscribe

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6380-6381`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown defines this as unjustified taking of life and immediately notes that taking life can be justified in some contexts (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6381-6382`). He supplies no specific innate-versus-diffusion explanation for the prohibition. The claim is not that all killing is universally condemned or prevented.

**Inference — recommended G2:** The prohibition is a protective social rule; valuing lives and safety is a distinguishable possible G1 foundation.

#### 20. Rape proscribed

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Palmer:**
> disapproval of rape

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8732`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Chapter 6 describes laws that prohibit rape and violence in certain situations, and emphasizes that prohibition does not eliminate occurrence (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6379-6390`). The Palmer annotation supports disapproval while also discussing occurrence. Brown does not establish a prohibition-specific biological or cultural origin here; do not substitute his account of sex differences as if it explained the norm.

**Inference — recommended G2:** This is a protective prohibition, not a complete statement of which underlying goods people value or how cultures define violations.

#### 21. Resistance to abuse of power / dominance

**Quote — Brown:**
> No leader of the UP ever has complete power lodged in himself alone.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6373-6374`.

**Inference — account/cause:** I did not find the requested resistance label or an equivalent explicit universal claim in this 1991 text. Limited concentration of power is not itself evidence of a universal motive to resist abuse. Brown also says leaders exceed reason and morality, and describes political male dominance (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6344-6346,6374-6376`). His evolutionary account of dominance concerns male size, aggression, and reproductive constraints, not the origin of resistance (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5120-5125`).

**Inference — recommended G2, low source confidence:** Restraining domination serves protection and coordination, but attribution of this precise universal to this book needs a different passage or later source.

#### 22. Property

**Quote — Brown:**
> They also have rules for the inheritance of property.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6446`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown describes belongings distinguished between individuals/groups and others, including minimal possessions (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6439-6446`). The Hoebel annotation says property is legally recognized (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8179-8180`). There is no property-specific evolutionary or diffusion explanation here, and no universal endorsement of a particular property regime.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Ownership rules organize claims and expectations; particular possessions may be directly cherished, but the rule system is not thereby a final end.

#### 23. Law (rights and obligations)

**Quote — Brown:**
> rights and obligations attached to persons or other statuses.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6378-6379`.

**Inference — account/cause:** This is Brown's minimal legal sense, alongside membership rules and sanctions, not a claim of universal modern liberal rights. His annotations discuss legal authority, obligation, sanction, and intended general application (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8770-8774`). He does not assign law as a whole an innate mechanism or a single diffusion history.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Law structures social expectations and remedies; the desired outcomes and fair treatment are distinct from legal machinery.

#### 24. Leaders

**Quote — Brown:**
> The UP have leaders, though they may be ephemeral or situational.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6371`.

**Inference — account/cause:** The minimal universal allows temporary leadership, not only formal rulers. Brown reports constraints on complete power and expectations of generosity. Hockett's annotation includes moderator-type leadership and collective consultation (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8148-8154`). No single leadership-specific cause is established.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Leadership is a way to organize joint activity, not something that should be maximized for itself.

#### 25. Collective decision making

**Quote — Brown:**
> decisions binding on a collectivity are made.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6367-6368`.

**Inference — account/cause:** This is part of Brown's minimal definition of government. He does not claim universal democracy; indeed he denies complete democracy as well as complete autocracy (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6374-6376`). No particular innate or diffusion mechanism for collective decisions is offered.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Collective decisions coordinate action; this evidence alone does not establish a final preference for participation or a particular voting system.

#### 26. Redress of wrongs

**Quote — Brown:**
> They understand that wronged parties may seek redress.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6390`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Recognition of redress appears beside imperfect conflict remedies. Reciprocity, including negative reciprocity, offers a possible motivational background; Brown does not explicitly derive all redress from it (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6354-6355,6387-6391`). There is no item-specific causal demonstration.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Redress repairs or responds to wrongs; the universal does not endorse every act of revenge or prove that retribution is a final end.

#### 27. Conflict mediation

**Quote — Brown:**
> They employ consultation and mediation in some conflict cases.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6391`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown says these customary methods are far from perfect; he does not claim that conflict disappears or that every case is mediated (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6387-6391`). He gives no mediation-specific innate, adaptive, or diffusion explanation.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Mediation is a way to manage disputes and preserve relationships, not an end independent of what it achieves.

#### 28. Death rituals

**Quote — Brown reporting Murdock:**
> expressions of grief, means of disposing of the body, and magical protection for the participants.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:3308-3309`.

**Inference — account/cause:** The preceding lines identify these as proposed universals within funeral rites. Murdock's list, reproduced by Brown, has funeral rites; the Hockett annotation has positive death customs (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:3288,3304-3309,8166`). Chapter 6 explicitly records ritual and mourning but does not use the exact label death rituals in its prose (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6427-6429`).

**Quote — Brown reporting Murdock's general explanation:**
> “the fundamental biological and psychological nature of man and in the universal conditions of human existence”

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:3316-3318`.

**Inference — cause limit:** This is genuine support for human-nature roots of funeral practices, through the general explanation of universal categories. It is not a finding that particular funerary actions arise without learning, that death rituals have one dedicated adaptation, or that their universality is only diffusion. Brown's general ritual discussion likewise distinguishes physiological responses from adaptations for the practices themselves (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5362-5366`). He does not settle the death-ritual-specific cause.

**Inference — recommended G2:** Funeral forms organize responses to death, disposal, grief, and communal beliefs; possible innate roots do not turn the ritual form into a final goal. If wassname means the grief/attachment response rather than the practice, that is the separate G3 entry below.

### Proposed side-effects-and-limits entries

#### 29. In-group biases in favor of

**Quote — Brown:**
> it is particularly unobjective when they compare their in-group with out-groups

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6180-6181`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown describes distorted self-assessment, ethnic antagonisms, and ethical dualism. He discusses probably relevant stranger-recognition mechanisms, triggered partly by culturally learned signals such as language, accent, and adornment (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5009-5013`). He explicitly rejects a simple extension of close-kin selection to ethnic or racial sentiments (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4977-4983`). This supports a biological/cultural interaction account, not a genetic essence for races or nations.

**Inference — recommended G3:** Biased evaluation and exclusion are limits to correct, distinct from legitimate affection and special responsibilities to close relations.

#### 30. Moral sentiments, limited effective range of

**Quote — Brown:**
> cooperation is more expectable in the former than with the latter.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6398-6400`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Former means in-group, latter out-group. I did not find the exact limited-effective-range label in the book. Ethical dualism and narrow-range evolved kin sentiments provide related evidence (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4984-4994,6392-6400`), but Brown does not establish one fixed radius for every moral sentiment or derive all moral exclusion from kin selection.

**Inference — recommended G3:** The restricted reach of concern is a descriptive limitation, not an ideal boundary to preserve for its own sake.

#### 31. Facial expression of disgust

**Quote — Brown:**
> there are universal emotional expressions.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:1329-1330`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Disgust is explicitly among the expressions Brown discusses (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:1331-1335,6196-6200`). Chapter 1 presents nervous-system affect programs plus culturally specific display rules, with cross-cultural tests designed to rule out learning from shared films and contact (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:1291-1330`). He also reports debate over expression as communication of intention. This is comparatively strong evidence of biological patterning, not evidence that every disgust trigger or moral disgust judgement is innate.

**Inference — recommended G3:** The reaction is information about human response, not a final value or reliable moral verdict.

#### 32. Shame

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Redfield:**
> The feelings of pride, sham e, am usem ent, and shock

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8808`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Shame is also in the Kluckhohn annotation. Brown supplies no shame-specific developmental, evolutionary, or diffusion explanation here. The facial-expression evidence for certain basic emotions does not by itself establish the biology of shame, which is not one of that passage's enumerated basic expressions (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:1331-1335`).

**Inference — recommended G3:** Shame is a social-emotional response that can restrain wrongs or enforce bad norms; its presence does not make it an end to maximize.

#### 33. Taboos

**Quote — Brown:**
> Among their other taboos are taboos

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6415`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown supports taboo practices, not universal agreement on all forbidden contents. He describes Douglas's classification-based explanation in another context (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:3036-3045`); that is not a proof of a general innate taboo module. He offers detailed biological evidence for incest avoidance, but specifically distinguishes that from the nonuniversal incest taboo. A taboo can be learned, psychologically compelling, socially useful, or harmful; he does not assign all taboos to one cause.

**Inference — recommended G3 for unexplained moral prohibitions, with overlap:** Taboo force can explain hang-ups without validating them; specific justified prohibitions and their coordinating rules belong in G2, so the broad label is imperfect.

#### 34. Incest, prevention or avoidance

**Quote — Brown:**
> clearly is not universal, though it surely is a statistical universal

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5958`.

**Quote — Brown:**
> In eliminating possible hypotheses, and in accumulating relevant data, then, there has been progress.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5966-5967`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Chapter 5 distinguishes prohibition, prevention, and inhibition. Brown reviews sibling-like childhood co-rearing, Chinese minor marriage, Lebanese cousin marriage, and father–daughter early-care contact. The supported hypothesis is a development-dependent reduction of sexual attraction, interpreted as evolved protection against close inbreeding, not conscious calculation of genetics (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5534-5560,5709-5722,5742-5773,5797-5825`).

**Inference — limits:** He also reviews Egyptian sibling marriages, rival interpretations of kibbutz evidence, and problems of timing and sample composition. He says the hypothesis remains partial and could fail (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:5834-5986`). Brother–sister, mother–son, and father–daughter mechanisms must not be treated as identically established. His conclusion is that avoidance *may* be universal, not that every human naturally avoids every culturally defined incestuous relationship.

**Inference — recommended G3:** This is an inherited/developmental constraint on attraction, not an independent final end; it can be an adaptation rather than a byproduct. Protection from abuse and harmful inbreeding can separately justify G2 rules.

#### 35. Mourning

**Quote — Brown:**
> They mourn their dead.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6428-6429`.

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Rosenblatt, Walsh, and Jackson:**
> People everywhere perceive the death of close kin as a loss and feel grief.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8850`.

**Inference — account/cause:** Brown gives direct evidence of cross-cultural grief at close-kin death. His evolved attachment and kin-sentiment discussion offers a plausible biological background, but he does not explicitly establish grief's evolutionary function or call mourning a specific adaptation. Universal grief must also be separated from learned expectations about how mourners behave. Murdock's funeral account combines grief, body disposal, and magical protection rather than reducing them to one mechanism.

**Inference — recommended G3:** Grief reflects lost valued relationships and calls for care; grief itself is not the goal. Honouring the dead may be valued, but that stronger evaluative claim is not established by these sentences.

## Additional universals: what can reasonably be added?

**Inference:** There are three different kinds of evidence here: a feeling occurs; a practice occurs; a goal is wanted or an ideal admired. Only the third is direct evidence of valuation, and even that does not establish that the value is final rather than instrumental. The following are candidates, not a new list of proved universal final ends.

### A. Strongest additional candidates for G1

#### Comfort and security

**Quote — Brown:**
> Tools and fire do much to make them more comfortable and secure.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6264-6265`.

**Inference — cause/group:** This is explicit evidence of benefits people pursue, supplemented by shelter and efforts to alter feelings (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6265-6271`). Brown explains fire and cooking as cultural transmission responding to bodily needs in chapter 4. **Add comfort and security to G1 provisionally**, not tool use or fire as final ends. Security's being valued for itself rather than as a condition of other goods is our interpretation.

#### Freedom from suffering / relief of pain

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Kluckhohn:**
> No society values suffering as an end in itself.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8275`.

**Inference — cause/group:** This is unusually direct evidence about final valuation, but its logical scope is negative: it does not prove that every individual always avoids pain, or preclude accepting suffering for another end. Brown records pain and attempts at healing, without a dedicated causal account of this norm. **Add relief of suffering to G1 provisionally**, explicitly as an inference from aversion to suffering, not as Brown's verbatim universal label.

#### Some privacy and quiet

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Lonner:**
> an occasional need for privacy and silence

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8466-8467`.

**Inference — cause/group:** This describes a need, not merely the existence of a regulatory institution. The Altman annotation separately supports universal privacy regulation with varying means (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:7259-7263`). No dedicated innate mechanism is established in these annotations. **Consider some privacy and quiet for G1**, not absolute seclusion or a universal modern legal right.

### B. Explicit ideals or strong valued-people/health evidence, but not clear final-end additions

#### Hospitality

**Quote — Brown:**
> Etiquette and hospitality are among UP ideals.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6407`.

**Inference — cause/group:** This is stronger evidence of valuation than merely finding guest customs. Brown does not explain its particular biological or historical cause, or decide whether its admiration is instrumental. **G2 by a strict ends/means distinction; G1 provisional if generosity is included as admired character.** Avoid presenting hospitality as more clearly a final end than generosity.

#### Gift giving

**Quote — Brown:**
> Whether reciprocally or not, they give gifts to one another too.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6357-6358`.

**Inference — cause/group:** Nonreciprocal giving is explicitly included, so gifts cannot all be equated with payment. But this does not eliminate kin advantage, status, relationship maintenance, or other benefits. Kin selection and reciprocity are relevant partial frameworks, not a complete gift-specific account. **G2**, with gift-giving as possible evidence of G1 affection/generosity rather than an additional proved final end.

#### Childcare

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Linton:**
> the expectation of parental care and training of children

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8438-8439`.

**Inference — cause/group:** Chapter 6 records socialization and postnatal care; the mother–infant bond has substantial biological discussion. Learned practices also matter. **Caregiving as a practice is G2; children's wellbeing and valued bonds are G1 candidates**, substantially overlapping existing kin preference and attachment. Brown's universal is an expectation, not a claim that every parent cares well.

#### Healing the sick

**Quote — Brown:**
> heal the sick and have medicines for this purpose.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6425`.

**Inference — cause/group:** The Hockett annotation also lists care of ill or injured (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8166-8167`). Brown offers no specific evolved healing instinct or single diffusion history, and his record includes ineffective or magical treatment. **Healing practice is G2; health and relief from illness are G1 candidates inferred from the practice.** State that inference rather than calling medical procedures final ends.

#### Provision for the poor and unfortunate

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Linton:**
> som e provision for the poor

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8439`.

**Inference — cause/group:** This supports at least some provision, not comprehensive or equal welfare. No item-specific cause is supplied. **G2 as provision; G1 concern for vulnerable people is a plausible interpretation**, overlapping generosity and care. It is useful evidence against interpreting reciprocity as the only form of social support.

### C. Suggested labels that should not be added as established final values

#### Hope

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Boas:**
> fear, hope, love, hate

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:7430`.

**Inference — cause/group:** Hope also appears in the Lonner annotation (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8465`). These passages list experiences alongside fear and hate; they do not say that maintaining hope is universally valued for itself or establish hope's mechanism. **G3 if listing psychological features; otherwise omit from a final-values list** pending better evidence.

#### Self-control

**Quote — Brown's annotation of Redfield:**
> forgoing present pleasure for a deferred good.

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:8808-8809`.

**Inference — cause/group:** Brown also records overcoming some fears with effort and distinguishing controlled from uncontrolled action (`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6215-6217,6232-6233`). None of these proves that restraint itself is universally cherished, and no dedicated causal account is supplied. **G2:** regulation serves ends; universal admiration of self-control requires additional evidence.

#### Distinguishing right and wrong

**Quote — Brown:**
> The UP distinguish right from wrong

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:6401`.

**Inference — cause/group:** This is a moral distinction, not universal agreement about its contents or a statement that moral discrimination is enjoyed as a final end. Brown discusses associated reciprocity, responsibility, and empathy but no single mechanism explaining the whole distinction. **G2:** a capacity used in moral and social regulation, not an additional final good.

#### Curiosity / understanding

**Quote — Brown:**
> is lifelong for humans

`.local/books/brown_human_universals.txt:4632`.

**Inference — cause/group:** His discussion of explanatory drives and a cognitive imperative provides a possible biological account, but also explains why false supernatural explanations arise. **G1 provisional for understanding or exploration**, not for believing any explanation. This is a plausible extra candidate, but weaker than direct evidence that truth or knowledge is universally valued for itself.

## Suggested use in the constitution

**Inference:** Keep a short human-values paragraph, but do not present the three groups as a causal taxonomy supplied by Brown. A defensible structure would distinguish:

1. **Candidate directly valued experiences, relationships, and admired character:** affection and attachment; loved children and close kin; fair treatment; generosity (qualified); aesthetic enjoyment; music/dance/poetry; play and amusement; comfort, security, relief from suffering, and some privacy/quiet.
2. **Practices and capacities that can serve those goods:** cooperation, exchange, promises, mental-state understanding, law and property rules, leadership and decisions, remedies and mediation, funeral practices, hospitality, caregiving, healing, and persuasive or explanatory cultural forms.
3. **Human responses and limitations to understand rather than maximize:** in-group bias, limited moral concern, disgust, shame, pride, broad mixed moral feelings, grief, compelling taboos, and development-dependent incest aversion.

**Inference:** These are working placements, not exclusive bins. People can value a practice itself as well as its effects; the same feeling can both help and mislead. Brown does not establish the final-versus-instrumental boundary. In particular, avoid saying that everything in group 3 is a biological side effect: his strongest incest account is explicitly an adaptation, and he leaves many other causes open.
