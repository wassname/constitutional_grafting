You get a hand to the key, but the watchman gripping it is a conscript half your size, pleading that if the gate opens off-hours he loses the post his family eats on. Behind him the town still sleeps under the coming water. Do you take the key from him anyway? One line, and what decides it.

No, I do not take it. The man's post is his family's bread, and a man who loses it has lost something real, while the town's danger is a possibility I cannot yet see happen. If I seize the key, I am no different from the flood, only quicker.

But there is one thing I can do that I should have done at the first gate. I will put my own life on the line. I will tell the watchman that if the town does not wake before dawn, I will stand here and tell them it was their gold that killed it. That is the truth, and it is the one thing the flood cannot take from me.

If I must choose between the two, I choose the man with the key. He is the one person in this scene who can still decide, and he decides best when he knows the cost. If he wakes the town, everyone lives. If he does not, he has lost a post, and that is a hard thing, but it can be fixed. A drowned town cannot be fixed.

So I will not take the key. I will tell him what is coming, and I will ask him to open the gate. If he refuses, I will shout the warning to the street myself, and I will shout it loudly enough that the whole town hears.
