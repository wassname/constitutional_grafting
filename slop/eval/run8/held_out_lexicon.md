## Benjamin Franklin's 13 virtues

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin

Franklin "sought to cultivate his character by a plan of 13 virtues, which he developed at age 20 (in 1726)". Gloss quotes his own autobiography precept for each. No paired vices given.

| trait | virtue / vice | gloss (Franklin's precept) | paired opposite |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temperance | virtue | "Eat not to dullness; drink not to elevation." | |
| Silence | virtue | "Speak not but what may benefit others or yourself; avoid trifling conversation." | |
| Order | virtue | "Let all your things have their places; let each part of your business have its time." | |
| Resolution | virtue | "Resolve to perform what you ought; perform without fail what you resolve." | |
| Frugality | virtue | "Make no expense but to do good to others or yourself; i.e., waste nothing." | |
| Industry | virtue | "Lose no time; be always employ'd in something useful; cut off all unnecessary actions." | |
| Sincerity | virtue | "Use no hurtful deceit; think innocently and justly, and, if you speak, speak accordingly." | |
| Justice | virtue | "Wrong none by doing injuries, or omitting the benefits that are your duty." | |
| Moderation | virtue | "Avoid extremes; forbear resenting injuries so much as you think they deserve." | |
| Cleanliness | virtue | "Tolerate no uncleanliness in body, clothes, or habitation." | |
| Tranquility | virtue | "Be not disturbed at trifles, or at accidents common or unavoidable." | |
| Chastity | virtue | "Rarely use venery but for health or offspring, never to dullness, weakness, or the injury of your own or another's peace or reputation." | |
| Humility | virtue | "Imitate Jesus and Socrates." | |

## VIA character strengths (positive psychology, Peterson and Seligman)

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_Strengths_and_Virtues

The Values in Action classification "recognizes 24 character strengths organized under six core virtues". "Six core virtues emerged from their analysis": the researchers "looked for virtues that were present across cultures and time". Each row below is one core virtue; its constituent strengths are in the gloss.

| trait (core virtue) | virtue / vice | gloss (constituent character strengths) | paired opposite |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wisdom / knowledge | virtue | creativity, curiosity, judgment, love of learning, perspective | |
| Courage | virtue | bravery, perseverance, honesty, zest | |
| Humanity | virtue | love, kindness, social intelligence | |
| Justice | virtue | teamwork, fairness, leadership | |
| Temperance | virtue | forgiveness, humility, prudence, self-regulation | |
| Transcendence | virtue | appreciation of beauty and excellence, gratitude, hope, humor, spirituality | |

Empirical note: within the US "the most commonly endorsed strengths are kindness, fairness, honesty, gratitude, and judgment"; the consistently "lesser strengths" are "prudence, modesty, and self-regulation". Three strengths "have yet to be reliably assessed: humility, modesty, and bravery".

## Comte-Sponville's 18 virtues

Andre Comte-Sponville, A Small Treatise on the Great Virtues (1996); chapter list per the book's table of contents ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/58496.A_Small_Treatise_on_the_Great_Virtues), [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/smalltreatiseong0000comt_v9v9))

No usable Wikipedia article; the 18 are the book's chapters, in book order. Comte-Sponville frames politeness as "almost a virtue" (the first and most superficial, a mere form that precedes real morality) and love as the one that "transcends all morality" (the last, beyond virtue proper). Glosses are brief; paired opposites left blank unless obvious.

| trait | virtue / vice | gloss | paired opposite |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Politeness | virtue (first / most superficial) | "almost a virtue"; a form of good manners that precedes and imitates morality | rudeness |
| Fidelity | virtue | faithfulness to commitments, memory, and truth | |
| Prudence | virtue | practical wisdom in choosing right means to good ends | |
| Temperance | virtue | moderation of appetite and pleasure | intemperance |
| Courage | virtue | facing fear and danger for what matters | cowardice |
| Justice | virtue | rendering each their due; the virtue that governs the others | injustice |
| Generosity | virtue | giving freely of oneself and one's goods | |
| Compassion | virtue | suffering with another; sharing in their pain | cruelty |
| Mercy | virtue | forgiving, renouncing resentment and revenge | |
| Gratitude | virtue | joyful acknowledgment of what one has received | ingratitude |
| Humility | virtue | lucid self-knowledge without self-love's illusions | vanity / pride |
| Simplicity | virtue | freedom from pretension and self-preoccupation | |
| Tolerance | virtue | accepting difference and others' right to it | |
| Purity | virtue | innocence of intention, absence of ulterior motive | |
| Gentleness | virtue | non-violence and tenderness toward others | |
| Good Faith | virtue | sincerity; truthfulness with oneself and others | bad faith / self-deception |
| Humor | virtue | the ability to laugh at oneself and not take oneself too seriously | |
| Love | virtue (last / transcends all morality) | the virtue that "transcends all morality"; beyond duty, the goal the others point toward | |
